# Lesson 5: Analytic Aesthetics with Institutional Frameworks

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The project of analytic aesthetics emerges from a fundamental dissatisfaction with romantic and formalist accounts of art. Across the twentieth century, Anglo-American philosophers confronted a persistent puzzle: aesthetic experience seemed to possess irreducible phenomenological properties, yet the boundary between what counts as art and what does not appeared radically unstable. A porcelain urinal signed "R. Mutt" by Marcel Duchamp in 1917 inaugurated a century of institutional interrogation. If we cannot point to perceptual properties that distinguish art from non-art, how do we account for the manifest fact that *something* has shifted when an everyday object enters the gallery?

The analytic tradition resolved to answer this question with precision. Rather than retreating into mysticism or appeals to ineffable genius, philosophers like Monroe Beardsley, Arthur Danto, George Dickie, and Nelson Goodman constructed rigorous frameworks for understanding aesthetic experience, artistic definition, and the ontological status of artworks themselves. Their collective achievement was not to *solve* the problem of art—a hubristic goal that proved impossible—but to map its logical terrain with such clarity that subsequent inquiry could proceed with warranted humility.

At the core of this enterprise lies a recognition that aesthetic and artistic properties operate at multiple levels simultaneously. There is the level of *phenomenal experience*—the immediate, first-person sensory encounter with an object. There is the level of *intentionality*—the mental states and purposes that an agent brings to engagement with an artwork. There is the level of *social practice*—the institutions, conventions, and expert communities that frame what counts as art within a given historical moment. And there is the level of *formal structure*—the systems of symbols, marks, shapes, and sounds that constitute the artwork's material substrate. A complete account of aesthetics must navigate these levels without collapsing them into one another, a feat that demands conceptual precision and resistance to seductive oversimplification.

The invariants that structure this inquiry are three. First, *the intentionality condition*: aesthetic experience and artistic status are not purely intrinsic properties of objects but depend essentially on how those objects are regarded, presented, and contextualized by agents and communities. A canvas covered in random pigment marks becomes a Jackson Pollock painting only within a framework of artistic intention and institutional recognition. Second, *the complexity condition*: definitions of art that appeal to a single necessary and sufficient condition inevitably face counterexamples. The hunt for a universal essence of art has repeatedly failed, not from lack of ingenuity but from the genuine pluralism of artistic practice itself. Third, *the normative condition*: aesthetic judgment and artistic value are not reducible to subjective preference but admit of rational scrutiny, criticism, and debate grounded in formal properties, historical context, and shared standards of evaluation.

These invariants establish the terrain upon which all subsequent analysis must operate. They tell us that we cannot understand art by studying isolated objects in phenomenological isolation; we must attend to the complex ecology of intentions, practices, and social frameworks that make artistic status possible. Yet they also insist that this social dimension does not render aesthetic judgment wholly arbitrary or subjective. The challenge is to construct a framework that honors both the genuine objectivity of aesthetic reasoning and the inescapable social mediation through which art achieves its identity.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

### Beardsley's Phenomenological Foundation

Monroe Beardsley's work provides the essential foundation for understanding aesthetic experience as a distinct psychological phenomenon. For Beardsley, aesthetic experience is characterized by five constitutive features that differentiate it from ordinary perception or appreciation of utility. The experience exhibits *object directedness*—attention is focused on the intrinsic properties of the object itself, not on its instrumental use or practical function. When you aesthetically appreciate a Persian carpet, you attend to color relationships, pattern complexity, and textural nuance; you do not calculate its thermal insulation properties or resale value. Second, aesthetic experience involves *sensory discrimination*—a heightened capacity to notice fine distinctions within the perceptual field, to register subtle variations of tone, form, and spatial relationship that ordinary attention might overlook. Third, aesthetic experience generates *affective engagement*—emotional responses that arise not from the object's practical consequences for the observer but from its formal and representational properties. The experience manifests *cognitive richness*—it opens up networks of associations, interpretive possibilities, and semantic connections that enlarge the observer's understanding of the work and the domains it references. Finally, aesthetic experience exhibits *a sense of coherence*—the various elements of the work appear to fit together in an integrated whole, to manifest purposiveness without any external purpose, in Kant's celebrated formulation.

These features do not constitute a simple checklist. Rather, they form an integrated syndrome, a cluster of mutually reinforcing characteristics that distinguish genuinely aesthetic encounters from mere sensory input or technical appreciation. A engineer examining a bridge might discriminate fine details of structural engineering with considerable acuity, but this discrimination does not necessarily constitute aesthetic experience unless it becomes object-directed in the specific sense Beardsley intends—attending to the bridge's form *as such*, not as a means to crossing a river.

Critically, Beardsley's framework establishes that aesthetic experience is not identical to any particular perceptual modality. One can have aesthetic experience of visual art, music, poetry, dance, architecture, and natural objects. The commonality lies not in the sensory channel but in the *structure* of attention and appreciation itself. This insight proves vital for what follows: if aesthetic experience is structurally unified across diverse media, then any adequate theory of art must explain how radically different physical substrates—pigment on canvas, organized sounds in time, words arranged on a page—can all serve as vehicles for aesthetic experience.

Beardsley's phenomenological framework also introduces a crucial distinction between aesthetic *experience* and aesthetic *judgment*. One may have a vivid, richly textured aesthetic experience of a work without being able to articulate its grounds or defend one's appreciation discursively. Conversely, one may construct rigorous aesthetic arguments and critical interpretations about works one has never directly experienced in their full phenomenological presence. The relationship between experience and judgment becomes, therefore, a central problem requiring careful analysis.

### Danto's Artworld and the Problem of Indistinguishable Objects

Arthur Danto's revolutionary contribution begins with a deceptively simple observation: there exist pairs of objects that are *perceptually indistinguishable* yet differ profoundly in their aesthetic and artistic status. His canonical example involves two visually identical red canvases. One is an abstract expressionist painting titled *Red Painting*, created by an artist working within the contemporary art world with deliberate aesthetic intention. The other is a piece of painted canvas created without artistic intent—the product of chance, accident, or pure utility. Perceptual inspection alone cannot determine which is which. Yet the first is unambiguously an artwork while the second is not.

This observation demolishes any aesthetics that grounds artistic status solely in perceptual or phenomenal properties. If we cannot visually distinguish artworks from non-artworks, then artistic identity must depend on something other than intrinsic visual character. Danto concluded that artworks require an *interpretation*—a framework of meaning-making that places the object within the history of art and the contemporary discourse of the artworld.

The artworld, in Danto's conception, is the totality of practices, institutions, expert communities, critical discourse, historical knowledge, and interpretive frameworks through which art achieves identity and significance. It includes not only museums, galleries, and collecting practices but the entire apparatus of art history, art criticism, philosophical aesthetics, and informed audience appreciation. The artworld is where *aboutness* becomes possible—where an object, through its placement within the right interpretive context, comes to be *about* something, to carry meaning, to express ideas or emotions that transcend its material surface.

Crucially, Danto recognizes that the artworld is itself historically constituted and evolving. The artworld that made Renaissance painting possible differed fundamentally from the nineteenth-century salon system, which differed from the twentieth-century museum complex and contemporary global art market. Each historical configuration of the artworld made certain kinds of artistic practice possible while foreclosing others. Duchamp's readymades became possible only because the modern artworld had developed sufficient reflexivity to permit questioning of the very definition of art itself.

The practical implication of Danto's framework is that understanding any artwork requires *thick historical knowledge*. One cannot adequately appreciate Duchamp's *Fountain* by examining the urinal's visual properties in isolation. One must understand its relationship to Dada practice, to the conventions of the Society of Independent Artists, to the history of avant-garde provocation, and to the philosophical questions about authorship and artistic intention that it was designed to raise. The artwork's meaning is constitutively dependent on this entire interpretive apparatus.

Danto's theory also clarifies why aesthetic experience, in Beardsley's sense, can be *informed* by and responsive to these contextual factors. We do not perceive historical meaning directly, yet knowledge of history genuinely transforms aesthetic experience. Learning that a seemingly abstract composition was created as a deliberate response to political oppression, or that a representational work employs a technique derived from a specific cultural tradition, changes how we aesthetically encounter the work—not by adding external information but by deepening and reorienting the focus of attention itself.

### Dickie's Institutional Definition

George Dickie's institutional theory of art attempts to formalize and extend Danto's insights into a precise definition. For Dickie, an object qualifies as an artwork if and only if it has been *conferred* the status of candidate for appreciation by a representative of the institutional world of art. The institutional world comprises artists, curators, critics, collectors, museum professionals, and other gatekeepers whose practices and decisions collectively determine what counts as art within a given historical moment.

The elegance of Dickie's formulation lies in its capacity to accommodate both paradigmatic artworks and provocative borderline cases. A painting executed in traditional techniques, displayed in a museum, and discussed in art criticism is unambiguously an artwork because it has received institutional conferral. But so is Duchamp's urinal, because although its elevation to art status seemed scandalous when it occurred, it genuinely was presented to the artworld through institutional channels—it was submitted to a society exhibition, and despite its rejection, it circulated through artistic and critical discourse. The institutional framework conferred candidacy for aesthetic appreciation upon it.

However, Dickie's theory also reveals significant limitations when applied to edge cases and practical criticism. Consider the work of Jean-Michel Basquiat, who began his career creating *graffiti paintings* on New York street walls—interventions deliberately positioned outside institutional sanction. These works were subsequently discovered by the artworld, exhibited in galleries, and integrated into mainstream art discourse. Was Basquiat creating artworks even before institutional recognition occurred? Or did his graffiti become art only retroactively, through institutional absorption? Dickie's framework struggles with such historical sequences, though it can be amended to accommodate retrospective conferral and to recognize that artists may create outside institutional structures, with the understanding that their work can subsequently receive institutional status.

More fundamentally, the institutional theory raises the question of *authority*—who exactly possesses the right to confer artistic status? Is this authority distributed democratically among all members of the artworld, or does it concentrate in particular institutions like major museums or prestigious galleries? Does the artworld have centers and peripheries? Can marginalized or excluded communities create their own artworld institutions, establishing alternative criteria for artistic status? These questions move beyond pure philosophy into the sociology and politics of art, yet they arise necessarily from Dickie's framework.

The practical value of the institutional approach emerges most clearly when one confronts genuinely ambiguous cases. Suppose an engineer designs a bridge with meticulous attention to proportional harmony and visual elegance, intending the structure as an artwork first and a functional crossing second. The design is exhibited in architecture galleries, discussed in design publications, and appreciated by informed audiences for its aesthetic properties. Has the engineer created art? According to Dickie's theory, the answer depends on whether the appropriate institutional representatives have conferred artistic status upon the work. If architects, curators, and critics recognize it as art, then it is art. If they decline to do so, treating it instead as excellent engineering, then it remains outside the category of art despite its aesthetic ambitions.

This might seem to rob artistic status of meaningful content, but it actually clarifies what is at stake in artistic practice. Artists do not create artworks in isolation; they create them *for* an artworld, with the intention of securing a certain kind of recognition and entering a particular discourse. The institutional framework makes explicit what is often implicit in artistic practice: that art is a fundamentally social phenomenon involving communication, recognition, and collective validation.

### Goodman's Symbol Systems and Aesthetic Functions

Nelson Goodman approaches the problem of art from an entirely different angle, asking not "What is art?" but "What aesthetic function does art perform?" His answer centers on *symbol systems*—the various ways that objects can represent, denote, exemplify, and express meaning through their formal and structural properties.

For Goodman, a single physical object can serve as a symbol in multiple ways simultaneously. A painting might denote a historical scene (representational reference), exemplify certain color relationships and compositional principles (formal properties that the work possesses and presents as aesthetically salient), and express emotional or political positions (meaning that the work conveys through convention and context). These symbol functions are not added on top of the artwork's material substrate; they are aspects of how the artwork exists as a meaningful object.

Critically, Goodman argues that the *same physical object* can function as art in one context and as non-art in another, depending on how it is being used symbolically. A piece of driftwood discovered on a beach is nature; placed in a gallery, labeled as sculpture, and engaged as a repository of aesthetic attention, it functions as art. The transition does not require any change in the physical object itself but rather a change in the symbolic functions it is asked to perform and the frameworks through which those functions are recognized.

Goodman's insight extends classical institutional theory by clarifying what the conferral of artistic status actually *does*. It establishes new semantic conditions; it opens up possibilities for aesthetic function that did not previously obtain. The driftwood in the gallery exemplifies formal properties—its curves, its weathered surface, its spatial presence—in a way that demands aesthetic attention, whereas the identical object on the beach exemplifies only natural processes and utilitarian indifference.

This framework also illuminates the relationship between aesthetic experience and formal property recognition. When Beardsley describes aesthetic experience as involving discrimination of sensory properties and cognitive richness, he is describing the experience of a symbol system in action. The observer attends to formal properties not as mere sensory input but as *meaningful properties*, as elements that carry aesthetic significance precisely because they exemplify or express something worthy of attention.

Goodman's account furthermore explains why aesthetic properties often seem both *objective* and *responsive to interpretation*. A color chord in a painting objectively exhibits certain chromatic relationships—this is a fact about the physical pigments and light wavelengths. But whether this chord is aesthetically powerful, harmonious, or jarring depends on how it functions within the larger symbol system of the work and the interpretive framework through which the work is engaged. The same objective color relationship might exemplify serene unity in one context and aggressive cacophony in another. This is not subjective relativism but rather recognition that aesthetic properties are relational—they depend on the interaction between the object's intrinsic properties and the symbolic functions it is made to perform.

### Ontological Structures and the Problem of Multiple Instantiation

Goodman's symbol systems approach connects to deeper questions about the *ontological status* of artworks—the question of what kind of entity an artwork actually is. This question becomes especially vexed in temporal and performative arts. A musical composition exists in multiple instantiations: the score, various performances, recorded versions, and the abstract work itself. Which of these is the "real" artwork? Are they all instances of a single underlying work, or are they distinct artworks related by performance or interpretation?

Consider a string quartet by Beethoven. The physical score—the manuscript or printed notation—is certainly an artwork, or at least a component of an artwork. But the score is not the work itself; it is a set of instructions for creating performances. Each performance, rendered by different quartets at different times in different concert halls, realizes the work in a particular temporal instance. Yet we do not say that each performance is a separate artwork; we say they are all performances of the same work. The artwork is thus not identical to any particular performance but rather constituted by the abstract structure that the score specifies and that various performances instantiate.

This ontological analysis reveals that not all artworks have the same metaphysical structure. Paintings are typically *non-repeatable*—a particular canvas is the artwork, and reproductions are copies, not instances of the work itself. Musical compositions are *repeatable*—they admit of multiple correct instantiations that are equally authentic expressions of the work. Novels occupy an intermediate position: the abstract structure of the work (the sequence of words and narrative) is the artwork, and particular bound volumes are instantiations of this structure, yet we often speak of "first editions" and "the original manuscript" as though they possess special status.

These ontological differences have profound implications for how we preserve, restore, and critically engage with artworks. A painting can be damaged, and restoration attempts might alter its perceptual properties; the question of whether the restored work is still the "same" artwork depends on judgments about how much material change preserves identity. A musical work cannot be damaged in this way—if a score is lost, the work is lost as a definable entity, but existing performances do not become less authentic. A novel's manuscript can be lost without loss to the work itself; any correct printing instantiates the novel fully.

Furthermore, these ontological distinctions connect back to Goodman's symbol systems. The reason a painting cannot be "re-instantiated" the way a musical work can is that paintings function primarily as *autographic* symbols—symbols where the origin and identity of the work depend on the specific creative act and physical object. A Rembrandt painting is what it is partly because *Rembrandt created it*; an exact perceptual duplicate created by someone else would not be the same work, though it might be indistinguishable. Musical works, by contrast, function as *allographic* symbols—their identity depends on conformity to a notational scheme, not on the specific origin of each instantiation. Any competent performance that realizes the score correctly exemplifies the work.

This distinction proves crucial for understanding how the artworld recognizes and authenticates works. For autographic arts, questions of provenance and original production are central. For allographic arts, correctness of instantiation matters more than historical origin. These are not merely scholastic distinctions; they shape how institutions preserve, reproduce, and market artworks, and they ground our intuitions about forgery, restoration, and artistic authenticity.

### The Definitional Impossibility Theorem

Despite the considerable sophistication achieved by Beardsley, Danto, Dickie, and Goodman, the philosophical enterprise of providing a universally adequate definition of art has persistently failed. Each proposed definition encounters counterexamples or edge cases that challenge its scope. This is not accidental; it reflects a genuine feature of artistic practice that resists formalization.

Consider the classic move: "Art is an object created with the intention of being appreciated aesthetically." Counterexample: Duchamp's readymades are not created with aesthetic intention; they are existing industrial objects presented in art contexts. One might revise: "Art is an object that is presented to the artworld as a candidate for aesthetic appreciation." Counterexample: Protest art, documentary photography, and political performance art may reject aesthetic appreciation as their primary aim, insisting instead on ethical witness or political effect. One might revise again: "Art is any object whose creators intend it to have significance for human experience and meaning-making." But this becomes so broad that it encompasses everything from sacred ritual objects to educational textbooks.

The definitional impossibility theorem emerges from recognizing that artistic practice exhibits irreducible pluralism. Different traditions, historical periods, and communities have operated with fundamentally different conceptions of what art is and does.

This irreducible pluralism suggests that the search for a single, non-disjunctive definition of art may be a category error. Ludwig Wittgenstein's notion of family resemblance offers a more honest account: artworks share overlapping clusters of features—representation, expression, form, craft, institutional context, historical lineage—without any one feature being necessary or sufficient. Kendall Walton extended this insight by arguing that the correct aesthetic properties of a work are determined partly by the category under which it is perceived, meaning that aesthetic judgment is always already framed by conceptual and institutional context. What analytic aesthetics achieves, then, is not a definition of art but a cartography of the conditions under which art-talk functions coherently. Danto's historical matrices, Dickie's institutional roles, and Goodman's notational schemes are not competing answers to the same question; they are orthogonal frameworks that illuminate different dimensions of artistic practice. The lesson is methodological: philosophical clarity about art requires not a unified theory but a toolkit of precisely articulated distinctions, each applicable where it has traction and silent where it does not.

The lasting contribution of analytic aesthetics to institutional theory is precisely this disciplined pluralism. By insisting on conceptual rigor while remaining responsive to artistic practice, the tradition has produced frameworks robust enough to inform curatorial policy, copyright jurisprudence, conservation ethics, and market regulation without collapsing into ideology. The institutional theory does not tell us what art is worth making; it tells us what conditions must obtain for something to enter the space of artistic evaluation at all. That separation—between the conditions of art-status and the criteria of artistic value—is the theoretical achievement that distinguishes analytic aesthetics from its romantic and idealist predecessors. Future work in the field must extend these frameworks to digital and generative art, to community and participatory practices, and to non-Western institutional contexts where the artworld itself is configured differently, testing whether the core distinctions survive translation or require fundamental revision.
