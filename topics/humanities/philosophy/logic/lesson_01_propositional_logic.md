# Lesson 1: Propositional Logic — Syntax, Semantics, Connectives

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Propositional logic represents the foundational stratum of formal reasoning, the bedrock upon which all subsequent logical systems construct their edifices. At its core lies a deceptively simple insight: complex arguments decompose into atomic declarative sentences that possess precisely one of two truth values—true or false—with no intermediary states, no gradations, no ambiguity. This bivalent constraint represents the first and most critical invariant of classical propositional logic, distinguishing it sharply from fuzzy logics, modal logics, and other non-classical systems that permit truth-value pluralism or contextual dependency.

The genius of propositional logic emerges from its dual commitment to syntactic rigor and semantic determinacy. Syntactically, the system provides a finite alphabet of symbols and recursive formation rules that generate an infinite hierarchy of well-formed formulas. Semantically, it furnishes a truth-functional interpretation apparatus that mechanically computes the truth value of any compound formula from the truth values of its constituent atomic propositions, without reference to temporal context, possible worlds, or epistemic states of agents. This truth-functionality constitutes the second core invariant: the truth value of a compound proposition depends exclusively on the truth values of its components, never on their meaning, their causal relations, or their intensional content.

Consider the contrast with natural language reasoning, where the sentence "If John had studied harder, he would have passed the exam" invokes counterfactual conditionals, temporal ordering, and causal dependencies that resist truth-functional analysis. Propositional logic deliberately sacrifices this expressive richness to achieve something far more valuable for analytical philosophy: mechanical decidability. Every formula admits algorithmic verification. Every argument permits exhaustive truth-table analysis. Every alleged logical law submits to finite computational checking. This trade-off between expressiveness and decidability permeates all of formal logic, and propositional logic occupies the austere extreme where decidability reaches its zenith.

The historical genesis of propositional logic traces to Stoic philosophers of ancient Greece, particularly Chrysippus, who formalized argument patterns using unanalyzed propositions as primitive units. However, the modern symbolic formulation emerged in the nineteenth century through the work of George Boole, who recognized the algebraic structure underlying logical operations, and Gottlob Frege, who embedded propositional logic within his ambitious logicist program to reduce mathematics to pure logic. The twentieth-century refinement by Bertrand Russell, Ludwig Wittgenstein, and the Vienna Circle transformed propositional logic into a precision instrument for philosophical analysis, capable of exposing hidden fallacies in metaphysical argumentation and clarifying the logical structure of scientific theories.

The practical utility of propositional logic extends far beyond abstract philosophical debate. Legal reasoning frequently requires determining whether a statutory rule logically entails a particular judgment given established facts. Ethical deliberation demands assessing whether a proposed action coheres with one's professed moral principles or generates logical contradictions. Policy analysis necessitates evaluating whether the conjunction of empirical premises genuinely supports a recommended intervention. In each domain, propositional logic provides the diagnostic toolkit for detecting invalid inferences, identifying suppressed premises, and reconstructing implicit argumentative structures that resist ordinary language scrutiny.

The system operates through a clean separation of concerns. Syntax governs formula construction, establishing which symbol strings constitute legitimate logical expressions and which represent meaningless gibberish. Semantics governs truth-value assignment, establishing how formulas relate to the world through truth conditions. Proof theory, which we defer to subsequent lessons, governs derivational rules that permit transforming one formula into another through purely syntactic manipulations that preserve truth. This tripartite architecture—syntax, semantics, proof—recurs throughout formal logic, from first-order predicate logic through modal logic to higher-order systems, making propositional logic the essential training ground for all subsequent logical study.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The syntactic apparatus of propositional logic begins with an infinite countable alphabet partitioned into three disjoint classes. First, the propositional variables, conventionally denoted by lowercase Latin letters from the middle of the alphabet—*p*, *q*, *r*, *s*, and so forth, potentially subscripted for disambiguation as *p*₁, *p*₂, *p*₃—serve as atomic formulas representing indivisible declarative sentences. These variables function as placeholders for specific propositions such as "Socrates is mortal" or "The inflation rate exceeds five percent," deliberately abstracting away all internal grammatical structure to focus exclusively on truth-value behavior.

Second, the logical connectives provide the combinatorial operators that construct compound formulas from simpler components. Classical propositional logic employs five principal connectives, each characterized by its unique truth-functional behavior. The negation operator, symbolized as ¬ or ~, represents a unary connective that reverses truth value: if *p* is true, ¬*p* is false, and vice versa. The conjunction operator, symbolized as ∧ or &, represents a binary connective that yields truth only when both operands are true simultaneously. The disjunction operator, symbolized as ∨ or |, represents a binary connective that yields truth when at least one operand is true, embracing the inclusive interpretation where both operands may be true together. The material conditional, symbolized as → or ⊃, represents a binary connective that yields falsehood exclusively when the antecedent is true and the consequent false, a truth-functional interpretation that notoriously diverges from natural language conditionals. Finally, the biconditional operator, symbolized as ↔ or ≡, represents a binary connective that yields truth when both operands share identical truth values, either both true or both false.

Third, the punctuation symbols—parentheses, brackets, and braces—resolve structural ambiguity in compound formulas, ensuring unique syntactic parsing. The formula *p* ∧ *q* → *r* admits two distinct bracketings: (*p* ∧ *q*) → *r* asserts that the conjunction of *p* and *q* implies *r*, whereas *p* ∧ (*q* → *r*) asserts the conjunction of *p* with the conditional from *q* to *r*. These represent logically inequivalent propositions with divergent truth conditions, making explicit punctuation non-negotiable for syntactic precision.

The recursive definition of well-formed formulas (WFFs) establishes the boundary between legitimate logical expressions and meaningless symbol strings. The base clause stipulates that every propositional variable standing alone constitutes a well-formed formula. The recursive clauses stipulate that if φ is a well-formed formula, then ¬φ is a well-formed formula, and if φ and ψ are well-formed formulas, then (φ ∧ ψ), (φ ∨ ψ), (φ → ψ), and (φ ↔ ψ) are well-formed formulas. The closure clause stipulates that nothing else qualifies as a well-formed formula unless generated by finite application of the base and recursive clauses. This inductive architecture enables rigorous metalogical proofs by structural induction, a technique exploited extensively in completeness and soundness theorems.

The semantic apparatus assigns meaning to well-formed formulas through truth valuations. A valuation function *v* maps each propositional variable to exactly one element of the truth-value set {T, F}, where T represents truth and F represents falsehood. The valuation extends recursively to compound formulas according to the truth-functional definitions of connectives. For negation, *v*(¬φ) = T if and only if *v*(φ) = F. For conjunction, *v*(φ ∧ ψ) = T if and only if both *v*(φ) = T and *v*(ψ) = T. For disjunction, *v*(φ ∨ ψ) = T if and only if at least one of *v*(φ) = T or *v*(ψ) = T holds. For the material conditional, *v*(φ → ψ) = F if and only if *v*(φ) = T and *v*(ψ) = F, defaulting to truth in all other cases. For the biconditional, *v*(φ ↔ ψ) = T if and only if *v*(φ) and *v*(ψ) share the same truth value.

Truth tables provide the canonical computational instrument for evaluating propositional formulas. The table enumerates all possible truth-value assignments to atomic variables, computes intermediate compound subformulas from innermost to outermost according to connective definitions, and terminates with the truth value of the complete formula under each valuation. For a formula containing *n* distinct propositional variables, the truth table requires 2ⁿ rows, each representing one complete valuation. The exponential growth imposes practical computational limits: a formula with ten variables demands 1,024 rows, while twenty variables explodes to over one million rows, rendering hand computation infeasible and motivating algorithmic approaches such as binary decision diagrams and SAT solvers.

Consider the truth table for the formula (*p* → *q*) ↔ (¬*p* ∨ *q*), which asserts logical equivalence between the material conditional and a disjunction involving negation:

```text
p  q  | ¬p | p→q | ¬p∨q | (p→q)↔(¬p∨q)
------|-------|-------|---------|------------------
T  T  |  F  |  T  |  T   |       T
T  F  |  F  |  F  |  F   |       T
F  T  |  T  |  T  |  T   |       T
F  F  |  T  |  T  |  T   |       T
```

The final column displays T across all four rows, demonstrating that the biconditional holds under every possible valuation. This property characterizes the formula as a tautology, a proposition that cannot be false, a logical truth that remains valid independently of empirical facts. Tautologies represent the theorems of propositional logic, the formulas derivable from the empty set of premises through pure logical reasoning.

Contradictions occupy the opposite semantic extreme: formulas that evaluate to falsehood under every valuation. The formula *p* ∧ ¬*p* exemplifies a contradiction, asserting simultaneously that *p* holds and *p* fails to hold. The truth table contains F across all rows in the final column, certifying logical impossibility. No state of affairs, no matter how bizarre or counterfactual, could render a contradiction true, distinguishing logical impossibility from mere physical or metaphysical impossibility.

Contingent formulas occupy the semantic middle ground, evaluating to truth under some valuations and falsehood under others. The formula *p* ∨ *q* represents a contingency, true when at least one variable receives truth assignment T but false when both receive F. Contingent formulas correspond to ordinary empirical propositions whose truth depends on how the world actually is, contrasting with the necessary truth of tautologies and the necessary falsehood of contradictions.

Logical equivalence obtains between two formulas φ and ψ when they share identical truth values under every valuation, notated as φ ≡ ψ. The truth table method verifies equivalence by confirming that the final columns for φ and ψ match row by row. Logical equivalence represents a stronger relation than the biconditional connective: while φ ↔ ψ may be contingent—true under some valuations and false under others—the equivalence φ ≡ ψ holds only when φ ↔ ψ constitutes a tautology, true under all valuations. This distinction between object-language connectives and metalanguage relations pervades logical theory and demands careful navigation to avoid confusion.

The semantic consequence relation, notated as Γ ⊨ φ where Γ represents a set of formulas and φ represents a single formula, asserts that every valuation making all members of Γ true simultaneously makes φ true. This captures the informal notion of valid inference: the conclusion follows logically from the premises. When Γ is empty, the notation ⊨ φ reduces to asserting that φ is a tautology. When Γ contains a single formula ψ, the notation ψ ⊨ φ asserts that φ is true whenever ψ is true. The semantic consequence relation admits algorithmic verification through truth tables: construct the table for all formulas in Γ ∪ {φ}, identify rows where every formula in Γ receives T, then verify that φ receives T in all such rows.

The substitution principle provides a powerful equivalence-preserving transformation. If φ and ψ are logically equivalent, then replacing every occurrence of φ within a larger formula χ by ψ yields a formula logically equivalent to χ. This principle licenses algebraic manipulation of logical formulas, transforming complex expressions into simpler canonical forms. The equivalence (*p* → *q*) ≡ (¬*p* ∨ *q*) permits systematically eliminating conditionals from formulas by substituting disjunctions, a preprocessing step exploited by many automated theorem provers to reduce formulas to clausal normal form.

De Morgan's laws constitute the most frequently deployed equivalence transformations, asserting that ¬(*p* ∧ *q*) ≡ (¬*p* ∨ ¬*q*) and ¬(*p* ∨ *q*) ≡ (¬*p* ∧ ¬*q*). These laws describe how negation distributes across conjunctions and disjunctions, enabling systematic negation propagation inward toward atomic variables. The double negation law ¬¬*p* ≡ *p* permits eliminating redundant negation pairs. The associativity laws (*p* ∧ *q*) ∧ *r* ≡ *p* ∧ (*q* ∧ *r*) and (*p* ∨ *q*) ∨ *r* ≡ *p* ∨ (*q* ∨ *r*) justify dropping internal parentheses in iterated conjunctions and disjunctions. The commutativity laws *p* ∧ *q* ≡ *q* ∧ *p* and *p* ∨ *q* ≡ *q* ∨ *p* permit reordering operands. The distributivity laws *p* ∧ (*q* ∨ *r*) ≡ (*p* ∧ *q*) ∨ (*p* ∧ *r*) and *p* ∨ (*q* ∧ *r*) ≡ (*p* ∨ *q*) ∧ (*p* ∨ *r*) enable converting between conjunctive and disjunctive normal forms.

These equivalence laws generate a complete algebra of propositional formulas, enabling mechanical simplification and transformation. The practical workflow for complex formula evaluation proceeds through a systematic pipeline. First, parse the formula to identify syntactic structure, making all implicit parentheses explicit according to standard precedence conventions where negation binds most tightly, followed by conjunction, disjunction, conditional, and biconditional. Second, identify all distinct propositional variables, ordering them alphabetically for systematic enumeration. Third, construct the truth table with 2ⁿ rows, filling in variable assignments using binary counting patterns. Fourth, evaluate subformulas column by column, proceeding from atomic variables through progressively larger compounds until reaching the main connective. Fifth, analyze the final column to classify the formula as tautology, contradiction, or contingency, and identify specific valuations witnessing satisfiability.

The computational complexity of truth-table evaluation scales exponentially with variable count, but practical optimizations exploit structural properties. Short-circuit evaluation terminates conjunction evaluation as soon as one conjunct evaluates to false, and terminates disjunction evaluation as soon as one disjunct evaluates to true. Memoization caches intermediate subformula results to avoid redundant recomputation when identical subformulas appear multiple times. Symmetry detection identifies equivalent valuations under variable permutation, reducing the effective search space. Binary decision diagrams represent formulas as directed acyclic graphs, exploiting shared substructure to achieve polynomial space representation for many practically important formula classes.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

The primary bottleneck in propositional reasoning emerges from the exponential explosion of truth-table rows as variable count increases. A formula involving twenty-five variables requires evaluating over thirty-three million distinct valuations, rendering exhaustive truth-table construction computationally prohibitive even with modern processors. This complexity barrier motivates a repertoire of optimization strategies that exploit logical structure to avoid exhaustive enumeration.

Semantic tableaux, also called truth trees, provide a refutation-based decision procedure that often terminates far earlier than exhaustive enumeration by searching systematically for a satisfying valuation. The method constructs a branching tree structure where each branch represents a partial valuation, extending branches by decomposing compound formulas according to their main connectives, and terminating branches that generate explicit contradictions. When all branches terminate in contradictions, the original formula is unsatisfiable; when at least one branch remains open after exhaustive decomposition, that branch encodes a satisfying valuation. For many naturally occurring formulas, particularly those containing redundant structure or hidden contradictions, semantic tableaux terminate exponentially faster than truth tables by pruning the search space through early contradiction detection.

Resolution proof systems provide an alternative approach optimized for formulas in clausal normal form, where the formula consists of a conjunction of disjunctive clauses. The resolution rule permits inferring a new clause from two existing clauses that contain complementary literals: from (*p* ∨ *q*) and (¬*p* ∨ *r*), infer (*q* ∨ *r*). Iteratively applying resolution generates new clauses until either the empty clause is derived—certifying unsatisfiability—or no new clauses can be generated—certifying satisfiability. Resolution forms the computational foundation for modern SAT solvers, which incorporate sophisticated heuristics for clause selection, variable ordering, and conflict-driven learning to handle industrial-scale formulas with millions of variables.

Normal form transformations represent critical preprocessing steps that convert arbitrary formulas into standardized canonical representations amenable to algorithmic manipulation. Negation normal form (NNF) requires that negations appear only immediately before propositional variables, achieved by recursively applying De Morgan's laws and double negation elimination. Conjunctive normal form (CNF) requires the formula to be structured as a conjunction of disjunctive clauses, each clause consisting of a disjunction of literals. Disjunctive normal form (DNF) inverts this structure, requiring a disjunction of conjunctive clauses. The Tseytin transformation converts arbitrary formulas to equisatisfiable CNF formulas in linear time by introducing auxiliary variables representing subformulas, trading a modest increase in variable count for dramatic structural simplification.

The practical workflow for verifying semantic consequence Γ ⊨ φ leverages the relationship between consequence and satisfiability. The consequence holds if and only if the formula (γ₁ ∧ γ₂ ∧ ... ∧ γₙ ∧ ¬φ) is unsatisfiable, where γ₁ through γₙ enumerate the premises in Γ. This reduction transforms the consequence problem into a satisfiability problem, enabling deployment of optimized SAT-solving algorithms rather than constructing multi-formula truth tables. When the formula is unsatisfiable, the consequence is valid; when the formula is satisfiable, the satisfying valuation provides a counterexample demonstrating that the premises can be true while the conclusion is false.

Common failure modes in propositional reasoning typically stem from misunderstanding the material conditional's truth-functional semantics. The conditional *p* → *q* evaluates to true whenever the antecedent *p* is false, regardless of the consequent's truth value. This behavior, termed vacuous truth, generates counterintuitive results: the proposition "If the moon is made of cheese, then Paris is the capital of France" counts as true, and so does "If the moon is made of cheese, then Paris is the capital of Germany." Natural language conditionals typically carry implicit causal, temporal, or counterfactual content that material conditionals ignore entirely. Recognizing this semantic gap prevents misapplying propositional logic to arguments whose validity depends essentially on non-truth-functional factors.

The affirming-the-consequent fallacy exemplifies invalid inference patterns that appear superficially plausible. From the premises *p* → *q* and *q*, one cannot validly infer *p*. The truth table reveals valuations where both premises are true but the conclusion is false: specifically, when *p* is false and *q* is true. The conditional *p* → *q* merely asserts that *p* suffices for *q*, not that *p* is necessary for *q*. Multiple distinct antecedents may produce the same consequent, rendering backward inference from consequent to antecedent invalid. Medical diagnosis frequently falls prey to this fallacy: from "If disease D is present, symptom S appears" and "Symptom S appears," one cannot conclude "Disease D is present" without ruling out alternative causal pathways.

The denying-the-antecedent fallacy represents the symmetric error. From *p* → *q* and ¬*p*, one cannot validly infer ¬*q*. Again, the conditional asserts sufficiency rather than necessity: the falsity of *p* leaves entirely open whether *q* holds through some alternative pathway. Policy debates routinely commit this fallacy: from "If we implement policy P, outcome O will improve" and "We did not implement policy P," one cannot conclude "Outcome O did not improve," as other interventions may have achieved the same result.

Functional completeness represents a critical metalogical property: a set of connectives is functionally complete if every possible truth function can be expressed using only connectives from that set. The five classical connectives {¬, ∧, ∨, →, ↔} contain redundancy; smaller subsets suffice. The set {¬, ∧} is functionally complete, as is {¬, ∨}, since disjunction can be defined via De Morgan's law as ¬(¬*p* ∧ ¬*q*), and conjunction can similarly be defined as ¬(¬*p* ∨ ¬*q*). Even more remarkably, the single Sheffer stroke (NAND) operator proves functionally complete in isolation, as does the NOR operator. This theoretical result has practical implications for circuit design in digital electronics, where implementing a universal gate simplifies manufacturing, and for proof theory, where selecting a minimal complete connective set streamlines derivational rules.

The scope ambiguity failure mode arises when implicit operator precedence leads to misinterpreting formula structure. The expression *p* ∨ *q* ∧ *r* admits two distinct parsings: *p* ∨ (*q* ∧ *r*) versus (*p* ∨ *q*) ∧ *r*, and these represent logically inequivalent formulas. Standard convention assigns conjunction higher precedence than disjunction, resolving the ambiguity to *p* ∨ (*q* ∧ *r*), but relying on implicit precedence invites error. Defensive practice mandates explicit parenthesization for all complex formulas, sacrificing notational brevity for absolute clarity.

The substitution instance confusion arises when distinguishing between schematic formula variables appearing in metalogical statements and propositional variables within object-language formulas. When a logical law states "φ ∧ ψ ≡ ψ ∧ φ for all formulas φ and ψ," the variables φ and ψ range over arbitrary formulas, not merely atomic propositions. The law permits inferring ((*p* → *q*) ∧ *r*) ≡ (*r* ∧ (*p* → *q*)), where the compound formula (*p* → *q*) has been substituted for φ. Failing to recognize that schematic variables admit compound formula substitution leads to artificially restricting the applicability of logical laws.

Resource optimization in large-scale propositional reasoning demands strategic formula decomposition. When evaluating whether a complex argument is valid, resist the temptation to construct a single monolithic truth table encompassing all premises and the conclusion. Instead, incrementally verify that each inferential step preserves truth, building a chain of smaller verified inferences that collectively establish the conclusion. This modular approach exploits human short-term memory constraints by limiting the working set of formulas under simultaneous consideration, and creates reusable intermediate results that may accelerate verification of related arguments.

The metatheoretic landscape of propositional logic provides the foundation for assessing its scope and limits. The system is decidable: an effective algorithm exists for determining whether any given formula is a tautology. The system is sound: every formula derivable through proof rules is semantically valid. The system is complete: every semantically valid formula admits a formal derivation. These properties make propositional logic an ideal vehicle for learning formal reasoning, as syntactic derivation and semantic truth invariably converge. However, this convergence comes at the cost of expressiveness. Propositional logic cannot represent relational structure, quantification over domains, or temporal progression—limitations that motivate the ascent to first-order logic, modal logic, and temporal logic in subsequent lessons. Understanding precisely what propositional logic can and cannot express prevents misapplying its tools to problems whose logical structure exceeds its representational capacity.
