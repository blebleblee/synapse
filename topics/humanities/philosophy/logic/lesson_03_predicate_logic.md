# Lesson 3: First-Order Predicate Logic — Quantifiers, Variables, Interpretations

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Propositional logic granted us the capacity to model compound claims through truth-functional connectives, yet it suffered a catastrophic expressive limitation: internal structure. Consider the argument "All humans are mortal; Socrates is human; therefore, Socrates is mortal." Propositional logic sees only three atomic propositions P, Q, R connected by modus ponens, blind to the shared subject-predicate architecture binding these claims together. First-order predicate logic—also called first-order logic (FOL) or the predicate calculus—surgically opens these atomic propositions to reveal their internal machinery: individual objects, properties those objects possess, relations holding between objects, and crucially, quantificational claims ranging over entire domains of discourse.

The foundational innovation lies in distinguishing **ontological commitments** from **functional structure**. Where propositional logic treated "Socrates is mortal" as an indivisible semantic unit, first-order logic decomposes it into a predicate M (the property of being mortal) applied to an individual constant s (the specific object Socrates), notated M(s). This seemingly modest typographical shift purchases enormous expressive power: we can now articulate claims not merely about specific named individuals but about all objects satisfying certain conditions or asserting the existence of at least one object with particular properties. The universal quantifier ∀ and existential quantifier ∃ transform first-order logic from a calculus of specific facts into a language capable of expressing general laws, mathematical theorems, scientific hypotheses, and metaphysical commitments about the furniture of reality.

The semantic architecture rests on three interdependent structures: syntax (the formal grammar rules governing well-formed formulas), interpretation (the assignment of meaning by mapping symbols to objects and relations in a domain), and satisfaction (the recursive truth conditions determining when a formula holds relative to an interpretation and variable assignment). Unlike propositional logic where truth valuations mapped sentence letters directly to truth values, first-order semantics introduces a critical mediating layer: the **domain of discourse** D, a non-empty set of objects constituting the universe about which we make claims. An interpretation I pairs this domain with functions mapping individual constants to specific domain elements, n-ary predicate symbols to sets of n-tuples from D, and n-ary function symbols to operations on D. This interpretation machinery creates a **first-order structure** 〈D, I〉, the mathematical object serving as the semantic foundation for all truth evaluations.

The most philosophically consequential feature emerges from the interaction between quantifiers and variable binding. A variable x appearing within the scope of a quantifier ∀x or ∃x becomes **bound**, its reference constrained by the quantifier's ranging behavior over the domain. Variables not governed by any quantifier remain **free**, functioning analogously to parameters in mathematical functions—their interpretation depends on context supplied externally through variable assignments. This distinction between bound and free variables generates subtle semantic phenomena: the same formula may express different propositions depending on how free variables receive interpretation, and formulas containing free variables lack determinate truth values until those variables receive assignments. A **sentence** in first-order logic is precisely a formula with no free variables, a closed expression asserting a complete proposition independent of external variable assignments.

The expressive reach of first-order logic extends remarkably far: we can formalize mathematical structures (groups, fields, ordered sets), axiomatize scientific theories (Newtonian mechanics, thermodynamics), encode database queries, and model reasoning in natural language—yet critical expressive boundaries remain. First-order logic cannot directly express claims about properties of properties (second-order quantification), cannot pin down the natural numbers up to isomorphism (no first-order theory with an infinite model is categorical), and cannot define certain mathematical concepts like well-ordering or finiteness. These limitations are not accidental defects but structural consequences of the logic's design, trading unlimited expressive power for tractable proof theory and decidability results. Understanding first-order logic means grasping both its formidable reach and its principled boundaries, recognizing where it illuminates and where higher-order systems or modal extensions become necessary.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The syntactic machinery of first-order logic stratifies into three hierarchical layers: the vocabulary (primitive symbols available for formula construction), the formation rules (recursive grammar generating well-formed formulas), and the variable-binding apparatus (scope rules governing quantifier influence). The vocabulary partitions into logical symbols—common across all first-order languages and including variables, quantifiers, connectives, equality, and parentheses—and non-logical symbols specific to particular domains, comprising individual constants, predicate symbols, and function symbols. Each predicate and function symbol carries an **arity** specifying the number of arguments it accepts: a unary predicate like M(x) expresses a property, a binary predicate like L(x,y) expresses a relation between two objects, and an n-ary predicate captures complex relational structures. Function symbols similarly accept arguments and return domain elements, enabling terms like f(x) or g(a,b) representing computed objects rather than bare constants.

The formation rules construct **terms** and **formulas** through mutually recursive definitions. A term represents a referring expression denoting a domain object: every individual constant qualifies as a term, every variable qualifies as a term, and if f is an n-ary function symbol and t₁...tₙ are terms, then f(t₁...tₙ) qualifies as a term. This recursive structure permits arbitrarily complex nested terms like f(g(x,a),h(b)). Atomic formulas arise by applying predicates to terms: if P is an n-ary predicate and t₁...tₙ are terms, then P(t₁...tₙ) constitutes an atomic formula, as does the equality expression t₁ = t₂. Complex formulas build recursively: if φ and ψ are formulas, then ¬φ, (φ ∧ ψ), (φ ∨ ψ), (φ → ψ), and (φ ↔ ψ) are formulas; critically, if φ is a formula and x is a variable, then ∀x φ and ∃x φ are formulas. This grammar ensures that every syntactically legitimate expression admits unambiguous parsing despite potentially deep nesting.

Variable binding operates through scope mechanics precisely analogous to lexical scoping in programming languages. The **scope** of a quantifier ∀x or ∃x extends over the formula immediately following it, defining the region within which that quantifier binds the variable x. A variable occurrence is **bound** if it falls within the scope of a quantifier governing that variable; otherwise it remains **free**. Consider the formula ∀x (P(x) → Q(x,y)). The variable x appears bound throughout by the initial universal quantifier, but y remains free, uncontrolled by any quantifier. The same variable can exhibit both bound and free occurrences in different subformulas: in ∀x P(x) ∧ Q(x), the first x-occurrence is bound while the second is free. When multiple quantifiers govern the same variable letter, innermost binding takes precedence—∀x P(x) ∧ ∃x Q(x) contains two distinct binding relationships despite surface notation reuse.

The semantic interpretation machinery constructs meaning through a three-stage process: establishing a first-order structure, defining variable assignments, and recursively computing satisfaction relations. A **first-order structure** or **interpretation** consists of a non-empty domain D paired with an interpretation function I mapping each individual constant c to an element I(c) ∈ D, each n-ary function symbol f to a function I(f): Dⁿ → D, and each n-ary predicate symbol P to a set I(P) ⊆ Dⁿ representing the n-tuples satisfying the predicate. The domain choice profoundly affects semantic outcomes: interpreting arithmetic formulas over natural numbers yields different truths than interpreting them over real numbers or finite fields. A **variable assignment** s maps each variable to a domain element, notated s(x) ∈ D. These assignments handle free variables, providing the contextual reference needed to evaluate formulas that are not sentences.

Satisfaction—the recursive truth definition—proceeds by structural induction on formula complexity. For an atomic formula P(t₁...tₙ), we evaluate each term tᵢ under interpretation I and assignment s to obtain domain elements, then check whether the resulting tuple belongs to I(P). Term evaluation interprets constants through I, variables through s, and function applications by recursively evaluating arguments then applying the interpreted function. The satisfaction relation, notated I ⊨_s φ (read "I satisfies φ under assignment s"), extends to complex formulas through the standard truth-functional clauses for connectives, then introduces novel clauses for quantifiers. The formula ∀x φ is satisfied by I under s if and only if I satisfies φ under every x-variant of s—that is, under every assignment s' differing from s at most in what it assigns to x. Dually, ∃x φ is satisfied if and only if I satisfies φ under at least one x-variant of s. These clauses capture the intuitive ranging behavior: universal quantifiers demand satisfaction across all domain elements, existential quantifiers require satisfaction for some witness element.

```text
First-Order Satisfaction Recursion:

Atomic:     I ⊨_s P(t₁...tₙ)  iff  〈I_s(t₁),...,I_s(tₙ)〉 ∈ I(P)
Negation:   I ⊨_s ¬φ          iff  I ⊭_s φ
Conjunction: I ⊨_s (φ ∧ ψ)    iff  I ⊨_s φ and I ⊨_s ψ
Disjunction: I ⊨_s (φ ∨ ψ)    iff  I ⊨_s φ or I ⊨_s ψ
Conditional: I ⊨_s (φ → ψ)    iff  I ⊭_s φ or I ⊨_s ψ
Universal:   I ⊨_s ∀x φ        iff  I ⊨_s' φ for all x-variants s' of s
Existential: I ⊨_s ∃x φ        iff  I ⊨_s' φ for some x-variant s' of s

where I_s(t) denotes term evaluation:
  I_s(c) = I(c) for constants
  I_s(x) = s(x) for variables
  I_s(f(t₁...tₙ)) = I(f)(I_s(t₁),...,I_s(tₙ)) for function terms
```

When evaluating sentences—formulas with no free variables—the variable assignment becomes semantically inert since no free variables require assignment. We write I ⊨ φ (omitting the assignment) to indicate that interpretation I satisfies sentence φ. This observation justifies the philosophical significance of sentences as expressing complete, context-independent propositions. A sentence φ is **valid** (notated ⊨ φ) if every interpretation in every domain satisfies it, capturing logical truth independent of empirical facts. A sentence is **satisfiable** if at least one interpretation satisfies it, and **unsatisfiable** if no interpretation satisfies it. An argument from premises Γ to conclusion φ is **valid** (Γ ⊨ φ) if every interpretation satisfying all premises in Γ also satisfies φ, formalizing the notion of truth-preserving inference.

The interplay between quantifier order and scope generates critical semantic distinctions. The formula ∀x ∃y L(x,y) asserts that for every x there exists some y (potentially depending on x) such that L(x,y) holds—perhaps "everyone loves someone," where the beloved may vary per lover. Reversing quantifiers yields ∃y ∀x L(x,y), claiming there exists a single y such that for all x, L(x,y) holds—"someone is loved by everyone," a far stronger claim requiring one universally beloved individual. These formulas are not logically equivalent; quantifier order matters decisively. Similarly, negation interacts with quantifiers through duality laws: ¬∀x φ is logically equivalent to ∃x ¬φ (denying universal satisfaction means some element fails), and ¬∃x φ is logically equivalent to ∀x ¬φ (denying existential satisfaction means all elements fail). These equivalences enable systematic formula manipulation, pushing negations inward through De Morgan-style transformations extended to quantificational structure.

Prenex normal form provides a canonical representation isolating quantifier complexity from propositional structure. A formula is in **prenex normal form** if all quantifiers appear at the outermost syntactic layer, followed by a quantifier-free matrix: Q₁x₁ Q₂x₂ ... Qₙxₙ ψ, where each Qᵢ is ∀ or ∃ and ψ contains no quantifiers. Every first-order formula admits logically equivalent prenex reformulation through systematic application of quantifier scope expansion rules, distributing quantifiers outward while renaming bound variables to avoid capture. This normal form clarifies the quantifier alternation depth—a measure of formula complexity correlating with computational difficulty in automated theorem proving and model checking. Formulas with quantifier prefix ∃...∃∀...∀ (existential-universal) or ∀...∀∃...∃ (universal-existential) form important decidability boundary cases in automated reasoning systems.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Practical mastery of first-order logic requires cultivating operational facility across three performance dimensions: formula construction fluency (translating natural language or mathematical claims into precise logical notation), semantic analysis competence (rapidly determining satisfiability, validity, and consequence relations through model construction and countermodel generation), and proof-theoretic manipulation skill (applying inference rules to derive conclusions or establish semantic properties syntactically). Each dimension harbors characteristic failure modes that impede analytical progress; recognizing and systematically mitigating these failure patterns separates competent practitioners from expert logicians.

Formula construction stumbles most frequently at quantifier scope determination and variable binding hygiene. Natural language exhibits systematic scope ambiguities absent explicit disambiguation: "Every student read a book" admits two logical representations depending on whether the existential claim scopes narrowly (each student read some book, possibly different books) or widely (there exists one particular book every student read). The narrow-scope reading translates as ∀x (Student(x) → ∃y (Book(y) ∧ Read(x,y))), placing existential quantification within the conditional consequent. The wide-scope reading becomes ∃y (Book(y) ∧ ∀x (Student(x) → Read(x,y))), hoisting the existential quantifier outside the universal. Determining intended scope requires pragmatic context analysis beyond syntactic parsing—a skill developed through extensive translation practice comparing scopal alternatives against intuitive truth conditions.

Variable capture during formula manipulation poses a second critical failure mode. Consider attempting to distribute a quantifier over conjunction: given ∀x (P(x) ∧ Q(y)), one might incorrectly push the quantifier inward to obtain (∀x P(x)) ∧ (∀x Q(y)), which appears legitimate superficially. However, this transformation becomes catastrophically wrong if we subsequently substitute a term containing x for y. The safe move requires restricting quantifier distribution to subformulas in which the quantified variable actually appears free: ∀x (P(x) ∧ Q(y)) is equivalent to (∀x P(x)) ∧ Q(y), where Q(y) escapes quantification since y remains unbound by ∀x. Variable renaming (alpha-conversion) provides the defensive technique: systematically rename bound variables to fresh symbols not appearing elsewhere in the formula before performing transformations. This discipline, borrowed from lambda calculus implementation, prevents accidental binding conflicts during structural manipulation.

Semantic analysis accelerates through strategic model construction focusing on minimal witnesses. When assessing satisfiability of a formula like ∃x (P(x) ∧ ¬Q(x)) ∧ ∀y (P(y) → R(y)), construct the smallest domain sufficient to provide witnesses for existential claims: a single-element domain D = {a} where I(P) = {a}, I(Q) = ∅, and I(R) = {a}. This interpretation satisfies both conjuncts since a witnesses the existential claim (being P but not Q) and the universal claim holds vacuously over the singleton domain. Conversely, establishing invalidity demands countermodel construction: to show ∀x P(x) → ∀x Q(x) is not valid, construct an interpretation where P holds universally but Q fails somewhere, perhaps D = {a,b}, I(P) = {a,b}, I(Q) = {a}. Element b falsifies the consequent while the antecedent holds, demonstrating the conditional can fail. This countermodel-driven refutation technique exploits the semantic definition of validity directly, short-circuiting proof-theoretic search.

Quantifier instantiation—the logical operation replacing quantified variables with specific terms—requires careful attention to variable freedom constraints. Universal instantiation permits replacing ∀x φ with φ[t/x] (φ with t substituted for x) provided no variable free in t becomes bound by a quantifier in φ during substitution. The substitution φ[t/x] is **admissible** only when this capture-freedom condition holds. Consider ∀x ∃y L(x,y): instantiating x with y yields ∃y L(y,y), where the formerly free y in the term becomes bound by the internal existential quantifier, altering the formula's meaning. The correct instantiation requires renaming the bound y first: from alpha-equivalent ∀x ∃z L(x,z), instantiation with y safely yields ∃z L(y,z). Existential instantiation operates inversely: from ∃x φ we may infer φ[c/x] where c is a fresh constant symbol not appearing elsewhere, representing an arbitrary witness to existential satisfaction. This asymmetry between universal and existential instantiation reflects their dual semantic behaviors.

The expressive limitations of first-order logic manifest most acutely in three domains: defining finiteness, expressing well-foundedness, and quantifying over properties. No single first-order sentence can assert "the domain is finite" in a way true precisely of finite structures and false of infinite ones. We can write formulas ∃x₁...∃xₙ ∀y (y=x₁ ∨ ... ∨ y=xₙ) asserting domain size exactly n for any specific natural number n, but no finite set of such sentences captures arbitrary finiteness. This limitation stems from the compactness theorem: if every finite subset of a sentence set has a model, the entire infinite set has a model. An attempted axiomatization of finiteness through infinitely many size-bound sentences admits infinite models satisfying every finite subset. Similarly, first-order logic cannot define the transitive closure of a relation or assert that a relation is well-founded—concepts requiring impredicative second-order quantification over all subsets.

Prenex transformation workflow optimization exploits systematic quantifier migration patterns. Given an arbitrary formula, drive negations inward using De Morgan laws and quantifier negation duality until negations apply only to atomic formulas. Next, rename bound variables throughout to ensure all quantified variables use distinct symbols, eliminating shadowing. Finally, migrate quantifiers outward using scope expansion equivalences: (∀x φ) ∧ ψ becomes ∀x (φ ∧ ψ) when x does not occur free in ψ; (∀x φ) ∨ ψ becomes ∀x (φ ∨ ψ) under the same condition; similar rules govern existential quantifiers. This mechanical procedure converts any formula into prenex form while preserving logical equivalence, yielding a standardized representation amenable to automated theorem proving algorithms like resolution or tableaux methods.

Diagnostic reasoning with first-order formalizations requires distinguishing model-theoretic consequence from proof-theoretic derivability. Model-theoretic validity (Γ ⊨ φ) asserts that every interpretation satisfying all premises in Γ satisfies conclusion φ, a semantic property potentially requiring infinite case analysis over all possible interpretations. Proof-theoretic derivability (Γ ⊢ φ) asserts the existence of a finite formal derivation from premises to conclusion using specified inference rules, a syntactic property checkable mechanically. Gödel's completeness theorem for first-order logic establishes that these notions coincide: Γ ⊨ φ if and only if Γ ⊢ φ, meaning semantic consequence always admits syntactic demonstration. This powerful metatheoretic result guarantees that automated proof search is not futile—valid arguments always possess finite proofs, though finding those proofs may require unbounded search (first-order validity is semidecidable but not decidable).

When first-order formalization proves inadequate, recognize the symptoms indicating need for extended logics. If you must quantify over properties or relations (expressing "all transitive relations" or "there exists a function"), second-order logic becomes necessary, trading first-order's complete proof theory for vastly greater expressive power. If you need to express knowledge, belief, necessity, or temporal evolution, modal logics augment first-order apparatus with possible-worlds semantics and accessibility relations. If you need to reason about definability or self-reference, higher-order logics or type theories provide the necessary impredicative resources. First-order logic occupies a sweet spot balancing expressiveness against tractability: rich enough to formalize substantial mathematical and scientific theories, yet constrained enough to admit complete proof calculi and partial decision procedures. Understanding its boundaries clarifies when to extend the formalism and when to reformulate problems to exploit its considerable but limited reach.
