# Lesson 2: Extensive Form Games, Backward Induction, Subgame Perfection

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The strategic-form representation of games, explored in our first lesson, treats decision-making as a simultaneous phenomenon where players commit to complete contingent plans without observing intermediate moves. Yet many economically and computationally relevant interactions unfold sequentially: a firm enters a market and an incumbent responds; a prosecutor offers a plea bargain and the defendant accepts or rejects; a chess player moves a knight and the opponent calculates optimal replies. The extensive form provides the natural mathematical language for such dynamic scenarios, encoding not merely the outcome function over strategy profiles but the full temporal structure of information revelation and decision sequencing.

An extensive-form game specifies a finite or infinite tree where nodes represent decision points, edges represent available actions, and terminal nodes carry payoff vectors. The tree structure imposes a strict partial order on decision moments, distinguishing the primitives of *actions* (choices available at a single node) from *strategies* (complete contingent plans specifying an action at every decision node belonging to a player). This distinction proves critical: while a strategy in strategic form is an atomic object, in extensive form it decomposes into a potentially infinite conjunction of local action specifications, one per information set. The strategy space therefore explodes combinatorially with tree depth, creating both computational challenges and rich equilibrium structures absent from the strategic-form model.

Information sets partition the nodes assigned to a player into equivalence classes where that player cannot distinguish between nodes within the same set. Perfect information games assign each node its own singleton information set, granting players full knowledge of the history when choosing. Imperfect information games contain non-singleton information sets, modeling uncertainty about prior moves or simultaneous choice. The mathematical requirement that actions available at all nodes within an information set must coincide ensures that strategies remain well-defined functions from information sets to actions. This seemingly innocuous condition imposes deep structural constraints: for instance, it forbids trees where a player moves at two nodes connected by a directed path unless an opponent's move intervenes, as the player would then "forget" their own earlier action.

The fundamental tension in extensive-form solution concepts arises from the interplay between global optimality and local rationality. A Nash equilibrium requires each player's complete strategy to be a best response to opponents' strategies, but this global condition permits incredible threats—moves that would be suboptimal if reached yet nonetheless sustain equilibrium because the equilibrium path avoids them. Consider the canonical entry-deterrence game: an entrant chooses whether to enter a market, and an incumbent threatened with entry chooses whether to fight or accommodate. If fighting yields negative payoff for the incumbent relative to accommodation, the threat to fight is incredible; yet the Nash equilibrium concept allows it to deter entry because on the equilibrium path, entry never occurs and the threat never executes.

Subgame perfection refines Nash equilibrium by imposing sequential rationality: strategies must prescribe optimal continuation play not merely on the equilibrium path but at every decision node, even those reached only through deviations. Formally, a subgame perfect equilibrium requires the strategy profile to constitute a Nash equilibrium when restricted to every proper subgame. This eliminates incredible threats through a recursive optimality principle: if a strategy profile is subgame perfect, then its restriction to any subgame must itself be subgame perfect. The concept thus embeds a dynamic programming principle into equilibrium analysis, decomposing global optimality into a hierarchical chain of local optimality conditions.

Backward induction operationalizes subgame perfection in finite perfect-information games through a recursive algorithm that traverses the tree from terminal nodes toward the root. At each decision node, the algorithm selects the action maximizing the player's payoff under the assumption that all subsequent play follows the already-computed optimal continuations. Zermelo's theorem, first proved in 1913 for chess, establishes that every finite perfect-information game admits a subgame perfect equilibrium computable via backward induction, and moreover that this equilibrium yields a unique path of play and outcome. The proof constructs the equilibrium explicitly through the backward induction recursion, demonstrating that the algorithm terminates and produces a well-defined strategy profile satisfying sequential rationality at every node.

The one-deviation principle provides an efficient characterization of subgame perfection, reducing the infinite collection of potential deviations to a finite set of one-step deviations. Specifically, a strategy profile is subgame perfect if and only if no player can profit by deviating at a single decision node while conforming to their prescribed strategy at all subsequent nodes. This principle converts the verification of subgame perfection from an infinite-dimensional fixed-point problem into a finite collection of local optimality checks, enabling practical computation even in large games. The proof exploits the recursive structure of subgame perfection: if a multi-step deviation were profitable, the first deviation yielding a strict improvement would violate the one-deviation condition, establishing logical equivalence.

Kuhn's theorem establishes the fundamental bridge between extensive and strategic forms, proving that every finite extensive-form game admits a strategic-form reduction where players' pure strategies correspond to complete contingent plans in the extensive form. The theorem guarantees that Nash equilibria of the strategic form map to Nash equilibria of the extensive form, though the converse fails when the extensive form contains non-singleton information sets. This asymmetry reflects the richer informational structure of the extensive form: two extensive-form strategy profiles may be behaviorally equivalent on the equilibrium path yet differ off-path, inducing the same strategic-form strategy but distinct extensive-form beliefs and continuation play. Consequently, the strategic-form reduction destroys information critical for refining Nash equilibrium through subgame perfection or sequential rationality criteria.

The credibility of threats and promises emerges as the central economic insight delivered by subgame perfection. A threat is a commitment to punish deviations, while a promise is a commitment to reward compliance. Nash equilibrium permits incredible threats that would never be executed if tested, yet subgame perfection demands that every threat prescribed by equilibrium strategies must be sequentially rational to execute. This winnows the equilibrium set, often dramatically: in entry-deterrence games, only the accommodation equilibrium survives subgame perfection; in finitely repeated games, cooperation supported by incredible punishment threats unravels to the stage-game Nash equilibrium. The formal apparatus thus provides a mathematical foundation for intuitions about credible commitment, reputation, and time inconsistency that pervade economic and political analysis.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

An extensive-form game with perfect information consists of the following mathematical objects. The game tree $\Gamma = (V, E)$ is a finite directed tree with vertex set $V$ and edge set $E \subseteq V \times V$. The root node $v_0 \in V$ has in-degree zero and represents the initial position. Terminal nodes $Z \subseteq V$ have out-degree zero and carry payoff vectors $u: Z \to \mathbb{R}^N$ where $N$ denotes the number of players. Non-terminal nodes $V \setminus Z$ are partitioned among players via the assignment function $\rho: V \setminus Z \to \{1, \ldots, N\}$, where $\rho(v) = i$ means player $i$ chooses an action at node $v$. For each non-terminal node $v$, the set of available actions $A(v)$ corresponds bijectively to the outgoing edges $\{(v, w) \in E\}$. A history $h$ is a directed path from the root to some node $v$, and the length of the history equals the number of edges traversed.

The extensive form imposes a temporal structure through the tree's partial order. If node $w$ lies on the unique path from the root to node $v$, we write $w \preceq v$ and say $w$ precedes $v$. This partial order encodes the informational asymmetry inherent in sequential play: when choosing at node $v$, a player observes the actions taken along the history leading to $v$. Perfect information means each player knows the complete history when moving, formalized by assigning each non-terminal node its own singleton information set. This contrasts with imperfect information, where information sets may contain multiple nodes representing indistinguishable decision points.

A pure strategy for player $i$ is a function $s_i$ assigning an action to every node where player $i$ moves. Formally, $s_i: \{v \in V \setminus Z : \rho(v) = i\} \to \bigcup_{v \in V \setminus Z} A(v)$ with $s_i(v) \in A(v)$ for all $v$. The Cartesian product $S = S_1 \times \cdots \times S_N$ forms the strategy space, and each strategy profile $s \in S$ induces a unique path $\pi(s)$ from the root to a terminal node $z(s)$ by following the actions prescribed by $s$ at each node along the path. The outcome function $\xi: S \to Z$ maps strategy profiles to terminal nodes via $\xi(s) = z(s)$, and the payoff functions factor as $u_i(s) = u_i(\xi(s))$, delivering player $i$'s payoff from the terminal node reached under profile $s$.

The distinction between strategies and actions requires careful attention. An action is a local choice at a single node, an element of $A(v)$. A strategy is a global object, a complete contingent plan specifying behavior at every decision point. The strategy space grows exponentially with tree depth: if each node has branching factor $b$ and player $i$ controls $k$ nodes, then $|S_i| = b^k$. This combinatorial explosion renders explicit strategic-form analysis intractable for even moderately sized games, motivating solution algorithms that exploit the tree's recursive structure rather than enumerating the full strategy space.

Subgames provide the mathematical substrate for refining Nash equilibrium. A subgame rooted at node $v$ consists of the subtree $\Gamma_v$ containing $v$ and all its descendants, together with the restriction of the payoff function to terminal nodes within this subtree. In perfect-information games, every non-terminal node induces a well-defined subgame. In imperfect-information games, a subgame must begin at a singleton information set to ensure that players entering the subgame share common knowledge of the current node. The collection of proper subgames (all subgames except the game itself) forms a partially ordered set under the subgame inclusion relation, with maximal elements corresponding to immediate predecessors of terminal nodes.

A Nash equilibrium $(s_1^*, \ldots, s_N^*)$ of the full game restricts to a strategy profile in each subgame $\Gamma_v$ by conditioning on play reaching node $v$. However, this restriction need not constitute a Nash equilibrium of the subgame when viewed as a standalone game. A strategy profile is a subgame perfect equilibrium (SPE) if its restriction to every subgame forms a Nash equilibrium of that subgame. Formally, for every node $v$ and every player $i$, the restriction $s_i^*|_{\Gamma_v}$ must satisfy

$$u_i(s_i^*|_{\Gamma_v}, s_{-i}^*|_{\Gamma_v}) \geq u_i(s_i|_{\Gamma_v}, s_{-i}^*|_{\Gamma_v})$$

for all strategies $s_i$ of player $i$ in the original game. This condition embeds Nash equilibrium into a recursive hierarchy indexed by the subgame lattice.

Backward induction computes subgame perfect equilibria in finite perfect-information games through a recursive algorithm. Initialize the algorithm at terminal nodes by recording their payoff vectors. Proceed inductively: suppose we have computed optimal continuations and resulting payoffs for all proper subgames rooted at nodes strictly succeeding $v$. At node $v$ where player $i$ moves, compute the payoff player $i$ obtains from each action $a \in A(v)$ by following the optimal continuation in the subgame $\Gamma_w$ where $w$ is the immediate successor of $v$ along action $a$. Select an action $a^*(v) \in \arg\max_{a \in A(v)} u_i(a, \text{optimal continuation})$. Assign this action to player $i$'s strategy at node $v$ and propagate the resulting payoff vector upward to $v$. When the recursion reaches the root, the constructed strategy profile constitutes a subgame perfect equilibrium.

The backward induction algorithm is depicted procedurally as follows. Let $V^{(k)}$ denote the set of nodes at depth $k$ from the terminal layer, where depth zero corresponds to terminal nodes.

```
Algorithm: Backward Induction

Input: Extensive-form game tree Γ = (V, E), assignment ρ, payoffs u
Output: Subgame perfect equilibrium strategy profile s*

1. Initialize: Set k = 0 (terminal layer)
2. For each z ∈ Z (terminal nodes):
      Record payoff vector U(z) = u(z)
3. Increment k ← k + 1
4. For each node v ∈ V^(k):
      Let i = ρ(v) be the player moving at v
      For each action a ∈ A(v):
          Let w = successor of v along action a
          Compute continuation payoff V_i(v, a) = U(w)_i
      Select a*(v) ∈ argmax_{a ∈ A(v)} V_i(v, a)
      Set s*_i(v) = a*(v)
      Compute resulting payoff vector U(v) by following a*(v)
5. If v ≠ v_0, return to step 3
6. Output s*
```

The recursion terminates because the game tree is finite, and at each stage the algorithm performs a finite maximization over actions. The resulting strategy profile satisfies sequential rationality by construction: at each node, the prescribed action maximizes the player's payoff given optimal continuation play, ensuring that no profitable one-step deviation exists.

Zermelo's theorem formalizes the correctness and uniqueness properties of backward induction. The theorem comprises two parts. Existence asserts that every finite perfect-information game admits at least one subgame perfect equilibrium. Uniqueness asserts that the outcome path induced by backward induction is unique even when the equilibrium strategy profile is not (ties in the maximization step may admit multiple optimal actions, but these yield the same terminal node). The proof constructs the equilibrium through the backward induction algorithm, establishing existence directly. Uniqueness follows from the observation that if two actions at the same node yield distinct terminal nodes, the maximization step strictly ranks them, so ties can occur only when distinct actions lead to the same outcome.

The one-deviation principle characterizes subgame perfection through a finite collection of local optimality conditions, dramatically simplifying verification. A strategy profile $s^*$ satisfies the one-deviation property if no player can increase their payoff by deviating at a single node while conforming to $s^*$ at all subsequent nodes. Formally, for every player $i$ and every node $v$ where player $i$ moves, and for every action $a \in A(v)$, let $s_i^{v \to a}$ denote the strategy that matches $s_i^*$ everywhere except at node $v$ where it selects action $a$. Then $s^*$ satisfies the one-deviation property if

$$u_i(s^*) \geq u_i(s_i^{v \to a}, s_{-i}^*)$$

for all players $i$, all nodes $v$ with $\rho(v) = i$, and all actions $a \in A(v)$.

The equivalence between subgame perfection and the one-deviation property is non-trivial and proceeds as follows. First, suppose $s^*$ is subgame perfect. Then at every node $v$, the restriction $s^*|_{\Gamma_v}$ is a Nash equilibrium of $\Gamma_v$, so no deviation within $\Gamma_v$ is profitable. In particular, no one-step deviation is profitable, establishing the one-deviation property. Conversely, suppose $s^*$ satisfies the one-deviation property but is not subgame perfect. Then some player $i$ has a profitable deviation in some subgame $\Gamma_v$, meaning there exists a strategy $\tilde{s}_i$ for the subgame such that $u_i(\tilde{s}_i, s_{-i}^*|_{\Gamma_v}) > u_i(s^*|_{\Gamma_v})$. Let $w$ be the first node along the path induced by $(\tilde{s}_i, s_{-i}^*)$ where $\tilde{s}_i$ deviates from $s_i^*$. By construction, $\tilde{s}_i$ and $s_i^*$ agree at all nodes strictly preceding $w$ and at all successors of $w$. Thus $\tilde{s}_i$ differs from $s_i^*$ only at node $w$, constituting a one-step deviation. Since this deviation is profitable, $s^*$ violates the one-deviation property, yielding a contradiction. This proves logical equivalence: subgame perfection holds if and only if the one-deviation property holds.

Kuhn's theorem establishes that every finite extensive-form game induces a well-defined strategic-form game where the strategy sets are inherited from the extensive form and payoffs are computed by tracing the outcome path. Specifically, given extensive form $\Gamma$, the associated strategic form has strategy spaces $S_i$ as defined above and payoff functions $\tilde{u}_i: S \to \mathbb{R}$ given by $\tilde{u}_i(s) = u_i(\xi(s))$. The theorem asserts that if $s^*$ is a Nash equilibrium of the strategic form, then $s^*$ is also a Nash equilibrium of the extensive form (interpreting extensive-form equilibria as strategy profiles inducing no profitable deviations given the strategies of others). The proof is immediate: if $s^*$ is a Nash equilibrium in strategic form, then $\tilde{u}_i(s^*) \geq \tilde{u}_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*)$ for all $s_i \in S_i$, which by definition of $\tilde{u}_i$ means $u_i(s^*) \geq u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*)$ for all $s_i$, confirming that $s^*$ is a Nash equilibrium of the extensive form.

The converse, however, fails for extensive forms with imperfect information. Two extensive-form strategy profiles may induce the same strategic-form strategy if they differ only at information sets unreached under the equilibrium path, yet these profiles may assign different beliefs and continuation strategies off-path. Consequently, the strategic-form reduction collapses distinctions essential for subgame perfection and sequential rationality. Kuhn's theorem thus serves as both a bridge and a warning: it guarantees that Nash equilibria transport from strategic to extensive form, but it does not preserve the refinements that make extensive-form analysis powerful.

Credible threats are formalized as strategies prescribing punishments or rewards that remain optimal if executed. In the entry-deterrence game, the incumbent's threat to fight entry is incredible if fighting yields lower payoff than accommodating, since upon reaching the fight-or-accommodate node, the incumbent prefers accommodation. Subgame perfection rules out the fighting threat, forcing the equilibrium to feature entry and accommodation. More generally, any Nash equilibrium sustained by off-path threats that are irrational to execute fails subgame perfection. The mathematical criterion thus operationalizes the intuitive notion of credibility, filtering the equilibrium set to strategies that could be implemented by rational forward-looking players with commitment power only over their own future actions, not over opponents' beliefs about those actions.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Computing subgame perfect equilibria via backward induction achieves polynomial time complexity in perfect-information games when each player's payoff can be evaluated in constant time. Specifically, if the game tree has $|V|$ nodes and branching factor bounded by $b$, backward induction requires $O(|V| \cdot b)$ operations: one maximization per non-terminal node, each over at most $b$ actions. This compares favorably to the naive approach of enumerating the strategic-form strategy space, which has size exponential in the tree depth. The recursive structure of subgame perfection thus yields computational tractability through dynamic programming, avoiding the combinatorial explosion inherent in strategic-form analysis.

Optimal implementation exploits the tree's topological structure to minimize memory overhead and redundant computation. A post-order traversal visits each node exactly once, computing optimal continuations for children before processing parents. Storing only the optimal action and resulting payoff vector at each node requires $O(|V| \cdot N)$ space where $N$ is the number of players, avoiding explicit representation of the full strategy space. When the game tree is large but shallow, iterative deepening or best-first search can prioritize exploration of high-payoff branches, pruning suboptimal subtrees early through alpha-beta pruning techniques adapted from game-playing algorithms.

Tie-breaking in the backward induction maximization step introduces potential equilibrium multiplicity. When multiple actions at a node yield identical continuation payoffs, any selection constitutes an optimal action, generating distinct subgame perfect equilibria. These equilibria are outcome-equivalent along the equilibrium path by Zermelo's uniqueness result, but they differ in off-path behavior and hence in the credibility of threatened punishments. Applications requiring a unique equilibrium prescription must impose a tie-breaking rule, such as lexicographic action ordering or random selection. The mathematical theory remains agnostic, treating all maximal elements as equally valid.

Imperfect information shatters the computational tractability of backward induction. When information sets contain multiple nodes, the game may lack proper subgames except at the root, rendering the recursive decomposition trivial. Subgame perfection then collapses to Nash equilibrium, failing to refine the equilibrium set. Sequential equilibrium and perfect Bayesian equilibrium provide stronger refinements by imposing consistency conditions on beliefs at off-path information sets, but computing these equilibria is PSPACE-complete in general, reflecting the need to reason about counterfactual beliefs and higher-order strategic reasoning. The perfect-information case thus represents a fortuitous special case where the informational structure aligns with the tree's recursive decomposition, enabling efficient computation.

Infinite-horizon games pose both conceptual and computational challenges. When the game tree is infinite, backward induction cannot initiate from terminal nodes, and the recursion fails to terminate. Subgame perfection remains well-defined through the fixed-point characterization, but explicit computation requires alternative techniques. Discounted infinite-horizon games with stationary structure admit value function iteration or policy iteration algorithms analogous to Markov decision process solvers, exploiting the contraction mapping induced by discounting. Undiscounted games may require topological arguments involving limits of finite-horizon approximations, and uniqueness guarantees generally fail.

Multiplayer games with more than two players introduce additional complexity through coalition formation and coordination. Even in perfect-information settings, subgame perfection does not ensure Pareto efficiency or fairness, and multiple equilibria may persist even when the outcome path is unique. Communication among players can facilitate coordination on Pareto-superior equilibria, but in the absence of binding agreements, cheap talk must be self-enforcing. The mathematics of subgame perfection extends seamlessly to multiplayer games, but the strategic richness and equilibrium multiplicity escalate with the number of players.

Behavioral deviations from backward induction have been documented extensively in experimental settings, particularly in centipede games where players sequentially choose between taking a growing pot and passing to the opponent. Backward induction predicts immediate take at the first node, yet experimental subjects frequently pass multiple times, suggesting bounded rationality, limited depth of reasoning, or social preferences. These deviations motivate level-$k$ reasoning models and cognitive hierarchy theories that truncate the backward induction recursion at finite depth, but such models abandon the fixed-point formulation of equilibrium in favor of descriptive heuristics. The normative force of subgame perfection as a rationality criterion remains robust, even as its descriptive accuracy falters in some environments.

Verifying subgame perfection in practice reduces to checking the one-deviation property at each decision node, a task parallelizable across nodes when the game tree is explicitly represented. Automated theorem provers and constraint solvers can encode the one-deviation inequalities as a system of linear constraints when payoffs are linear or polyhedral, enabling formal verification of equilibrium properties. For games specified algorithmically rather than explicitly (e.g., through code generating the tree dynamically), symbolic execution or abstract interpretation techniques can verify sequential rationality over equivalence classes of histories, though soundness and completeness guarantees depend on the expressiveness of the specification language.

The failure mode of most practical concern is the misapplication of backward induction to games with imperfect information or simultaneous moves. A common error treats simultaneous-move subgames as if players move sequentially, incorrectly computing a Stackelberg equilibrium (where the leader commits to a strategy and the follower best-responds) instead of the Nash equilibrium of the simultaneous subgame. The formal remedy is to represent simultaneous moves through non-singleton information sets, forcing the analyst to recognize that the players cannot condition on each other's actions. This reinforces the principle that the extensive form is not merely a notational variant of the strategic form but a richer structure encoding the informational asymmetries that govern rational play.
