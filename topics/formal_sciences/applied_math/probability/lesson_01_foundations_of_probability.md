# Lesson 1: Foundations of Probability — Sample Spaces, Events, Axioms

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Probability theory, as it is practiced today, is not a theory about belief, frequency, or propensity in any philosophically committed sense. It is a branch of measure theory, and its objects — sample spaces, events, probability measures — are mathematical structures governed by precise axioms. This is Kolmogorov's decisive contribution, published in his 1933 monograph *Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung*: to strip probability of its interpretive baggage and ground it in the same foundational machinery that underpins Lebesgue integration and functional analysis. For mathematicians, logicians, and theoretical computer scientists, this is exactly the right starting point. The interpretive questions — frequentist, Bayesian, propensity — are downstream philosophical commitments that do not alter the structure of the theory one axiom.

The fundamental object is the **probability space**, a triple $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$. Each component carries a precise role, and understanding why each component is necessary — not merely what it is — is the first invariant of the discipline.

### The Sample Space $\Omega$

The sample space $\Omega$ is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment. The word "experiment" is used loosely; it means any process whose result is not predetermined from the perspective of the model. When you roll a standard die, $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$. When you observe the lifetime of a radioactive atom, $\Omega = [0, \infty)$. When you model a random walk on the integers over infinite time, $\Omega = \mathbb{Z}^\mathbb{N}$, the space of all infinite integer-valued sequences. The sample space can be finite, countably infinite, or uncountably infinite, and the richness of the theory is proportional to the complexity of $\Omega$ one is willing to handle.

A critical point for theoreticians: the sample space is not unique for a given physical situation. It is a modeling choice. If you care only about whether a die shows an even or odd number, you may take $\Omega = \{\text{even}, \text{odd}\}$. The choice of $\Omega$ determines what questions are even askable within the model.

### The $\sigma$-Algebra $\mathcal{F}$

Once $\Omega$ is fixed, the next question is: to which subsets of $\Omega$ should we assign probability? The naive answer — all subsets — collapses immediately when $\Omega$ is uncountable. The Vitali construction (1905) demonstrates, assuming the axiom of choice, that there exist subsets of $[0,1]$ to which no translation-invariant measure can be consistently assigned. Attempting to measure every subset of an uncountable space leads to contradiction. We must therefore specify a collection $\mathcal{F} \subseteq 2^\Omega$ of *measurable* subsets — the events — and this collection must have enough closure properties to support countable operations.

**Definition (σ-Algebra).** A collection $\mathcal{F}$ of subsets of $\Omega$ is a $\sigma$-algebra (also called a $\sigma$-field) if it satisfies three conditions:

$$\Omega \in \mathcal{F}$$

$$A \in \mathcal{F} \implies A^c \in \mathcal{F}$$

$$A_1, A_2, A_3, \ldots \in \mathcal{F} \implies \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n \in \mathcal{F}$$

These three axioms are not arbitrary. Closure under complementation encodes the logical principle that if the event $A$ is observable, so is "not $A$." Closure under countable unions encodes the ability to form events by countable disjunction — the kind of reasoning that underpins infinite-horizon processes, limit theorems, and convergence arguments. The requirement that $\Omega \in \mathcal{F}$ ensures that the certain event is always measurable.

From these three axioms, closure under countable intersections follows immediately by De Morgan's law:

$$\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n = \left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n^c\right)^c$$

Since each $A_n^c \in \mathcal{F}$ by closure under complementation, and $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n^c \in \mathcal{F}$ by closure under countable unions, applying complementation again yields $\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n \in \mathcal{F}$. The $\sigma$-algebra is therefore closed under all countable Boolean operations, making it precisely the right algebraic structure for logic over infinite collections of events.

The smallest $\sigma$-algebra on $\Omega$ is $\{\emptyset, \Omega\}$, the trivial $\sigma$-algebra, which admits no non-trivial events. The largest is $2^\Omega$, the power set, which is usable when $\Omega$ is finite or countable. For $\Omega = \mathbb{R}$, the standard choice is the **Borel $\sigma$-algebra** $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$, generated by the open sets of the standard topology — equivalently, generated by all intervals of the form $(-\infty, x]$ for $x \in \mathbb{R}$. The Borel $\sigma$-algebra is the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing all open sets, and it is the natural measurable structure for any probability model over real-valued random variables.

**Definition ($\sigma$-Algebra Generated by a Collection).** For any collection $\mathcal{C} \subseteq 2^\Omega$, the $\sigma$-algebra *generated* by $\mathcal{C}$, written $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$, is the intersection of all $\sigma$-algebras containing $\mathcal{C}$:

$$\sigma(\mathcal{C}) = \bigcap \{\mathcal{G} : \mathcal{G} \text{ is a } \sigma\text{-algebra and } \mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{G}\}$$

This intersection is again a $\sigma$-algebra (a standard argument: each axiom is preserved by arbitrary intersections of $\sigma$-algebras), and it is the coarsest $\sigma$-algebra that makes every set in $\mathcal{C}$ measurable. This construction is ubiquitous: when defining the natural filtration of a stochastic process $\{X_t\}$, one forms $\mathcal{F}_t = \sigma(X_s : s \le t)$, the $\sigma$-algebra generated by all observations up to time $t$.

### The Kolmogorov Axioms and the Probability Measure $P$

With $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$ established as a *measurable space*, Kolmogorov defines a probability measure as a function $P : \mathcal{F} \to [0,1]$ satisfying:

**Axiom K1 (Non-negativity).**
$$P(A) \ge 0 \quad \text{for all } A \in \mathcal{F}$$

**Axiom K2 (Normalization).**
$$P(\Omega) = 1$$

**Axiom K3 (Countable Additivity / $\sigma$-Additivity).** For any countable collection of pairwise disjoint events $A_1, A_2, A_3, \ldots \in \mathcal{F}$ with $A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset$ for $i \ne j$:

$$P\!\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty P(A_n)$$

The first two axioms are unsurprising. The third is where the real mathematical content lives. Finite additivity — requiring only that $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$ for disjoint $A, B$ — is strictly weaker than countable additivity and leads to a deficient theory. Countable additivity is what makes it possible to pass probability through limits, to apply dominated convergence, to prove the law of large numbers, and to build the entire edifice of modern stochastic analysis. Bruno de Finetti's finitely additive subjective probability exists as a coherent alternative, but it forfeits the analytic power that makes the Kolmogorov framework so productive.

The triple $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ is called a **probability space**. Every theorem in classical probability theory is a theorem about such triples.

### Fundamental Consequences of the Axioms

Every elementary property of probability is a theorem, derived from K1–K3, not an additional axiom. This derivation practice is the correct way to build fluency with the theory.

**Theorem (Probability of the Empty Set).** $P(\emptyset) = 0$.

*Proof.* Write $\Omega = \Omega \cup \emptyset \cup \emptyset \cup \cdots$, a disjoint union of countably many sets. By K3:

$$P(\Omega) = P(\Omega) + P(\emptyset) + P(\emptyset) + \cdots$$

Since $P(\Omega) = 1$ by K2, the left side is $1$. Subtracting $P(\Omega)$ from both sides:

$$0 = P(\emptyset) + P(\emptyset) + \cdots = \sum_{n=1}^\infty P(\emptyset)$$

A non-negative series summing to zero requires every term to be zero, so $P(\emptyset) = 0$. $\square$

**Theorem (Finite Additivity).** For pairwise disjoint $A_1, \ldots, A_n \in \mathcal{F}$:

$$P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^n P(A_i)$$

*Proof.* Set $A_k = \emptyset$ for all $k > n$ and apply K3 together with $P(\emptyset) = 0$. $\square$

**Theorem (Complement Rule).** For any $A \in \mathcal{F}$:

$$P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$$

*Proof.* Since $A$ and $A^c$ are disjoint and $A \cup A^c = \Omega$, finite additivity gives $P(A) + P(A^c) = P(\Omega) = 1$. Rearranging yields the result. $\square$

**Theorem (Monotonicity).** If $A \subseteq B$ then $P(A) \le P(B)$.

*Proof.* Write $B = A \cup (B \setminus A)$, a disjoint union. By finite additivity, $P(B) = P(A) + P(B \setminus A)$. Since $P(B \setminus A) \ge 0$ by K1, we have $P(B) \ge P(A)$. $\square$

**Theorem (Inclusion-Exclusion for Two Events).** For any $A, B \in \mathcal{F}$:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

*Proof.* Decompose $A \cup B$ into three disjoint parts: $A \setminus B$, $A \cap B$, and $B \setminus A$. By finite additivity:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A \setminus B) + P(A \cap B) + P(B \setminus A)$$

Now $A = (A \setminus B) \cup (A \cap B)$ disjointly, so $P(A \setminus B) = P(A) - P(A \cap B)$. Similarly $P(B \setminus A) = P(B) - P(A \cap B)$. Substituting:

$$P(A \cup B) = [P(A) - P(A \cap B)] + P(A \cap B) + [P(B) - P(A \cap B)] = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \qquad \square$$

The general inclusion-exclusion principle extends this to $n$ events by induction:

$$P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\right) = \sum_{k=1}^n (-1)^{k-1} \sum_{1 \le i_1 < \cdots < i_k \le n} P(A_{i_1} \cap \cdots \cap A_{i_k})$$

This identity is indispensable in combinatorial probability, where it is used to count configurations via the sieve method.

**Theorem (Continuity of Probability Measure).** If $A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq A_3 \subseteq \cdots$ is an increasing sequence of events with $A_n \nearrow A = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n$, then:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} P(A_n) = P(A)$$

*Proof.* Define $B_1 = A_1$ and $B_n = A_n \setminus A_{n-1}$ for $n \ge 2$. The sets $B_n$ are pairwise disjoint and $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty B_n = A$. By $\sigma$-additivity:

$$P(A) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty P(B_n) = \lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{n=1}^N P(B_n) = \lim_{N \to \infty} P\!\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^N B_n\right) = \lim_{N \to \infty} P(A_N)$$

where the last equality uses $\bigcup_{n=1}^N B_n = A_N$. $\square$

This continuity theorem is the reason $\sigma$-additivity, rather than mere finite additivity, is essential. The result converts an infinite computation (the probability of a limiting event) into a limit of finite computations — the operational foundation for every convergence theorem in probability.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

### Conditional Probability: The Ratio Definition and Its Structural Implications

With the probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ in hand, the most consequential derived concept is conditional probability. The motivation is straightforward: given that we know event $B$ has occurred, what is the probability of event $A$? Knowing $B$ restricts the effective sample space to $B$, and the probability of $A$ within that restricted space should be proportional to $P(A \cap B)$, normalized so that the conditional probabilities over the restricted space still sum to one.

**Definition (Conditional Probability).** For events $A, B \in \mathcal{F}$ with $P(B) > 0$, the conditional probability of $A$ given $B$ is:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$

This is a *definition*, not a theorem. It is a modeling choice encoding the logical structure of updated belief under partial information. Its justification comes from verifying that it satisfies the Kolmogorov axioms when $B$ is fixed: $P(\cdot \mid B)$ is itself a probability measure on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$, assigning probability zero to any event disjoint from $B$ and probability one to $B$ itself.

**Verification that $P(\cdot \mid B)$ is a probability measure.** Fix $B \in \mathcal{F}$ with $P(B) > 0$.

For K1: $P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B) \ge 0$ since both numerator and denominator are non-negative.

For K2: $P(\Omega \mid B) = P(\Omega \cap B)/P(B) = P(B)/P(B) = 1$.

For K3: Let $A_1, A_2, \ldots$ be pairwise disjoint events. Then $A_1 \cap B, A_2 \cap B, \ldots$ are also pairwise disjoint (since $(A_i \cap B) \cap (A_j \cap B) = (A_i \cap A_j) \cap B = \emptyset \cap B = \emptyset$ for $i \ne j$). Therefore:

$$P\!\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n \,\middle|\, B\right) = \frac{P\!\left(\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right) \cap B\right)}{P(B)} = \frac{P\!\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty (A_n \cap B)\right)}{P(B)} = \frac{\sum_{n=1}^\infty P(A_n \cap B)}{P(B)} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty P(A_n \mid B)$$

The measure-theoretic structure is preserved under conditioning. $\square$

### The Multiplication Rule and the Chain Rule

Rearranging the definition immediately gives the **multiplication rule**:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B)$$

Iterating this across a chain of events $A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_n \in \mathcal{F}$ yields the **chain rule of probability**, one of the most heavily used identities in probabilistic graphical models and information theory:

$$P\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^n A_i\right) = P(A_1) \cdot P(A_2 \mid A_1) \cdot P(A_3 \mid A_1 \cap A_2) \cdots P\!\left(A_n \,\middle|\, \bigcap_{i=1}^{n-1} A_i\right)$$

**Proof by induction.** The base case $n = 2$ is the multiplication rule. Assume the identity holds for $n - 1$ events. Then:

$$P\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^n A_i\right) = P\!\left(A_n \,\middle|\, \bigcap_{i=1}^{n-1} A_i\right) \cdot P\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n-1} A_i\right)$$

Applying the inductive hypothesis to $P\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n-1} A_i\right)$ completes the induction. $\square$

### The Law of Total Probability

A *partition* of $\Omega$ is a countable collection $\{B_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset \mathcal{F}$ of pairwise disjoint events whose union is $\Omega$, with $P(B_n) > 0$ for each $n$. The partition decomposes every event $A$ as a disjoint union:

$$A = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty (A \cap B_n)$$

Applying $\sigma$-additivity and the multiplication rule to each term:

**Theorem (Law of Total Probability).**

$$P(A) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty P(A \mid B_n) \cdot P(B_n)$$

*Proof.* Since the events $A \cap B_n$ are pairwise disjoint and their union is $A$:

$$P(A) = P\!\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty (A \cap B_n)\right) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty P(A \cap B_n) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty P(A \mid B_n) \cdot P(B_n) \qquad \square$$

This theorem is the computational workhorse for marginalizing out latent variables. In a Bayesian network, summing over all instantiations of a hidden variable is an application of the law of total probability.

### Bayes' Theorem as a Logical Consequence

Bayes' theorem is not an independent principle. It is an algebraic consequence of the ratio definition of conditional probability and the law of total probability. Its derivation takes two lines.

**Theorem (Bayes' Theorem).** Let $\{B_n\}$ be a partition of $\Omega$ with $P(B_n) > 0$ for each $n$, and let $A \in \mathcal{F}$ with $P(A) > 0$. Then:

$$P(B_k \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_k) \cdot P(B_k)}{\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty P(A \mid B_n) \cdot P(B_n)}$$

*Proof.* Apply the definition of conditional probability to $P(B_k \mid A)$:

$$P(B_k \mid A) = \frac{P(B_k \cap A)}{P(A)}$$

The numerator $P(B_k \cap A) = P(A \mid B_k) \cdot P(B_k)$ by the multiplication rule. The denominator $P(A) = \sum_n P(A \mid B_n) \cdot P(B_n)$ by the law of total probability. Substituting both expressions gives the result. $\square$

The names attached to each quantity are worth fixing precisely: $P(B_k)$ is the **prior** probability of hypothesis $B_k$, reflecting what is known before observing $A$. $P(A \mid B_k)$ is the **likelihood** of observation $A$ under hypothesis $B_k$. $P(B_k \mid A)$ is the **posterior** probability of $B_k$ after observing $A$. The denominator $P(A)$ is the **marginal likelihood** or **evidence**, serving as a normalizing constant that ensures the posteriors sum to one.

### Independence: The Structural Definition

The concept of independence is defined measure-theoretically, not intuitively. The intuition — that two events do not "influence" each other — is not a mathematically precise statement. The definition is.

**Definition (Independence of Two Events).** Events $A, B \in \mathcal{F}$ are **independent** (written $A \perp B$) if:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$$

Notice that this definition is symmetric in $A$ and $B$, and it makes no reference to conditional probability. When $P(B) > 0$, independence is equivalent to $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$: conditioning on $B$ provides no information about $A$. But the definition via the product rule holds even when $P(B) = 0$, making it strictly more general.

A common source of error is confusing independence with mutual exclusivity. Mutually exclusive events $A, B$ with $P(A) > 0$ and $P(B) > 0$ satisfy $P(A \cap B) = P(\emptyset) = 0 \ne P(A) \cdot P(B) > 0$, so they are *not* independent. Knowing that one occurred tells you with certainty the other did not — the strongest possible dependence.

**Definition (Mutual Independence of a Collection).** A collection of events $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ is mutually independent if for every finite subcollection $\{A_{i_1}, \ldots, A_{i_k}\} \subseteq \{A_i\}_{i \in I}$:

$$P\!\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^k A_{i_j}\right) = \prod_{j=1}^k P(A_{i_j})$$

Pairwise independence — the condition that $P(A_i \cap A_j) = P(A_i) P(A_j)$ for all $i \ne j$ — does not imply mutual independence. The classic counterexample is due to Bernstein. Take $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ with uniform probability, and define $A = \{1, 2\}$, $B = \{1, 3\}$, $C = \{1, 4\}$. Each has probability $1/2$. Then $P(A \cap B) = P(\{1\}) = 1/4 = P(A)P(B)$, and similarly for the other pairs, so the events are pairwise independent. But $P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(\{1\}) = 1/4 \ne 1/8 = P(A)P(B)P(C)$. Mutual independence fails.

The distinction matters deeply in cryptography (where statistical independence of key bits is a security requirement on all joint subsets) and in the analysis of randomized algorithms (where the efficiency of hash functions depends on $k$-wise independence for specific values of $k$).

**Independence of $\sigma$-Algebras.** The most general form of independence in probability theory is between $\sigma$-algebras. Sub-$\sigma$-algebras $\mathcal{G}_1, \mathcal{G}_2 \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ are independent if $P(G_1 \cap G_2) = P(G_1) P(G_2)$ for all $G_1 \in \mathcal{G}_1$ and $G_2 \in \mathcal{G}_2$. Random variables $X$ and $Y$ are independent when the $\sigma$-algebras they generate, $\sigma(X) = \{X^{-1}(B) : B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\}$ and $\sigma(Y)$, are independent in this sense. This is the definition that connects cleanly to the factorization of joint densities and the product measure construction.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

### Working with Probability Spaces: Structural Discipline

For a theoretical computer scientist or logician, the probability space formalism is not merely scaffolding. It is an executable specification. Every claim about random variables, expectations, convergence, and conditional distributions must be traceable back to a valid $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$. The failures that arise in sloppy probabilistic reasoning can almost always be diagnosed by identifying which component of this triple is under-specified or misused.

The first failure mode is **under-specified $\sigma$-algebras**. This manifests when analysts write expressions like $P(X \in B)$ for a random variable $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ without verifying that $X^{-1}(B) = \{\omega \in \Omega : X(\omega) \in B\} \in \mathcal{F}$. If $X$ is not measurable with respect to $\mathcal{F}$, the expression $P(X \in B)$ is syntactically valid but semantically undefined — a type error at the measure-theoretic level. In probabilistic programming systems like Stan or PyMC, this corresponds to defining a stochastic variable over a space that the model's internal graph cannot represent, leading to silent logical errors rather than runtime exceptions.

The second failure mode is **conflating conditional probability with the conditional expectation operator**. The expression $P(A \mid B)$ is a number when $B$ is a fixed event. The expression $P(A \mid \mathcal{G})$, where $\mathcal{G}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra, is a *random variable* — specifically, the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the measure $A \mapsto P(A \cap G)$ with respect to $P$ restricted to $\mathcal{G}$. Mistaking one for the other causes errors in Bayesian filtering (the Kalman filter, the hidden Markov model forward algorithm) where the filtered distribution is a random variable indexed by the observation sequence, not a fixed number.

### Applying Bayes' Theorem: The Base Rate Error

Bayes' theorem is a theorem about ratios of measures, and its misapplication is a consistent failure mode in probabilistic reasoning. The structure of the error is always the same: the analyst focuses on the likelihood $P(A \mid B_k)$ and neglects the prior $P(B_k)$, producing a posterior that dramatically overstates the probability of a rare hypothesis.

Consider a population with disease prevalence $P(D) = 0.001$, so $P(D^c) = 0.999$. A diagnostic test has sensitivity $P(T^+ \mid D) = 0.99$ and specificity $P(T^- \mid D^c) = 0.95$, implying a false positive rate $P(T^+ \mid D^c) = 0.05$. A patient tests positive. What is $P(D \mid T^+)$?

Applying Bayes' theorem with the partition $\{D, D^c\}$:

$$P(D \mid T^+) = \frac{P(T^+ \mid D) \cdot P(D)}{P(T^+ \mid D) \cdot P(D) + P(T^+ \mid D^c) \cdot P(D^c)}$$

$$= \frac{0.99 \times 0.001}{0.99 \times 0.001 + 0.05 \times 0.999} = \frac{0.00099}{0.00099 + 0.04995} = \frac{0.00099}{0.05094} \approx 0.0194$$

A positive test result, despite the test's high sensitivity and reasonable specificity, yields only a 1.94% posterior probability of disease. The low prior crushes the posterior. This is not a paradox; it is the axioms working correctly. The failure mode is treating the likelihood alone as the diagnostic conclusion — a logical error structurally equivalent to affirming the consequent ($T^+ \to D$ does not follow from $D \to T^+$).

### The Continuity Theorem and Borel-Cantelli: Limit Events and Long-Run Behavior

The continuity of probability measures, proved in Section 1, connects to two lemmas that govern the probability of events happening infinitely often — the natural setting for analyzing algorithms with unbounded running time, random walks, and long-horizon games.

**Definition (Limsup Event).** For a sequence of events $\{A_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$, the event that *infinitely many* $A_n$ occur is:

$$\{A_n \text{ i.o.}\} = \limsup_{n \to \infty} A_n = \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty \bigcup_{k=n}^\infty A_k$$

This is a valid event in $\mathcal{F}$ because $\mathcal{F}$ is closed under countable unions and intersections. The Borel-Cantelli lemmas determine its probability.

**Theorem (First Borel-Cantelli Lemma).** If $\sum_{n=1}^\infty P(A_n) < \infty$, then $P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = 0$.

*Proof.* Let $B_n = \bigcup_{k=n}^\infty A_k$. Then $B_1 \supseteq B_2 \supseteq B_3 \supseteq \cdots$ is a decreasing sequence, and $\{A_n \text{ i.o.}\} = \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty B_n$. By continuity of probability (the decreasing-sequence version, obtained by complementing the increasing-sequence result):

$$P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(B_n) \le \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=n}^\infty P(A_k)$$

Since $\sum_{n=1}^\infty P(A_n) < \infty$, the tail of a convergent series goes to zero: $\sum_{k=n}^\infty P(A_k) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$. Therefore $P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = 0$. $\square$

**Theorem (Second Borel-Cantelli Lemma).** If the events $A_n$ are mutually independent and $\sum_{n=1}^\infty P(A_n) = \infty$, then $P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = 1$.

*Proof.* It suffices to show $P(A_n \text{ i.o.})^c = P(\text{only finitely many } A_n \text{ occur}) = 0$, which is equivalent to showing $P\!\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \bigcap_{k=n}^\infty A_k^c\right) = 1$... equivalently, $P\!\left(\bigcap_{k=n}^\infty A_k^c\right) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$. By mutual independence, for any $m \ge n$:

$$P\!\left(\bigcap_{k=n}^m A_k^c\right) = \prod_{k=n}^m P(A_k^c) = \prod_{k=n}^m (1 - P(A_k))$$

Using $1 - x \le e^{-x}$ for $x \in [0,1]$:

$$\prod_{k=n}^m (1 - P(A_k)) \le \prod_{k=n}^m e^{-P(A_k)} = \exp\!\left(-\sum_{k=n}^m P(A_k)\right)$$

Since $\sum_{k=1}^\infty P(A_k) = \infty$, the partial sums $\sum_{k=n}^m P(A_k) \to \infty$ as $m \to \infty$. Therefore the product tends to zero, and by continuity of probability, $P\!\left(\bigcap_{k=n}^\infty A_k^c\right) = 0$. This holds for every $n$, so $P(\text{only finitely many } A_n) = 0$, giving $P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = 1$. $\square$

Together, the Borel-Cantelli lemmas form the fundamental tool for analyzing tail events in sequences of random trials. In the analysis of randomized algorithms, they determine whether a probabilistic guarantee holds eventually almost surely or might fail infinitely often. In the theory of communication over noisy channels, they characterize whether errors accumulate unboundedly or die out. The key invariant to internalize is that the boundary between the two regimes is determined entirely by the summability of $\sum_n P(A_n)$ — a clean, computable criterion derived from nothing more than the Kolmogorov axioms and the closure properties of $\sigma$-algebras.

### The Kolmogorov Zero-One Law: Tail Events Have Trivial Probabilities

A profound structural consequence of independence is that any event determined entirely by the asymptotic behavior of an independent sequence has probability zero or one — no other value is possible.

**Definition (Tail $\sigma$-Algebra).** For an independent sequence of random variables $\{X_n\}$, the tail $\sigma$-algebra is:

$$\mathcal{T} = \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty \sigma(X_n, X_{n+1}, X_{n+2}, \ldots)$$

Events in $\mathcal{T}$ are those whose membership does not depend on any finite initial segment of the sequence. The convergence of $\sum X_n$, the event $\{\limsup_n X_n > c\}$, the event that the sequence is eventually positive — all these are tail events.

**Theorem (Kolmogorov's Zero-One Law).** If $\{X_n\}$ is a sequence of mutually independent random variables and $A \in \mathcal{T}$, then $P(A) \in \{0, 1\}$.

The proof proceeds by showing that $A$ is independent of itself: $P(A \cap A) = P(A) \cdot P(A)$, which gives $P(A) = P(A)^2$, forcing $P(A) \in \{0, 1\}$. The self-independence of $A$ is established by showing, through careful approximation arguments using finite sub-$\sigma$-algebras, that $\mathcal{T}$ is independent of $\sigma(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$ for every $n$, and hence independent of $\sigma(X_1, X_2, \ldots) = \bigcup_n \sigma(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$ (after closure), which contains $\mathcal{T}$ as a subset, yielding the self-independence.

The practical import for a theoretical computer scientist is immediate: for any randomized algorithm operating on independent random inputs, any tail property of the execution — does the algorithm halt, does the error term converge, does the output stabilize — has probability either zero or one, with no intermediate value possible. The interesting computational question is therefore not "with what probability" but "which of zero or one": a question that often reduces to determining whether a certain series of probabilities diverges.
