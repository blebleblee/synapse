# Lesson 1: Foundations of Probability Distributions

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Probability theory emerges as the formal mathematical language for reasoning under uncertainty, providing the axiomatic foundation upon which all statistical inference, stochastic modeling, and computational learning algorithms rest. At its core, probability theory transforms qualitative intuitions about randomness into quantitative machinery amenable to rigorous proof and computational implementation. The object of study is not the physical random phenomenon itself, but rather the mathematical structure that encodes the relative likelihood of outcomes and permits us to compute expectations, variances, and higher-order moments that characterize the behavior of complex stochastic systems.

The fundamental object in probability theory is the probability space, a triple $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ where $\Omega$ represents the sample space of all possible outcomes, $\mathcal{F}$ denotes a $\sigma$-algebra of measurable events, and $\mathbb{P}: \mathcal{F} \to [0,1]$ assigns probabilities to events in a manner consistent with the Kolmogorov axioms. The requirement that $\mathcal{F}$ form a $\sigma$-algebra ensures closure under countable unions and complements, permitting us to discuss the probability of limit events and infinite sequences. This measure-theoretic foundation, established by Andrey Kolmogorov in 1933, resolved longstanding philosophical debates about the nature of probability by grounding it firmly in the rigorous framework of Lebesgue integration theory.

A random variable $X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ is not a variable in the algebraic sense, but rather a measurable function mapping outcomes to real numbers. The induced probability measure on $\mathbb{R}$ is called the distribution of $X$, and it encapsulates all probabilistic information about the random variable. This distribution is characterized equivalently by the cumulative distribution function $F_X(x) = \mathbb{P}(X \le x)$, the probability mass function $p_X(x)$ for discrete random variables, or the probability density function $f_X(x)$ for absolutely continuous random variables satisfying $F_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f_X(t) \, dt$. The transition from outcomes in $\Omega$ to distributions on $\mathbb{R}$ permits us to work directly with the pushforward measure, abstracting away the underlying probability space and focusing on the induced structure on the target space.

The Kolmogorov axioms impose three fundamental constraints on any probability measure. First, non-negativity requires $\mathbb{P}(A) \ge 0$ for all events $A \in \mathcal{F}$. Second, normalization demands $\mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1$, ensuring that total probability mass equals unity. Third, countable additivity asserts that for any countable collection of pairwise disjoint events $\{A_i\}_{i=1}^\infty$, we have $\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty \mathbb{P}(A_i)$. This third axiom distinguishes probability theory from finitely additive set functions and ensures compatibility with limit operations essential for convergence theorems and stochastic process theory.

From these axioms, we derive fundamental consequences that govern all probabilistic reasoning. The complement rule $\mathbb{P}(A^c) = 1 - \mathbb{P}(A)$ follows immediately from normalization and additivity applied to the disjoint union $\Omega = A \cup A^c$. Monotonicity holds: if $A \subseteq B$, then $\mathbb{P}(A) \le \mathbb{P}(B)$, proven by writing $B = A \cup (B \setminus A)$ and invoking additivity with non-negativity. The inclusion-exclusion principle for finite collections generalizes the union bound, yielding for two events the familiar formula $\mathbb{P}(A \cup B) = \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B) - \mathbb{P}(A \cap B)$. For countable unions, Boole's inequality provides the upper bound $\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i\right) \le \sum_{i=1}^\infty \mathbb{P}(A_i)$, a workhorse result in bounding tail probabilities and establishing uniform convergence.

Parametric families of distributions arise when we restrict attention to collections of probability measures indexed by a finite-dimensional parameter vector $\theta \in \Theta \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k$. The Gaussian family $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with density $f(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$ is parameterized by location $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$ and scale $\sigma^2 > 0$. The exponential family encompasses a rich class of distributions admitting the canonical form $f(x \mid \theta) = h(x) \exp\left(\eta(\theta)^T T(x) - A(\theta)\right)$, where $\eta(\theta)$ denotes the natural parameter, $T(x)$ the sufficient statistic, and $A(\theta)$ the log-partition function ensuring normalization. Members include the Poisson, binomial, exponential, gamma, and beta distributions, each possessing elegant analytical properties stemming from the exponential structure.

The moment generating function $M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]$ serves as a powerful analytical tool, encoding the entire distribution through its Taylor series expansion $M_X(t) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{\mathbb{E}[X^n]}{n!} t^n$. The coefficient of $t^n/n!$ in this expansion recovers the $n$-th moment $\mathbb{E}[X^n]$, justifying the nomenclature. More profoundly, the moment generating function satisfies the uniqueness property: if $M_X(t) = M_Y(t)$ for all $t$ in a neighborhood of zero, then $X$ and $Y$ share the same distribution. This establishes an equivalence between the distribution and its moment generating function, permitting us to work in whichever domain proves more analytically tractable. For sums of independent random variables, the moment generating function of the sum factorizes as the product of individual moment generating functions, transforming convolution of distributions into pointwise multiplication of generating functions.

Central to applied statistics is the theory of convergence for sequences of random variables, formalizing the limiting behavior of estimators, test statistics, and empirical processes. We distinguish four principal modes of convergence, arranged in decreasing order of strength. Almost sure convergence $X_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} X$ requires $\mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{n \to \infty} X_n = X\right) = 1$, asserting that the sample paths converge with probability one. Convergence in probability $X_n \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}} X$ demands $\lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon) = 0$ for every $\varepsilon > 0$, a weaker condition permitting exceptional sets of diminishing probability. Convergence in distribution $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$ requires only $\lim_{n \to \infty} F_{X_n}(x) = F_X(x)$ at all continuity points of $F_X$, asserting convergence of cumulative distribution functions rather than random variables themselves. Finally, convergence in $L^p$ norm $X_n \xrightarrow{L^p} X$ stipulates $\lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{E}[|X_n - X|^p] = 0$, a metric convergence in the Banach space $L^p(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$.

The hierarchy of implications among these convergence modes follows from measure-theoretic considerations. Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability, proven by observing that the event $\{|X_n - X| > \varepsilon\}$ occurs with diminishing frequency along almost every sample path. Convergence in $L^p$ implies convergence in probability by Markov's inequality: $\mathbb{P}(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon) \le \frac{\mathbb{E}[|X_n - X|^p]}{\varepsilon^p} \to 0$. Convergence in probability implies convergence in distribution, verified by showing that pointwise convergence of characteristic functions at continuity points suffices for weak convergence of measures. The converse implications generally fail, though convergence in distribution to a constant implies convergence in probability to that constant, a useful technical result in asymptotic theory.

The Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem constitute the twin pillars of asymptotic statistical theory, providing the foundation for sample-based inference about population parameters. The Weak Law of Large Numbers asserts that for independent identically distributed random variables $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ with common mean $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X_i]$ and finite variance $\sigma^2 = \text{Var}(X_i)$, the sample mean $\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ converges in probability to $\mu$. The proof proceeds by verifying $\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}_n] = \mu$ and $\text{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \sigma^2/n$, then applying Chebyshev's inequality to obtain $\mathbb{P}(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| > \varepsilon) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2} \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$. This establishes the consistency of the sample mean as an estimator of the population mean, a cornerstone result in statistical inference.

The Strong Law of Large Numbers strengthens this conclusion to almost sure convergence, asserting $\mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{n \to \infty} \bar{X}_n = \mu\right) = 1$. The proof under the assumption of finite fourth moments employs Kolmogorov's maximal inequality and the Borel-Cantelli lemma to establish that the event $\{|\bar{X}_n - \mu| > \varepsilon\}$ occurs only finitely often with probability one. The strongest version, Kolmogorov's Strong Law, requires only the existence of the first moment, demonstrating the robustness of the convergence phenomenon. This almost sure convergence justifies the frequentist interpretation of probability as the long-run relative frequency of events, grounding statistical practice in firm mathematical soil.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The internal architecture of probability distributions rests on the interplay between the cumulative distribution function, the density or mass function, and the characteristic function $\varphi_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{itX}]$, which always exists even when the moment generating function diverges. These three representations provide complementary perspectives on the same probabilistic object, each suited to different analytical tasks. The cumulative distribution function $F_X(x) = \mathbb{P}(X \le x)$ is always right-continuous with left limits, non-decreasing, and satisfies $\lim_{x \to -\infty} F_X(x) = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to \infty} F_X(x) = 1$. These properties characterize the class of distribution functions completely: any function satisfying these constraints arises as the cumulative distribution function of some random variable, by the Skorokhod representation theorem.

For discrete random variables taking values in a countable set $\mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, the probability mass function $p_X(x) = \mathbb{P}(X = x)$ fully specifies the distribution. The cumulative distribution function is then the jump function $F_X(x) = \sum_{y \le x} p_X(y)$, exhibiting discontinuities at each point in the support. The expectation of a function $g(X)$ is computed via the discrete sum $\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} g(x) p_X(x)$, provided this sum converges absolutely. For the geometric distribution with parameter $p \in (0,1]$, representing the number of trials until the first success, the mass function is $p_X(k) = (1-p)^{k-1}p$ for $k = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$. The expectation is derived through the generating function technique: $\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{k=1}^\infty k(1-p)^{k-1}p = p \frac{d}{ds}\bigg|_{s=1-p} \sum_{k=1}^\infty s^k = p \frac{d}{ds}\bigg|_{s=1-p} \frac{s}{1-s} = p \cdot \frac{1}{p^2} = \frac{1}{p}$.

For absolutely continuous random variables, the probability density function $f_X(x)$ satisfies $\mathbb{P}(X \in A) = \int_A f_X(x) \, dx$ for all measurable sets $A$. The density is non-negative and integrates to one, but unlike the cumulative distribution function, it need not be continuous or even bounded. The cumulative distribution function is recovered via $F_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f_X(t) \, dt$, and when $F_X$ is differentiable, the density is its derivative: $f_X(x) = F_X'(x)$. Expectations of functions of $X$ are computed via $\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty g(x) f_X(x) \, dx$, provided the integral converges absolutely. The uniform distribution $\text{Unif}(a,b)$ on the interval $[a,b]$ has constant density $f_X(x) = \frac{1}{b-a}$ for $x \in [a,b]$ and zero elsewhere, yielding the linear cumulative distribution function $F_X(x) = \frac{x-a}{b-a}$ for $x \in [a,b]$.

The exponential distribution $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ with rate parameter $\lambda > 0$ models the waiting time until an event in a Poisson process. Its density $f_X(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ for $x \ge 0$ yields cumulative distribution function $F_X(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}$ for $x \ge 0$. The memoryless property characterizes the exponential distribution uniquely among continuous distributions: for all $s, t > 0$, we have $\mathbb{P}(X > s+t \mid X > s) = \mathbb{P}(X > t)$. This is verified algebraically as $\frac{\mathbb{P}(X > s+t)}{\mathbb{P}(X > s)} = \frac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} = e^{-\lambda t} = \mathbb{P}(X > t)$. The moment generating function is $M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}] = \int_0^\infty e^{tx} \lambda e^{-\lambda x} \, dx = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda - t}$ for $t < \lambda$, from which we extract moments by differentiation: $\mathbb{E}[X] = M_X'(0) = \frac{1}{\lambda}$ and $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = M_X''(0) = \frac{2}{\lambda^2}$, yielding variance $\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}$.

The Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ occupies a central position in probability theory due to its ubiquity as a limiting distribution and its analytical tractability. The density $f_X(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$ is symmetric about $\mu$ and decays exponentially in the tails. The moment generating function $M_X(t) = \exp\left(\mu t + \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2}\right)$ is finite for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$, and its logarithm $\log M_X(t) = \mu t + \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2}$ is a quadratic function encoding the cumulants of the distribution. The linear transformation property asserts that if $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, then $aX + b \sim \mathcal{N}(a\mu + b, a^2\sigma^2)$ for constants $a, b$, verified by computing the moment generating function of $aX + b$ and recognizing it as Gaussian. The convolution of independent Gaussians is Gaussian: if $X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)$ independently, then $\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\sum_{i=1}^n \mu_i, \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i^2\right)$, proven by factorizing the moment generating function of the sum.

The standard normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ serves as the canonical representative, with cumulative distribution function denoted $\Phi(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-t^2/2} \, dt$. This integral lacks a closed-form expression in terms of elementary functions, necessitating numerical approximation or tabulation. The error function $\text{erf}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_0^x e^{-t^2} \, dt$ is related to $\Phi$ via $\Phi(x) = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \text{erf}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)$, providing a bridge to numerical libraries implementing special functions. The quantile function $\Phi^{-1}(p)$ for $p \in (0,1)$ inverts the cumulative distribution function, yielding the value $x$ such that $\mathbb{P}(X \le x) = p$ for $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$. This function is essential for constructing confidence intervals and hypothesis tests based on asymptotic normality.

The gamma distribution $\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ with shape parameter $\alpha > 0$ and rate parameter $\beta > 0$ generalizes the exponential distribution. Its density $f_X(x) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta x}$ for $x > 0$ involves the gamma function $\Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^\infty t^{\alpha-1} e^{-t} \, dt$, which satisfies the functional equation $\Gamma(\alpha+1) = \alpha \Gamma(\alpha)$ and reduces to $\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!$ for positive integers $n$. The moment generating function $M_X(t) = \left(\frac{\beta}{\beta - t}\right)^\alpha$ for $t < \beta$ reveals that the sum of independent $\text{Gamma}(\alpha_i, \beta)$ random variables with common rate is $\text{Gamma}\left(\sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i, \beta\right)$, a convolution property exploited in queueing theory and Bayesian inference. The chi-squared distribution $\chi^2_k$ is the special case $\text{Gamma}(k/2, 1/2)$, representing the distribution of the sum of squares of $k$ independent standard normal random variables.

The beta distribution $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ on the interval $[0,1]$ with shape parameters $\alpha, \beta > 0$ has density $f_X(x) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)} x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}$ for $x \in [0,1]$. The normalizing constant involves the beta function $B(\alpha,\beta) = \int_0^1 x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1} \, dx = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}$, related to the gamma function through this identity. The beta distribution serves as the conjugate prior for the binomial likelihood in Bayesian inference, permitting analytical posterior computation. For integer parameters $\alpha = n$ and $\beta = m$, the beta distribution represents the distribution of the $n$-th order statistic of $n+m-1$ independent uniform random variables, a connection formalized through Pólya's urn model.

The Poisson distribution $\text{Pois}(\lambda)$ with rate parameter $\lambda > 0$ models the number of events in a fixed time interval when events occur independently at constant rate. Its mass function $p_X(k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}$ for $k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ satisfies both $\mathbb{E}[X] = \lambda$ and $\text{Var}(X) = \lambda$, a characteristic property distinguishing it from other count distributions. The moment generating function $M_X(t) = \exp(\lambda(e^t - 1))$ encodes the convolution property: the sum of independent Poisson random variables is Poisson with rate equal to the sum of individual rates. The Poisson distribution arises as the limit of binomial distributions $\text{Binom}(n,p)$ as $n \to \infty$ and $p \to 0$ with $np \to \lambda$, formalized in the Poisson limit theorem. This limiting relationship justifies the Poisson approximation for rare events in large populations, a cornerstone of actuarial science and reliability theory.

The multivariate Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}_d(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$ for random vectors $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is characterized by mean vector $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and positive semi-definite covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. When $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ is positive definite, the density is $f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{1/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\right)$. The moment generating function $M_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{t}) = \exp\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}^T \mathbf{t} + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{t}\right)$ generalizes the univariate formula, and affine transformations preserve Gaussianity: $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b} \sim \mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^T)$ for $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times d}$ and $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^k$. Marginal distributions of subvectors are Gaussian, and conditional distributions given observed components are Gaussian with mean and covariance determined by the partitioned inverse covariance matrix, the precision matrix.

The characteristic function $\varphi_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{itX}]$ extends the moment generating function to the complex exponential, always existing due to the boundedness $|e^{itX}| = 1$. The inversion formula $f_X(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-itx} \varphi_X(t) \, dt$ recovers the density from the characteristic function, establishing a Fourier duality between the distribution and its characteristic function. The continuity theorem asserts that convergence of characteristic functions pointwise to a continuous limit implies convergence in distribution, providing a powerful tool for proving limit theorems. For the Cauchy distribution with density $f_X(x) = \frac{1}{\pi(1+x^2)}$, the characteristic function is $\varphi_X(t) = e^{-|t|}$, and the moment generating function fails to exist since $\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]$ diverges for all $t \ne 0$. This illustrates the broader applicability of characteristic functions in distributions with heavy tails.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

The practical application of probability distributions in statistical computing demands careful attention to numerical stability, algorithmic complexity, and edge case handling. The computation of cumulative distribution functions, quantile functions, and random variate generation constitutes the core operational bottleneck in Monte Carlo simulation, bootstrap resampling, and parametric inference. For the standard normal distribution, naive evaluation of $\Phi(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-t^2/2} \, dt$ through numerical quadrature proves inefficient and numerically unstable for extreme tail probabilities. Instead, rational approximations based on Padé approximants achieve relative error below $10^{-15}$ with evaluation cost of approximately ten floating-point operations, exploiting symmetry via $\Phi(x) = 1 - \Phi(-x)$ for $x > 0$ to restrict computation to positive arguments.

For extreme tail probabilities $\Phi(x)$ with $|x| > 6$, direct evaluation suffers catastrophic cancellation in floating-point arithmetic. The complementary approach computes the survival function $\bar{\Phi}(x) = 1 - \Phi(x) = \mathbb{P}(X > x)$ using the Mills ratio bound $\frac{\phi(x)}{\bar{\Phi}(x)} = \frac{x}{1 + x^2/(1 + 3x^2/(\cdots))}$, where $\phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}$ denotes the standard normal density. Asymptotic expansions of $\bar{\Phi}(x)$ for large $x$ yield $\bar{\Phi}(x) \sim \frac{\phi(x)}{x}\left(1 - \frac{1}{x^2} + \frac{1 \cdot 3}{x^4} - \frac{1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5}{x^6} + \cdots\right)$, a divergent series that nonetheless provides accurate approximation when truncated appropriately. For $x > 8.3$, retaining only the leading term $\bar{\Phi}(x) \approx \phi(x)/x$ achieves relative error below $10^{-15}$.

The generation of random variates from specified distributions leverages transformations of uniform random variables. The inverse transform method generates $X$ from distribution $F_X$ by computing $X = F_X^{-1}(U)$ for $U \sim \text{Unif}(0,1)$, verified by observing $\mathbb{P}(X \le x) = \mathbb{P}(F_X^{-1}(U) \le x) = \mathbb{P}(U \le F_X(x)) = F_X(x)$. For the exponential distribution with cumulative distribution function $F_X(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}$, the inverse is $F_X^{-1}(u) = -\frac{1}{\lambda}\log(1-u)$, yielding the generation formula $X = -\frac{1}{\lambda}\log(U)$. Since $U$ and $1-U$ share the same distribution, the equivalent formula $X = -\frac{1}{\lambda}\log(U)$ avoids the subtraction, saving one floating-point operation per variate.

For distributions lacking closed-form quantile functions, numerical inversion of $F_X$ through Newton-Raphson iteration or bisection proves necessary. The Gaussian quantile function $\Phi^{-1}(p)$ admits rational approximations achieving relative error below $10^{-9}$ across the central range $0.025 < p < 0.975$, sufficient for most statistical applications. In the extreme tails $p < 10^{-10}$ or $p > 1 - 10^{-10}$, asymptotic inversion formulas based on the inverse Mills ratio provide stable evaluation. The complementary approach for $p$ near one computes $\Phi^{-1}(1-p)$ via $-\Phi^{-1}(p)$ using symmetry, maintaining precision when $p$ is represented as a small floating-point number.

Rejection sampling generates variates from a target density $f(x)$ by proposing candidates from an envelope density $g(x)$ satisfying $f(x) \le M g(x)$ for some constant $M \ge 1$. The algorithm generates $Y \sim g$ and $U \sim \text{Unif}(0,1)$ independently, accepting $X = Y$ if $U \le \frac{f(Y)}{Mg(Y)}$, and rejecting otherwise. The acceptance probability is $1/M$, so minimizing $M$ maximizes efficiency. For the standard normal distribution, the Ziggurat algorithm partitions the area under the density into horizontal rectangles and a tail region, achieving acceptance probability above ninety-eight percent and generating variates in under ten CPU cycles on modern architectures. This method outperforms the Box-Muller transformation $X = \sqrt{-2\log(U_1)}\cos(2\pi U_2)$, which requires expensive transcendental function evaluations.

The computation of moment generating functions $M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]$ for large $|t|$ suffers overflow for heavy-tailed distributions. The cumulant generating function $K_X(t) = \log M_X(t)$ provides a numerically stable alternative, with cumulants $\kappa_n$ extracted via $\kappa_n = K_X^{(n)}(0)$. For the Gaussian distribution, $K_X(t) = \mu t + \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2}$ is a quadratic polynomial, revealing that all cumulants beyond the second vanish. The exponential family structure $K_X(t) = \eta t - A(\eta + t) + A(\eta)$ in natural parameterization ensures that the cumulant generating function is convex, with second derivative $K_X''(t) = \text{Var}(e^{tX})/(M_X(t))^2 \ge 0$, a property exploited in large deviations theory.

The numerical evaluation of the gamma function $\Gamma(\alpha)$ for arbitrary real $\alpha > 0$ employs Stirling's approximation $\Gamma(\alpha) \sim \sqrt{2\pi/\alpha} \, (\alpha/e)^\alpha$ for large $\alpha$, achieving relative error $O(1/\alpha)$. For $\alpha$ near positive integers, the functional equation $\Gamma(\alpha+1) = \alpha \Gamma(\alpha)$ reduces the computation to evaluation at $\alpha \in [1,2]$, where minimax polynomial approximations provide high accuracy. The logarithm $\log \Gamma(\alpha)$ is computed directly to avoid overflow, with Lanczos approximation achieving fifteen-digit accuracy using seven to nine coefficients. The digamma function $\psi(\alpha) = \frac{d}{d\alpha}\log\Gamma(\alpha) = \frac{\Gamma'(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}$ appears in maximum likelihood estimation for gamma and beta distributions, computed via asymptotic series or recurrence relations.

The Beta distribution $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ with large parameters $\alpha, \beta \gg 1$ concentrates probability near the mode $((\alpha-1)/(\alpha+\beta-2)$, approaching a Gaussian distribution in the limit. The variance approximation $\text{Var}(X) \approx \frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)} \approx \frac{1}{4(\alpha+\beta)}$ for $\alpha \approx \beta$ large justifies the normal approximation $X \approx \mathcal{N}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}, \frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)}\right)$. For small parameters $\alpha, \beta < 1$, the beta density exhibits singularities at the endpoints $x=0$ and $x=1$, necessitating careful numerical handling in likelihood evaluation. Random variate generation employs the characterization $X = \frac{Y_1}{Y_1 + Y_2}$ for independent $Y_1 \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, 1)$ and $Y_2 \sim \text{Gamma}(\beta, 1)$, reducing beta generation to gamma generation.

The Poisson distribution $\text{Pois}(\lambda)$ for large $\lambda$ admits normal approximation $X \approx \mathcal{N}(\lambda, \lambda)$ by the Central Limit Theorem, since the Poisson arises as the sum of $\lambda$ independent $\text{Pois}(1)$ random variables through the convolution property. The continuity correction $\mathbb{P}(X \le k) \approx \Phi\left(\frac{k+0.5-\lambda}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)$ improves accuracy for moderate $\lambda > 10$. For very large $\lambda > 10^6$, direct computation of the mass function $p_X(k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}$ suffers severe underflow. The logarithmic form $\log p_X(k) = k \log \lambda - \lambda - \log \Gamma(k+1)$ permits stable evaluation, with Stirling's approximation applied to $\log(k!)$ for large $k$. Random variate generation for $\lambda < 10$ employs Knuth's algorithm, generating exponential inter-arrival times until their sum exceeds one, while for $\lambda \ge 10$, transformation methods based on normal approximation prove more efficient.

The multivariate Gaussian density evaluation for $d$-dimensional vectors requires $O(d^3)$ operations for naive inversion of the covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$. The Cholesky decomposition $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T$ for lower-triangular $\mathbf{L}$ reduces this to $O(d^3)$ once, with subsequent density evaluations requiring $O(d^2)$ operations through forward and backward substitution to compute $(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = \|\mathbf{L}^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\|^2$. The log-determinant $\log |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}| = 2\sum_{i=1}^d \log L_{ii}$ is computed from the diagonal elements of $\mathbf{L}$, avoiding explicit determinant calculation. For singular covariance matrices arising in constrained multivariate distributions, the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse replaces $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}$, and the density is supported on a lower-dimensional affine subspace.

The Central Limit Theorem asserts that for independent identically distributed random variables $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, the standardized sum $Z_n = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n X_i - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} = \frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}$ converges in distribution to $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ as $n \to \infty$. The proof employs characteristic functions: writing $\varphi_{X_i - \mu}(t) = 1 - \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2} + o(t^2)$ by Taylor expansion, the characteristic function of $Z_n$ is $\varphi_{Z_n}(t) = \left(\varphi_{X_i - \mu}\left(\frac{t}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\right)\right)^n = \left(1 - \frac{t^2}{2n} + o(1/n)\right)^n \to e^{-t^2/2}$ as $n \to \infty$, recognizing this as the characteristic function of $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$. The continuity theorem then guarantees convergence in distribution.

The Berry-Esseen theorem quantifies the rate of convergence in the Central Limit Theorem, asserting that under the existence of the third absolute moment $\rho = \mathbb{E}[|X_i - \mu|^3]$, the supremum distance between the cumulative distribution function of $Z_n$ and the standard normal satisfies $\sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} |F_{Z_n}(x) - \Phi(x)| \le \frac{C\rho}{\sigma^3\sqrt{n}}$ for an absolute constant $C < 0.5$. This $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ convergence rate justifies the use of normal approximations for sample sizes $n > 30$ in many practical applications, though heavy-tailed distributions with large $\rho/\sigma^3$ require substantially larger samples for accurate approximation.

The Lindeberg-Feller Central Limit Theorem extends the result to triangular arrays of independent but not identically distributed random variables, replacing the identical distribution assumption with the Lindeberg condition. For row-wise independent random variables $X_{n,k}$ with $\mathbb{E}[X_{n,k}] = 0$ and $\sum_{k=1}^n \mathbb{E}[X_{n,k}^2] = 1$, the Lindeberg condition $\lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^n \mathbb{E}[X_{n,k}^2 \mathbf{1}_{|X_{n,k}| > \varepsilon}] = 0$ for all $\varepsilon > 0$ ensures $\sum_{k=1}^n X_{n,k} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$. This condition formalizes the intuition that no single summand dominates the variance, permitting asymptotic normality even when individual variances differ across terms.

The Delta Method provides asymptotic distributions for smooth transformations of estimators. If $\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ and $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuously differentiable at $\theta$ with $g'(\theta) \ne 0$, then $\sqrt{n}(g(\hat{\theta}_n) - g(\theta)) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, (g'(\theta))^2 \sigma^2)$. The proof applies a first-order Taylor expansion $g(\hat{\theta}_n) \approx g(\theta) + g'(\theta)(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta)$, then Slutsky's theorem to handle the vanishing remainder term. For the variance-stabilizing transformation of a Poisson mean estimator $\hat{\lambda}_n$ with $\sqrt{n}(\hat{\lambda}_n - \lambda) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \lambda)$, the transformation $g(\lambda) = 2\sqrt{\lambda}$ satisfies $g'(\lambda) = 1/\sqrt{\lambda}$, yielding $\sqrt{n}(2\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}_n} - 2\sqrt{\lambda}) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, achieving constant asymptotic variance independent of $\lambda$.

Convergence diagnostics in Monte Carlo simulation require assessment of both bias and variance in estimators of expectations $\mathbb{E}[g(X)]$ based on independent samples $X_1, \ldots, X_n$. The sample mean $\hat{\mu}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n g(X_i)$ is unbiased with standard error $\text{SE}(\hat{\mu}_n) = \frac{\sigma_g}{\sqrt{n}}$, where $\sigma_g^2 = \text{Var}(g(X))$. The effective sample size $n_{\text{eff}} = n / (1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^\infty \rho_k)$ accounts for autocorrelation $\rho_k = \text{Corr}(g(X_t), g(X_{t+k}))$ in Markov chain Monte Carlo, with $\text{SE}(\hat{\mu}_n) \approx \sigma_g/\sqrt{n_{\text{eff}}}$. Batch means estimators partition the sample into $m$ batches of size $b = n/m$, computing batch means $\bar{g}_j = \frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=(j-1)b+1}^{jb} g(X_i)$ and estimating $\text{Var}(\hat{\mu}_n) \approx \frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{j=1}^m (\bar{g}_j - \hat{\mu}_n)^2$, which remains consistent even under autocorrelation.

The failure mode of distributional approximation arises when tail probabilities differ substantially between the true distribution and its approximation. For the binomial distribution $\text{Binom}(n,p)$, the normal approximation $\mathcal{N}(np, np(1-p))$ deteriorates for extreme $p$ near zero or one, with relative error in tail probabilities growing without bound. The Poisson approximation $\text{Pois}(np)$ proves superior for $p < 0.1$, capturing the discrete nature and achieving relative error $O(p)$. For $n$ large and $p$ moderate, the continuity-corrected normal approximation $\mathbb{P}(X \le k) \approx \Phi\left(\frac{k+0.5-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right)$ reduces discretization error from $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ to $O(1/n)$, essential for accurate hypothesis testing at conventional significance levels.
