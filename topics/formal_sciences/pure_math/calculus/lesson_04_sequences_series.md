# Lesson 4: Sequences, Series, Convergence Tests

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The theory of infinite sequences and series stands as one of the profound intellectual achievements in mathematical analysis, serving as the foundation upon which calculus, real analysis, and much of modern theoretical mathematics rests. When we confront an infinite sequence—a function from the natural numbers into the real numbers, written $a_n$ or $(a_n)_{n=1}^{\infty}$—we immediately encounter a conceptual leap beyond finite arithmetic. The question "what happens as $n$ approaches infinity?" demands a rigorous answer, one that cannot be satisfied by naive intuition alone. The epsilon-delta framework inherited from Cauchy and Weierstrass provides that rigor, translating the vague notion of "approaching a value" into a precise logical statement involving quantifiers and inequalities.

A sequence $(a_n)$ converges to a limit $L$ if for every tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, no matter how small, there exists a threshold index $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that all subsequent terms satisfy $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$ whenever $n \geq N$. This definition captures the essential behavior: beyond some point in the sequence, all terms cluster arbitrarily close to $L$. The limit $L$, when it exists, is unique—a fact that follows immediately from the triangle inequality. If $(a_n)$ converged to both $L$ and $M$ with $L \neq M$, then choosing $\varepsilon = |L - M|/2$ would force terms to lie simultaneously in disjoint neighborhoods, an impossibility.

The monotone convergence theorem stands as one of the most elegant existence results in real analysis. Every bounded monotone sequence converges. The proof exploits the completeness axiom of the real numbers, specifically the least upper bound property. Consider a monotone increasing sequence $(a_n)$ that is bounded above by some constant $M$. The set $\{a_n : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$ admits a supremum $L = \sup\{a_n\}$ by completeness. For any $\varepsilon > 0$, the definition of supremum guarantees the existence of some term $a_N$ with $L - \varepsilon < a_N \leq L$. Since the sequence is increasing, all terms $a_n$ with $n \geq N$ satisfy $L - \varepsilon < a_N \leq a_n \leq L < L + \varepsilon$, establishing convergence to $L$. The symmetric argument applies to bounded decreasing sequences with the infimum playing the role of limit. This theorem transforms existence questions into boundedness questions, a massive simplification in practice.

Cauchy sequences internalize the convergence criterion without reference to the limit itself. A sequence $(a_n)$ is Cauchy if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N$ such that $|a_m - a_n| < \varepsilon$ whenever both $m, n \geq N$. The terms become arbitrarily close to each other, clustering together without necessarily knowing where they cluster. In complete metric spaces—and the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ constitute the prototypical complete metric space—every Cauchy sequence converges. The proof proceeds by first showing every Cauchy sequence is bounded, then extracting a convergent subsequence via Bolzano-Weierstrass, and finally verifying that the full sequence converges to the same limit. The equivalence between convergence and the Cauchy property provides a powerful alternative characterization, one that proves indispensable in function spaces and abstract analysis.

The transition from sequences to series introduces summation into the infinite realm. An infinite series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ is defined through its sequence of partial sums $S_N = \sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n$. The series converges to $S$ if and only if the sequence $(S_N)$ converges to $S$. This definition immediately reveals that series convergence depends entirely on the limiting behavior of accumulated finite sums. A necessary condition for convergence emerges instantly: if $\sum a_n$ converges, then $a_n \to 0$. The proof is straightforward—since $a_n = S_n - S_{n-1}$ and both partial sums converge to the same limit $S$, we have $\lim a_n = S - S = 0$. This condition, while necessary, proves woefully insufficient. The harmonic series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n}$ diverges despite having terms that vanish, a fact demonstrated through the comparison $\sum_{n=1}^{2^k} \frac{1}{n} > k/2$ by grouping terms into blocks that each exceed $1/2$.

The distinction between absolute and conditional convergence cuts to the heart of series behavior. A series $\sum a_n$ converges absolutely if $\sum |a_n|$ converges. Absolute convergence implies ordinary convergence through the completeness of $\mathbb{R}$: the partial sums of $\sum a_n$ form a Cauchy sequence because $|S_m - S_n| = |\sum_{k=n+1}^{m} a_k| \leq \sum_{k=n+1}^{m} |a_k|$ can be made arbitrarily small by the Cauchy property of the absolute partial sums. Series that converge but not absolutely—conditionally convergent series—exhibit pathological behavior. The alternating harmonic series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}$ converges to $\ln 2$ by the alternating series test, yet its absolute version diverges. Riemann's rearrangement theorem reveals the profound instability of conditional convergence: any conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to converge to any prescribed value or to diverge. Absolute convergence, in contrast, guarantees that all rearrangements converge to the same sum, rendering the series robust under permutation.

Power series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n (x - a)^n$ represent functions through infinite polynomials, blending analysis with algebra. Every power series has a radius of convergence $R \in [0, \infty]$ such that the series converges absolutely for $|x - a| < R$ and diverges for $|x - a| > R$. The behavior at the boundary points $|x - a| = R$ requires individual investigation and varies wildly between series. The radius emerges from the root test through the formula $1/R = \limsup_{n \to \infty} \sqrt[n]{|c_n|}$, or equivalently from the ratio test via $1/R = \lim_{n \to \infty} |c_{n+1}/c_n|$ when the limit exists. Within the radius of convergence, power series enjoy remarkable properties: they are infinitely differentiable, term-by-term differentiation and integration preserve convergence, and they define analytic functions. The exponential series $e^x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n!}$ has infinite radius of convergence since $\sqrt[n]{1/n!} \to 0$, while the geometric series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x^n$ has radius one because $|x|^n$ behaves like $|x|^n$ asymptotically, with clear divergence for $|x| \geq 1$.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The convergence tests for series form a hierarchical toolkit, each test exploiting different structural properties of the terms. The comparison test operates on the principle of domination: if $0 \leq a_n \leq b_n$ for all sufficiently large $n$, then convergence of $\sum b_n$ implies convergence of $\sum a_n$, while divergence of $\sum a_n$ implies divergence of $\sum b_n$. The partial sums of the smaller series are bounded above by those of the larger, and bounded increasing sequences converge. The archetypal benchmark series is the $p$-series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^p}$, which converges if and only if $p > 1$. The proof for the convergence case employs the integral test, comparing the series to $\int_1^{\infty} \frac{1}{x^p} \, dx = \lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{x^{1-p}}{1-p}\big|_1^t$, which converges precisely when $p > 1$. The divergence of the $p=1$ case—the harmonic series—follows from the grouping argument or directly from the divergent integral $\int_1^{\infty} \frac{1}{x} \, dx = \ln x \big|_1^{\infty}$.

The limit comparison test refines the basic comparison by examining asymptotic ratios. Given two series $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ with positive terms, if $\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{a_n}{b_n} = L$ where $0 < L < \infty$, then the two series share the same convergence behavior. The proof establishes two-sided inequalities: for large $n$, we have $\frac{L}{2} b_n < a_n < 2L b_n$, allowing direct comparison. This test proves invaluable when dealing with rational functions or algebraic expressions where leading terms dominate. Consider $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{3n^2 + 5n + 1}{n^4 - 2n + 7}$. The leading behavior is $\frac{3n^2}{n^4} = \frac{3}{n^2}$, and indeed $\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{(3n^2 + 5n + 1)/(n^4 - 2n + 7)}{3/n^2} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n^2(3n^2 + 5n + 1)}{3(n^4 - 2n + 7)} = 1$. Since $\sum \frac{3}{n^2}$ converges as a multiple of the convergent $p$-series with $p=2$, the original series converges.

The ratio test exploits exponential-type behavior encoded in the ratio of consecutive terms. For a series $\sum a_n$ with positive terms, compute $L = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}$. If $L < 1$, the series converges absolutely; if $L > 1$, the series diverges; if $L = 1$, the test is inconclusive. The proof for the $L < 1$ case constructs a geometric domination: choose $r$ with $L < r < 1$. For sufficiently large $n \geq N$, we have $\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} < r$, yielding $a_{n+1} < r \cdot a_n < r^2 \cdot a_{n-1} < \cdots < r^{n+1-N} a_N$. The tail of the series is dominated by a convergent geometric series $a_N \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} r^k$. The divergence case follows similarly: when $L > 1$, the terms eventually increase, violating the necessary condition $a_n \to 0$. The inconclusiveness at $L = 1$ is demonstrated by the $p$-series: $\sum \frac{1}{n^p}$ yields $\lim \frac{n^p}{(n+1)^p} = 1$ for all $p$, yet the series converges for $p > 1$ and diverges for $p \leq 1$.

The root test addresses series through the $n$-th root of the $n$-th term, capturing exponential growth even more directly than the ratio test. Define $L = \limsup_{n \to \infty} \sqrt[n]{|a_n|}$. If $L < 1$, the series $\sum a_n$ converges absolutely; if $L > 1$, the series diverges; if $L = 1$, the test fails. The proof mirrors the ratio test but uses $\limsup$ rather than $\lim$, accommodating oscillatory behavior. When $L < 1$, choose $r$ with $L < r < 1$. The defining property of $\limsup$ guarantees that $\sqrt[n]{|a_n|} < r$ for all sufficiently large $n$, hence $|a_n| < r^n$, and the series is dominated by the geometric series $\sum r^n$. The root test proves superior to the ratio test when terms involve $n$-th powers, as in $\sum \left(\frac{n}{2n+1}\right)^n$. Here $\sqrt[n]{a_n} = \frac{n}{2n+1} \to \frac{1}{2}$, immediately establishing convergence without computing any ratios.

The integral test forges a bridge between series and improper integrals, applicable when terms are given by a positive, decreasing, continuous function. If $f : [1, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}$ is positive and decreasing with $f(n) = a_n$, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ and $\int_1^{\infty} f(x) \, dx$ share convergence behavior. The geometric argument visualizes the partial sums as right Riemann sums and the integral as the area under the curve. Since $f$ is decreasing, we have the sandwiching inequalities $\int_1^{N+1} f(x) \, dx \leq \sum_{n=1}^{N} f(n) \leq f(1) + \int_1^{N} f(x) \, dx$. If the integral converges to some finite value $I$, the partial sums are bounded above by $f(1) + I$, hence the series converges by the monotone convergence theorem. Conversely, if the integral diverges, the partial sums diverge as they bound the integral from above. This test proves the convergence of $\sum \frac{1}{n(\ln n)^p}$ for $p > 1$ through the substitution $u = \ln x$ in $\int_2^{\infty} \frac{1}{x(\ln x)^p} \, dx = \int_{\ln 2}^{\infty} \frac{1}{u^p} \, du$.

The alternating series test, also known as Leibniz's test, handles series of the form $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{n-1} b_n$ where $(b_n)$ is a decreasing sequence of positive terms converging to zero. Under these hypotheses, the series converges. The proof constructs two subsequences of partial sums: the even partials $S_{2k}$ and the odd partials $S_{2k+1}$. By telescoping, $S_{2k+2} - S_{2k} = -b_{2k+1} + b_{2k+2} = -(b_{2k+1} - b_{2k+2}) \leq 0$ since $(b_n)$ decreases, showing $(S_{2k})$ is decreasing. Similarly, $S_{2k+1} = S_{2k} + b_{2k+1}$ and $S_{2k+3} - S_{2k+1} = -b_{2k+2} + b_{2k+3} \geq 0$ proves $(S_{2k+1})$ is increasing. Furthermore, $S_{2k+1} = S_{2k} + b_{2k+1} \geq S_{2k}$, so the increasing odd partials lie below the decreasing even partials. Both subsequences are monotone and bounded, hence convergent by monotone convergence. Since $S_{2k+1} - S_{2k} = b_{2k+1} \to 0$, they converge to the same limit $S$. Thus all partial sums converge to $S$. The remainder estimate $|S - S_N| \leq b_{N+1}$ provides a quantitative error bound: the error in truncating an alternating series is no more than the first omitted term.

Power series convergence analysis reduces to determining the radius $R$ through the ratio or root test applied to the coefficients. Given $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n (x - a)^n$, the ratio test on $|c_n (x-a)^n|$ yields $\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{|c_{n+1}(x-a)^{n+1}|}{|c_n(x-a)^n|} = |x - a| \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{|c_{n+1}|}{|c_n|}$. Convergence requires this limit to be less than one, giving $|x - a| < R$ where $\frac{1}{R} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{|c_{n+1}|}{|c_n|}$. The root test similarly yields $\limsup_{n \to \infty} |c_n(x-a)^n|^{1/n} = |x - a| \limsup_{n \to \infty} |c_n|^{1/n} < 1$, defining $R$ through $\frac{1}{R} = \limsup_{n \to \infty} |c_n|^{1/n}$. The Cauchy-Hadamard formula $\frac{1}{R} = \limsup_{n \to \infty} \sqrt[n]{|c_n|}$ applies universally, even when the ratio limit fails to exist.

Consider the series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n!}$. The ratio of coefficients is $\frac{c_{n+1}}{c_n} = \frac{1/(n+1)!}{1/n!} = \frac{1}{n+1} \to 0$, hence $R = \infty$ and the series converges for all real $x$. This is the exponential series $e^x$, an entire function. In contrast, $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n! \, x^n$ has $\frac{c_{n+1}}{c_n} = n+1 \to \infty$, giving $R = 0$; the series converges only at $x = 0$. The geometric series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x^n$ has $c_n = 1$, so $R = 1$. At $x = 1$, the series diverges as $\sum 1$. At $x = -1$, the series becomes $\sum (-1)^n$, which oscillates and diverges. Thus the interval of convergence is $(-1, 1)$, open at both endpoints.

Abel's theorem governs boundary behavior: if a power series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n x^n$ with radius $R$ converges at an endpoint, say $x = R$, then the series converges uniformly on $[0, R]$, and the sum function is continuous at $R$. This allows the computation of limits like $\lim_{x \to R^-} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n x^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n R^n$ when the endpoint series converges. The logarithm series $\ln(1+x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n} x^n$ has radius one, converges conditionally at $x = 1$ to $\ln 2$, and diverges at $x = -1$. Abel's theorem guarantees $\lim_{x \to 1^-} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n} x^n = \ln 2$.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Practical convergence determination often begins with a rapid diagnostic: inspect the general term $a_n$. If $a_n \not\to 0$, the series diverges immediately by the divergence test. If the terms decay exponentially or factorially, the ratio test typically resolves convergence swiftly. If terms involve $n$-th powers or roots, the root test provides a cleaner calculation. For rational functions or algebraic expressions in $n$, the limit comparison test with a $p$-series benchmark yields the answer with minimal computation. When terms are given explicitly by a decreasing function, the integral test converts the problem into improper integral evaluation, often accessible through elementary calculus.

The $p$-series $\sum \frac{1}{n^p}$ serves as the universal comparison benchmark. Any series whose terms are asymptotically equivalent to $\frac{1}{n^p}$ inherits the $p$-series convergence behavior: convergence for $p > 1$, divergence for $p \leq 1$. Establishing asymptotic equivalence requires computing $\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{a_n}{1/n^p}$ and verifying the limit is positive and finite. This reduces vast swathes of convergence questions to determining the polynomial degree of growth in the numerator versus the denominator. The series $\sum \frac{n^3 + \sqrt{n}}{n^5 - 3n^2 + 1}$ has leading behavior $\frac{n^3}{n^5} = \frac{1}{n^2}$, matching the convergent $p=2$ series, establishing convergence via limit comparison.

The ratio test exhibits a failure mode at the boundary $L = 1$, requiring fallback to other tests. This occurs frequently with polynomial modifications of $p$-series. For $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$, the ratio is $\frac{n^2}{(n+1)^2} = \frac{1}{(1 + 1/n)^2} \to 1$, inconclusive. However, direct comparison with the convergent $p$-series resolves the issue instantly. The ratio test shines when factorials or exponentials appear: $\sum \frac{2^n}{n!}$ yields $\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} = \frac{2^{n+1}/(n+1)!}{2^n/n!} = \frac{2}{n+1} \to 0 < 1$, establishing convergence. The presence of factorial growth in the denominator dominates exponential growth in the numerator, a pattern the ratio test detects effortlessly.

Absolute convergence testing reduces to applying convergence tests to the series of absolute values $\sum |a_n|$. If this series converges, the original series converges absolutely, inheriting robustness properties like rearrangement invariance and Cauchy product compatibility. When $\sum |a_n|$ diverges but $\sum a_n$ converges, conditional convergence holds, signaling fragility. The alternating harmonic series exemplifies this: $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}$ converges by the alternating series test, but $\sum \frac{1}{n}$ diverges. Dirichlet's test and Abel's test provide broader criteria for conditional convergence, applicable when terms decompose into a product $a_n = b_n c_n$ with $(b_n)$ decreasing to zero and partial sums of $(c_n)$ bounded.

Power series manipulations—differentiation, integration, substitution—preserve the radius of convergence but may alter boundary behavior. Differentiating $\sum c_n x^n$ term-by-term yields $\sum n c_n x^{n-1}$, and the radius remains $R$ since $\limsup \sqrt[n]{n|c_n|} = \limsup \sqrt[n]{|c_n|}$ by the asymptotic equivalence $\sqrt[n]{n} \to 1$. Integration similarly preserves the radius. However, endpoint convergence can change: the geometric series $\sum x^n$ has radius one and diverges at both endpoints. Integrating term-by-term yields the logarithm series $\sum \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}$, which converges at $x = 1$, improving boundary behavior. Conversely, differentiating the logarithm series recovers the geometric series, losing endpoint convergence. This phenomenon reflects the smoothing effect of integration versus the sharpening effect of differentiation.

Numerical series evaluation for alternating series exploits the remainder estimate $|S - S_N| \leq b_{N+1}$. To achieve error tolerance $\varepsilon$, compute partial sums until $b_{N+1} < \varepsilon$. For $\sum \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}$ approximated to within $10^{-6}$, we need $\frac{1}{N+1} < 10^{-6}$, hence $N \geq 10^6 - 1$. This direct truncation proves inefficient. Acceleration techniques like Euler summation or Richardson extrapolation improve convergence rates dramatically. The Euler transform for an alternating series $\sum (-1)^n a_n$ is $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{2^{n+1}} \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} a_k$, often converging exponentially faster.

For non-alternating series, numerical evaluation requires estimating the tail $\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty} a_n$. When the integral test applies, the tail is bounded by $\int_N^{\infty} f(x) \, dx$, providing an upper estimate. For $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$, the tail beyond $N$ satisfies $\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} < \int_N^{\infty} \frac{1}{x^2} \, dx = \frac{1}{N}$. Summing the first $N$ terms and adding the tail bound yields an approximation with guaranteed error less than $\frac{1}{N}$. Achieving $10^{-6}$ accuracy requires $N = 10^6$ terms, computationally expensive but straightforward. The exact value $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$ emerges from Fourier series analysis, not elementary convergence tests, illustrating the gap between convergence verification and closed-form evaluation.

Power series representation of functions enables approximation through truncation. The exponential series $e^x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n!}$ with Taylor remainder $R_N(x) = \frac{e^c}{(N+1)!} x^{N+1}$ for some $c$ between zero and $x$ decays super-exponentially in $N$. For $x = 1$, approximating $e$ to $10^{-6}$ requires $\frac{e}{(N+1)!} < 10^{-6}$, achieved by $N = 9$ since $10! = 3628800$ far exceeds $e \times 10^6$. The partial sum $\sum_{n=0}^{9} \frac{1}{n!} = 2.7182818$ matches $e$ to seven decimal places, demonstrating the efficiency of factorial decay.

Failure modes in series convergence include misapplying tests beyond their hypotheses and neglecting boundary cases in power series. The ratio test requires positive terms or absolute values; applying it directly to an alternating series without absolute values yields incorrect conclusions. The integral test requires monotonicity and continuity; oscillating functions invalidate the comparison. Limit comparison demands positive terms and a nonzero finite limit; zero or infinite limits necessitate direct comparison instead. Power series endpoint analysis requires individual testing; the radius of convergence provides no information about convergence at $|x - a| = R$. Each endpoint may converge, diverge, or exhibit mixed behavior, demanding separate application of series convergence tests to the numerical series obtained by substituting the endpoint values.

The pathological case of conditionally convergent series underscores the necessity of absolute convergence for robust manipulation. Rearranging a conditionally convergent series can produce any desired sum or divergence, rendering term order significant. Multiplying two series via the Cauchy product $\left(\sum a_n\right)\left(\sum b_n\right) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left(\sum_{k=0}^{n} a_k b_{n-k}\right)$ requires at least one series to converge absolutely; if both converge conditionally, the product may diverge. The Mertens theorem provides a weaker guarantee: if one series converges absolutely and the other merely converges, the Cauchy product converges to the product of the sums. Absolute convergence eliminates these subtleties, allowing free rearrangement and multiplication as if the series were finite sums.

Advanced convergence tests like Raabe's test refine the ratio test near the boundary $L = 1$. When $\lim \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} = 1$, compute $\lim_{n \to \infty} n\left(1 - \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right) = \lim_{n \to \infty} n \frac{a_n - a_{n+1}}{a_n}$. If this limit exceeds one, the series converges; if less than one, it diverges. For the $p$-series $\sum \frac{1}{n^p}$, we have $\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} = \left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^p = \left(1 - \frac{1}{n+1}\right)^p \approx 1 - \frac{p}{n}$, so $n\left(1 - \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right) \approx p$. Thus convergence holds when $p > 1$ and divergence when $p < 1$, matching the known result. Raabe's test resolves cases where the ratio test is inconclusive, particularly for series with polynomial decay modulation.

The practical workflow for series convergence begins with a term inspection to identify structural features: exponential, factorial, polynomial, logarithmic factors. Exponential and factorial dominance in numerator or denominator suggests the ratio test. Polynomial expressions indicate limit comparison with $p$-series. Alternating signs with decreasing magnitude invoke the alternating series test. Monotonic terms defined by a decreasing function point to the integral test. Absolute convergence reduces to applying these tests to $\sum |a_n|$. Power series analysis computes the radius via the ratio or root test on coefficients, then separately tests endpoints by substitution. This hierarchical approach minimizes computational effort while ensuring rigorous verification of convergence behavior.
