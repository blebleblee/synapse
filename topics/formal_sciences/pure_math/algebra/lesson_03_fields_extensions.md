# Lesson 3: Fields, Extensions, Galois Theory

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The algebraic hierarchy from groups through rings culminates in the structure of fields, where every nonzero element possesses a multiplicative inverse and the interplay between addition and multiplication reaches its most refined form. A field is not merely a ring in which division is always possible—it is the algebraic arena where polynomial equations reveal their deepest symmetries, where the solvability of equations connects to the structure of permutation groups, and where finite arithmetic underpins modern cryptographic protocols.

The axiomatic foundation of a field $(F, +, \cdot)$ requires that $(F, +)$ form an abelian group with identity $0$, that $(F \setminus \{0\}, \cdot)$ form an abelian group with identity $1$, and that multiplication distribute over addition: $a \cdot (b + c) = a \cdot b + a \cdot c$ for all $a, b, c \in F$. This deceptively simple axiomatization encapsulates profound computational power. The rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$, real numbers $\mathbb{R}$, and complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$ all satisfy these axioms, as do the finite fields $\mathbb{F}_p$ for prime $p$, constructed as the integers modulo $p$ with arithmetic operations performed modulo $p$.

The concept of a field extension emerges from the question: given a field $F$ and a polynomial $f(x) \in F[x]$, can we construct a larger field $E$ containing $F$ in which $f(x)$ has a root? This question drives the entire edifice of Galois theory. If $F \subseteq E$ are fields with the operations of $E$ restricting to those of $F$, we call $E$ an extension field of $F$ and write $E/F$. The degree of the extension, denoted $[E:F]$, measures the dimension of $E$ as a vector space over $F$. When $[E:F] = n < \infty$, every element $\alpha \in E$ satisfies a polynomial equation of degree at most $n$ with coefficients in $F$, making $\alpha$ algebraic over $F$. Elements that satisfy no such polynomial equation are called transcendental over $F$—the canonical example being $\pi$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, whose transcendence was established by Lindemann in 1882, immediately implying the impossibility of squaring the circle with compass and straightedge.

The tower law for field extensions provides the fundamental dimensional calculus: if $F \subseteq K \subseteq E$ are fields, then $[E:F] = [E:K] \cdot [K:F]$. This multiplicative property governs all degree calculations and constrains the possible intermediate fields in an extension. For instance, since $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}) : \mathbb{Q}] = 4$, we immediately know that $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})$ cannot contain a subfield of degree $3$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, as $3$ does not divide $4$.

The minimal polynomial of an algebraic element $\alpha$ over $F$ is the unique monic irreducible polynomial $m(x) \in F[x]$ of smallest degree such that $m(\alpha) = 0$. This polynomial governs the arithmetic of $\alpha$: the quotient ring $F[x]/(m(x))$ is isomorphic to $F(\alpha)$, the smallest field containing both $F$ and $\alpha$. The degree of $m(x)$ equals $[F(\alpha):F]$, and every polynomial in $F[x]$ that vanishes at $\alpha$ is divisible by $m(x)$. Computing minimal polynomials is algorithmic: given $\alpha$ algebraic over $F$ of degree $n$, we construct the $F$-linear dependence relation among $\{1, \alpha, \alpha^2, \ldots, \alpha^n\}$ by solving a system of linear equations.

Splitting fields formalize the notion of the smallest field in which a polynomial factors completely into linear terms. Given $f(x) \in F[x]$, a splitting field for $f$ over $F$ is an extension $E/F$ such that $f(x) = c(x - \alpha_1)(x - \alpha_2) \cdots (x - \alpha_n)$ in $E[x]$ for some $c \in F$ and $\alpha_i \in E$, and $E = F(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \ldots, \alpha_n)$. The fundamental existence and uniqueness theorem states that splitting fields always exist and are unique up to $F$-isomorphism. This uniqueness, proven by induction on the degree of $f$, establishes that the splitting field construction is canonical—different construction sequences yield isomorphic results.

Finite fields, also called Galois fields, exhibit remarkable rigidity. For every prime power $q = p^n$, there exists a unique finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$ with $q$ elements, and every finite field has prime power order. The multiplicative group $\mathbb{F}_q^*$ is cyclic of order $q - 1$, so there exists a primitive element $g \in \mathbb{F}_q$ such that every nonzero element is a power of $g$. The additive group has characteristic $p$, meaning $p \cdot a = 0$ for all $a \in \mathbb{F}_q$. The field $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ is the splitting field of $x^{p^n} - x$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$, and its elements are precisely the roots of this polynomial. The Frobenius endomorphism $\phi : \mathbb{F}_{p^n} \to \mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ defined by $\phi(a) = a^p$ is a field automorphism whose fixed points are exactly the elements of $\mathbb{F}_p$, and iterating $\phi$ generates the Galois group $\text{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{p^n}/\mathbb{F}_p) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$.

The Galois group $\text{Gal}(E/F)$ of a field extension $E/F$ consists of all field automorphisms $\sigma : E \to E$ that fix $F$ pointwise: $\sigma(a) = a$ for all $a \in F$. This group measures the symmetry of the extension. For the splitting field of $x^2 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, the Galois group is $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, generated by the automorphism swapping $\sqrt{2}$ and $-\sqrt{2}$. For the splitting field of $x^3 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, adjoining a primitive cube root of unity $\omega$ and $\sqrt[3]{2}$, the Galois group is $S_3$, the symmetric group on three elements, reflecting the six permutations of the three roots $\{\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega^2\sqrt[3]{2}\}$.

An extension $E/F$ is Galois if it is both normal and separable. Normality requires that $E$ be the splitting field of some polynomial over $F$, ensuring that every irreducible polynomial in $F[x]$ with a root in $E$ splits completely in $E$. Separability requires that every element of $E$ satisfy a polynomial over $F$ with distinct roots, a condition automatically satisfied in characteristic zero but requiring care in positive characteristic. The Galois correspondence, the crowning achievement of Galois theory, establishes a bijection between intermediate fields $F \subseteq K \subseteq E$ and subgroups $H \subseteq \text{Gal}(E/F)$, given by $K \mapsto \text{Gal}(E/K)$ and $H \mapsto E^H = \{a \in E : \sigma(a) = a \text{ for all } \sigma \in H\}$. This correspondence reverses inclusions: larger intermediate fields correspond to smaller subgroups. Moreover, the intermediate field $K$ is a Galois extension of $F$ if and only if the corresponding subgroup $\text{Gal}(E/K)$ is normal in $\text{Gal}(E/F)$, in which case $\text{Gal}(K/F) \cong \text{Gal}(E/F)/\text{Gal}(E/K)$.

The insolvability of the quintic polynomial follows from this machinery. A polynomial $f(x) \in F[x]$ is solvable by radicals if its roots can be expressed using arithmetic operations and $n$-th roots starting from elements of $F$. Galois showed that $f$ is solvable by radicals if and only if the Galois group of its splitting field is a solvable group, meaning it has a composition series with abelian quotients. The general quintic $x^5 + ax^4 + bx^3 + cx^2 + dx + e$ has splitting field with Galois group $S_5$ over $\mathbb{Q}(a, b, c, d, e)$, and $S_5$ is not solvable because its composition series contains the simple group $A_5$, which has no nontrivial normal subgroups. Therefore, no formula analogous to the quadratic formula exists for the general quintic.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The internal structure of field extensions rests on the interplay between algebraic and transcendental elements, polynomial arithmetic in quotient rings, and the tower of fields connecting the base field to the splitting field. We dissect these mechanisms through explicit constructions and computational procedures.

### Field Extension Construction via Polynomial Adjoining

Given a field $F$ and an irreducible polynomial $p(x) \in F[x]$ of degree $n$, the quotient ring $F[x]/(p(x))$ is a field of dimension $n$ over $F$. Every element of this field has a unique representative as a polynomial of degree less than $n$. Arithmetic operations reduce modulo $p(x)$: addition is componentwise, and multiplication is polynomial multiplication followed by reduction. If $\alpha$ denotes the image of $x$ in the quotient, then $p(\alpha) = 0$, and $F(\alpha) \cong F[x]/(p(x))$ as $F$-algebras.

For example, construct $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ by adjoining a root of $p(x) = x^2 - 2$, which is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ by Eisenstein's criterion with prime $p = 2$. Elements of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ have the form $a + b\sqrt{2}$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$. Addition is componentwise: $(a_1 + b_1\sqrt{2}) + (a_2 + b_2\sqrt{2}) = (a_1 + a_2) + (b_1 + b_2)\sqrt{2}$. Multiplication uses the relation $(\sqrt{2})^2 = 2$:

$$(a_1 + b_1\sqrt{2})(a_2 + b_2\sqrt{2}) = a_1 a_2 + a_1 b_2 \sqrt{2} + b_1 a_2 \sqrt{2} + b_1 b_2 \cdot 2 = (a_1 a_2 + 2b_1 b_2) + (a_1 b_2 + b_1 a_2)\sqrt{2}.$$

The multiplicative inverse of $a + b\sqrt{2}$ (assuming $a + b\sqrt{2} \neq 0$) is computed by rationalizing the denominator:

$$\frac{1}{a + b\sqrt{2}} = \frac{a - b\sqrt{2}}{(a + b\sqrt{2})(a - b\sqrt{2})} = \frac{a - b\sqrt{2}}{a^2 - 2b^2} = \frac{a}{a^2 - 2b^2} - \frac{b}{a^2 - 2b^2}\sqrt{2}.$$

The norm map $N : \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) \to \mathbb{Q}$ defined by $N(a + b\sqrt{2}) = a^2 - 2b^2$ satisfies $N(\alpha\beta) = N(\alpha)N(\beta)$, providing the algebraic tool for computing inverses.

### Minimal Polynomial Computation via Linear Dependence

Given $\alpha$ algebraic over $F$, compute its minimal polynomial by finding the smallest linear dependence among powers of $\alpha$. Suppose $\alpha \in E$ where $[E:F] = n$. The elements $1, \alpha, \alpha^2, \ldots, \alpha^n$ are linearly dependent over $F$, so there exist $c_0, c_1, \ldots, c_n \in F$, not all zero, such that:

$$c_0 + c_1 \alpha + c_2 \alpha^2 + \cdots + c_n \alpha^n = 0.$$

The minimal polynomial is the monic polynomial of smallest degree in this linear dependence. Algorithmically, we construct the matrix whose columns are the coordinate vectors of $1, \alpha, \alpha^2, \ldots, \alpha^n$ with respect to an $F$-basis of $E$, then compute the kernel. The smallest-degree kernel element, after scaling to be monic, is the minimal polynomial.

For example, let $\alpha = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. We compute powers:

$$\alpha = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3},$$
$$\alpha^2 = 2 + 2\sqrt{6} + 3 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6},$$
$$\alpha^3 = \alpha \cdot \alpha^2 = (\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3})(5 + 2\sqrt{6}) = 5\sqrt{2} + 2\sqrt{12} + 5\sqrt{3} + 2\sqrt{18} = 5\sqrt{2} + 4\sqrt{3} + 5\sqrt{3} + 6\sqrt{2} = 11\sqrt{2} + 9\sqrt{3},$$
$$\alpha^4 = \alpha \cdot \alpha^3 = (\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3})(11\sqrt{2} + 9\sqrt{3}) = 22 + 11\sqrt{6} + 9\sqrt{6} + 27 = 49 + 20\sqrt{6}.$$

Since $\alpha^2 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6}$ and $\alpha^4 = 49 + 20\sqrt{6}$, we see that $\alpha^2 - 5 = 2\sqrt{6}$, so $(\alpha^2 - 5)^2 = 24$, yielding $\alpha^4 - 10\alpha^2 + 25 = 24$, thus $\alpha^4 - 10\alpha^2 + 1 = 0$. The minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $m(x) = x^4 - 10x^2 + 1$, which is irreducible by checking that it has no rational roots and is irreducible modulo $2$ or $3$.

### Splitting Field Construction and Factorization Sequences

To construct the splitting field of $f(x) \in F[x]$, we adjoin roots iteratively. Let $f(x) = f_0(x)$. Find an irreducible factor $p_1(x)$ of $f_0(x)$ in $F[x]$ and form $F_1 = F[x]/(p_1(x))$, adjoining a root $\alpha_1$. In $F_1[x]$, factor $f(x) = (x - \alpha_1) f_1(x)$. Repeat: find an irreducible factor $p_2(x)$ of $f_1(x)$ in $F_1[x]$, form $F_2 = F_1[x]/(p_2(x))$, adjoin $\alpha_2$, and continue until $f(x)$ factors completely into linear terms in $F_n[x]$. Then $F_n$ is the splitting field, and $F_n = F(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \ldots, \alpha_n)$.

For example, construct the splitting field of $f(x) = x^3 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. The polynomial is irreducible by Eisenstein with $p = 2$. Adjoin $\alpha = \sqrt[3]{2}$, forming $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ with $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$. In $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)[x]$, we have $x^3 - 2 = (x - \alpha)(x^2 + \alpha x + \alpha^2)$. The quadratic $x^2 + \alpha x + \alpha^2$ has discriminant $\alpha^2 - 4\alpha^2 = -3\alpha^2 = -3 \cdot 2^{2/3}$, which is not a square in $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ (as $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and the discriminant is negative). To adjoin the other roots, we need a primitive cube root of unity $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3} = (-1 + i\sqrt{3})/2$, which satisfies $\omega^2 + \omega + 1 = 0$. The roots of $x^3 - 2$ are $\alpha, \omega\alpha, \omega^2\alpha$. The splitting field is $E = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha, \omega)$, and since $[\mathbb{Q}(\omega):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$ (minimal polynomial $x^2 + x + 1$) and $\alpha \notin \mathbb{Q}(\omega)$ (as $\mathbb{Q}(\omega) \subseteq \mathbb{C}$ but $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$), we have $[E:\mathbb{Q}(\omega)] = 3$, so $[E:\mathbb{Q}] = 6$ by the tower law.

### Finite Field Arithmetic and the Frobenius Endomorphism

Finite fields $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ are constructed as splitting fields of $x^{p^n} - x$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$, or equivalently as $\mathbb{F}_p[x]/(f(x))$ where $f(x)$ is any irreducible polynomial of degree $n$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$. Addition and multiplication are performed modulo $p$ and modulo $f(x)$. The Frobenius map $\phi : a \mapsto a^p$ is an automorphism because $(a + b)^p = a^p + b^p$ in characteristic $p$ (the binomial coefficients $\binom{p}{k}$ are divisible by $p$ for $0 < k < p$), and $(ab)^p = a^p b^p$ always holds. The Frobenius map generates $\text{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{p^n}/\mathbb{F}_p)$, which is cyclic of order $n$, because $\phi^n = \text{id}$ (as $a^{p^n} = a$ for all $a \in \mathbb{F}_{p^n}$) and $\phi$ has no fixed points outside $\mathbb{F}_p$.

For example, construct $\mathbb{F}_4 = \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^2 + x + 1)$. The polynomial $x^2 + x + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{F}_2$ (it has no roots in $\mathbb{F}_2$). Let $\alpha$ denote the image of $x$, so $\alpha^2 + \alpha + 1 = 0$, thus $\alpha^2 = \alpha + 1$. The elements of $\mathbb{F}_4$ are $\{0, 1, \alpha, \alpha + 1\}$. The Frobenius map satisfies $\phi(0) = 0$, $\phi(1) = 1$, $\phi(\alpha) = \alpha^2 = \alpha + 1$, and $\phi(\alpha + 1) = (\alpha + 1)^2 = \alpha^2 + 2\alpha + 1 = \alpha^2 + 1 = (\alpha + 1) + 1 = \alpha$ (using $2 = 0$ in $\mathbb{F}_2$). Thus $\phi$ has order $2$, and $\text{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_4/\mathbb{F}_2) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

The multiplicative group $\mathbb{F}_4^* = \{1, \alpha, \alpha + 1\}$ is cyclic of order $3$. Both $\alpha$ and $\alpha + 1$ are primitive elements. We verify: $\alpha^1 = \alpha$, $\alpha^2 = \alpha + 1$, $\alpha^3 = \alpha \cdot \alpha^2 = \alpha(\alpha + 1) = \alpha^2 + \alpha = (\alpha + 1) + \alpha = 1$. Thus $\alpha$ generates $\mathbb{F}_4^*$.

### Galois Group Computation via Automorphism Determination

To compute $\text{Gal}(E/F)$ where $E = F(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n)$ is the splitting field of $f(x) \in F[x]$, we determine all $F$-automorphisms of $E$. An $F$-automorphism $\sigma$ permutes the roots of $f$, as $\sigma(f(\alpha_i)) = f(\sigma(\alpha_i)) = 0$ implies $\sigma(\alpha_i)$ is also a root. Conversely, any permutation of roots that respects algebraic relations extends to an automorphism. We enumerate possibilities by specifying $\sigma(\alpha_1), \sigma(\alpha_2), \ldots, \sigma(\alpha_n)$ and checking consistency.

For example, compute $\text{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})/\mathbb{Q})$. The extension is the splitting field of $(x^2 - 2)(x^2 - 3)$. An automorphism $\sigma$ must map $\sqrt{2}$ to $\pm\sqrt{2}$ (the roots of $x^2 - 2$) and $\sqrt{3}$ to $\pm\sqrt{3}$ (the roots of $x^2 - 3$). There are four possibilities:

$$\sigma_1: \sqrt{2} \mapsto \sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3} \mapsto \sqrt{3} \quad (\text{identity}),$$
$$\sigma_2: \sqrt{2} \mapsto \sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3} \mapsto -\sqrt{3},$$
$$\sigma_3: \sqrt{2} \mapsto -\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3} \mapsto \sqrt{3},$$
$$\sigma_4: \sqrt{2} \mapsto -\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3} \mapsto -\sqrt{3}.$$

Each of these extends to an automorphism of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})$. We verify that $\sigma_2$ and $\sigma_3$ both have order $2$, and $\sigma_4 = \sigma_2 \circ \sigma_3 = \sigma_3 \circ \sigma_2$, so the Galois group is isomorphic to the Klein four-group $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

### The Galois Correspondence Diagram

For a Galois extension $E/F$ with Galois group $G = \text{Gal}(E/F)$, the Galois correspondence establishes the following commutative diagram structure. Given subgroups $H_1, H_2 \subseteq G$, we have $H_1 \subseteq H_2$ if and only if $E^{H_2} \subseteq E^{H_1}$. The degree formula relates intermediate fields to subgroup indices: $[E:E^H] = |H|$ and $[E^H:F] = |G:H| = |G|/|H|$. For example, if $|G| = 12$ and $H$ is a subgroup of order $4$, then $[E:E^H] = 4$ and $[E^H:F] = 3$.

The correspondence preserves normal subgroups: $K/F$ is Galois if and only if $\text{Gal}(E/K) \trianglelefteq G$, in which case $\text{Gal}(K/F) \cong G/\text{Gal}(E/K)$. This quotient isomorphism is the restriction homomorphism: every $\sigma \in G$ restricts to an element of $\text{Gal}(K/F)$, and the kernel of this restriction is precisely $\text{Gal}(E/K)$.

Consider the extension $E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}, i)$ over $F = \mathbb{Q}$, which is the splitting field of $x^4 - 2$. The roots are $\{\sqrt[4]{2}, i\sqrt[4]{2}, -\sqrt[4]{2}, -i\sqrt[4]{2}\}$. An automorphism $\sigma$ maps $\sqrt[4]{2}$ to one of the four roots and $i$ to $\pm i$. However, $\sigma(\sqrt[4]{2})$ must be a root of $x^4 - 2$, and $\sigma$ must preserve the relation $(\sqrt[4]{2})^4 = 2$. Let $\alpha = \sqrt[4]{2}$. Then $\sigma(\alpha) \in \{\alpha, i\alpha, -\alpha, -i\alpha\}$. Since $i^2 = -1$ and $\sigma(i)^2 = -1$, we have $\sigma(i) \in \{i, -i\}$. The Galois group has order $8$: it is generated by $\tau : \alpha \mapsto i\alpha, i \mapsto i$ (rotation of roots) and $\rho : \alpha \mapsto \alpha, i \mapsto -i$ (complex conjugation). We compute $\tau^4 = \text{id}$, $\rho^2 = \text{id}$, and $\rho \tau \rho = \tau^{-1}$ (as $\rho(i\alpha) = -i\alpha = i^{-1}\alpha$). Thus $G \cong D_4$, the dihedral group of order $8$.

The subgroups of $D_4$ correspond to intermediate fields. The subgroup $H = \langle \tau^2 \rangle = \{\text{id}, \tau^2\}$ has order $2$, so $E^H$ has degree $2$ over $F$. We compute $\tau^2(\alpha) = \tau(i\alpha) = i^2\alpha = -\alpha$, so $\tau^2$ negates $\alpha$ but fixes $\alpha^2 = \sqrt{2}$ and $i$. Thus $E^H = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, i)$, which indeed has degree $2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ (as $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, i):\mathbb{Q}] = 4$ and $[E:\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, i)] = 2$). The subgroup $\langle \rho \rangle$ has order $2$, and $E^{\langle \rho \rangle} = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2})$, the real subfield of degree $4$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

The practical computation of Galois groups, splitting fields, and minimal polynomials in large-degree extensions demands algorithmic efficiency and awareness of degeneracies. We explore computational strategies, resource scaling, and edge cases where naive approaches fail.

### Algorithmic Complexity in Polynomial Factorization

Splitting field construction requires polynomial factorization over successively larger fields. Factoring a polynomial of degree $n$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ has worst-case complexity exponential in $n$ using classical algorithms, but modern implementations use Berlekamp's algorithm for factorization over finite fields (polynomial time in degree and field size) and Lenstra-Lenstra-Lovász (LLL) lattice reduction for factorization over $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$ (polynomial time in practical cases, though not proven polynomial in worst case).

For finite fields, Berlekamp's algorithm computes the kernel of the matrix $B - I$ where $B_{ij}$ is the coefficient of $x^{pi}$ in $x^{pj} \mod f(x)$, with $f(x)$ the polynomial to factor over $\mathbb{F}_p$. The dimension of the kernel equals the number of irreducible factors. Cantor-Zassenhaus probabilistic splitting refines this by using random polynomials to separate factors. For $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$, distinct-degree factorization first separates $f(x)$ into parts whose irreducible factors have degrees $d = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ by computing $\gcd(f(x), x^{p^d} - x)$ for each $d$.

Over $\mathbb{Q}$, we first factor modulo several small primes to obtain candidate factor degrees, then use Hensel lifting to lift factorizations from $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ to $\mathbb{Z}/p^k\mathbb{Z}$ for increasing $k$, and finally apply LLL to find short vectors in lattices encoding potential factors. The LLL algorithm runs in polynomial time in the bit length of input, but the bit length of coefficients in intermediate computations can grow, so careful coefficient control is necessary.

When computing Galois groups, we avoid enumerating all permutations by using resolvent polynomials and Galois group databases. For a polynomial $f(x)$ of degree $n$, the Galois group is a transitive subgroup of $S_n$. Resolvent polynomials distinguish between possible Galois groups: for example, the discriminant $\Delta(f) = \prod_{i < j} (\alpha_i - \alpha_j)^2$ is a square in $F$ if and only if the Galois group is contained in $A_n$. Higher resolvents involve symmetric functions of roots that are invariant under specific subgroups, allowing systematic determination of $\text{Gal}(f)$ by computing a sequence of resolvents and checking whether they have roots in $F$.

### Memory and Precision Management in Symbolic Computation

Field extensions over $\mathbb{Q}$ require exact rational arithmetic and symbolic representation of algebraic numbers. Representing an element $\alpha$ in $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_k)$ as a polynomial in the $\alpha_i$ with rational coefficients leads to exponential growth in coefficient bit length under repeated arithmetic operations. To mitigate this, modern computer algebra systems use modular arithmetic and Chinese remainder theorem reconstruction: perform computations modulo several primes, then reconstruct the rational result.

For example, to compute $(a + b\sqrt{2})^{100}$ exactly, we expand binomially, but the coefficients grow rapidly. Instead, compute $(a + b\sqrt{2})^{100} \mod p_1$, $(a + b\sqrt{2})^{100} \mod p_2$, $\ldots$, for distinct primes $p_i$, then use CRT to reconstruct the result modulo $\prod p_i$. If the product exceeds the bit length of the true coefficients (estimated a priori or checked via a Schwarz-Zippel probabilistic bound), the reconstruction is correct.

Another strategy is to use minimal polynomial representation: store $\alpha$ as the triple $(m(x), r(x), n)$ where $m(x)$ is the minimal polynomial, $r(x)$ is the representative of $\alpha$ in $\mathbb{Q}[x]/(m(x))$, and $n$ is a numerical approximation for fast magnitude checks. Arithmetic operations reduce modulo $m(x)$, and the numerical approximation accelerates comparison and sign determination without exact computation.

### Degenerate Cases in Galois Correspondence

The Galois correspondence requires the extension to be Galois, meaning normal and separable. Extensions that fail separability exhibit pathologies: if $\alpha$ is algebraic over $F$ with minimal polynomial $m(x)$ having repeated roots, then $F(\alpha)$ is not separable over $F$. This occurs only in characteristic $p > 0$, where inseparability arises from the Frobenius map. For example, in $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$ where $t$ is transcendental, the polynomial $x^p - t$ is irreducible but has a single root $\alpha$ of multiplicity $p$ in the splitting field, as $x^p - t = (x - \alpha)^p$ in characteristic $p$.

For non-normal extensions, the Galois group is trivial or smaller than expected. For instance, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is not normal because the minimal polynomial $x^3 - 2$ has only one real root in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$, while the other two complex roots lie outside. The automorphism group is trivial: the only $\mathbb{Q}$-automorphism is the identity. To restore normality, we must adjoin all roots, forming the splitting field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$, which is Galois with group $S_3$.

Computing fixed fields $E^H$ for a subgroup $H \subseteq \text{Gal}(E/F)$ algorithmically requires finding elements $\beta \in E$ such that $\sigma(\beta) = \beta$ for all $\sigma \in H$. If $E = F(\alpha)$ is a simple extension, we search for polynomials $p(\alpha)$ invariant under $H$. For example, if $H = \{\text{id}, \tau\}$ with $\tau(\alpha) = -\alpha$, then $\alpha^2$ is invariant, so $E^H = F(\alpha^2)$. For non-simple extensions, we use linear algebra: represent $E$ as an $F$-vector space, and find the subspace of elements $\beta$ satisfying $\sigma(\beta) - \beta = 0$ for all $\sigma \in H$. This is the intersection of the kernels of the linear maps $\sigma - \text{id}$.

### Boundary Cases in Finite Field Construction

For large primes $p$ and degrees $n$, constructing $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ requires finding an irreducible polynomial of degree $n$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$. The density of irreducible polynomials of degree $n$ is approximately $1/n$ (by Gauss, the number of monic irreducible polynomials of degree $n$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$ is $\frac{1}{n} \sum_{d|n} \mu(n/d) p^d$, which is asymptotically $p^n/n$). A random polynomial of degree $n$ is irreducible with probability roughly $1/n$, so probabilistic search succeeds quickly. However, for specific applications (e.g., cryptographic standards), deterministic constructions are preferred. The Conway polynomial is a canonical choice of irreducible polynomial for each $(p, n)$, defined by a lexicographic ordering criterion that ensures compatibility across different degrees.

For $\mathbb{F}_{2^n}$, irreducible trinomials $x^n + x^k + 1$ are often used for efficiency in hardware implementation, as multiplication modulo a trinomial requires fewer XOR operations than a general polynomial. However, irreducible trinomials do not exist for all $n$. For example, there is no irreducible trinomial of degree $n = 8$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$, so AES uses the irreducible polynomial $x^8 + x^4 + x^3 + x + 1$ (a pentanomial) to define $\mathbb{F}_{256}$.

Frobenius endomorphism iteration for solving $x^{p^n} = a$ in $\mathbb{F}_{p^m}$ (when $n | m$) uses the fact that the solutions form a subfield $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$. If $\gcd(n, m) = d$, then $|\{x \in \mathbb{F}_{p^m} : x^{p^n} = x\}| = p^d$, the size of $\mathbb{F}_{p^d}$. To test whether $a \in \mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ for $n | m$, compute $a^{p^n}$ by repeated Frobenius and check equality with $a$. This costs $n$ Frobenius operations, each being a polynomial exponentiation modulo the defining irreducible polynomial.

### Solvability by Radicals and Radical Towers

To determine whether a polynomial $f(x) \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ is solvable by radicals, compute the Galois group of its splitting field and test whether it is solvable. A group $G$ is solvable if it has a composition series $\{e\} = G_0 \trianglelefteq G_1 \trianglelefteq \cdots \trianglelefteq G_k = G$ with each quotient $G_{i+1}/G_i$ abelian. Equivalently, the derived series $G^{(0)} = G$, $G^{(i+1)} = [G^{(i)}, G^{(i)}]$ (the commutator subgroup) reaches $\{e\}$ in finitely many steps.

For polynomials of degree $\leq 4$, all Galois groups are solvable: $S_2, A_3 \cong S_3/A_3$ (solvable), $S_4$ has composition series $\{e\} \trianglelefteq V_4 \trianglelefteq A_4 \trianglelefteq S_4$ with abelian quotients $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, so $S_4$ is solvable. For degree $5$, the alternating group $A_5$ is simple (no nontrivial normal subgroups), so $S_5 \supseteq A_5$ is not solvable. Generic quintics have Galois group $S_5$, hence are not solvable by radicals.

For specific quintics, the Galois group may be smaller. The polynomial $x^5 - x - 1$ has Galois group $S_5$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ (verified by showing its discriminant is not a square and it is irreducible modulo $2$), so it is not solvable by radicals. The polynomial $x^5 - 5x + 5$ has Galois group $F_5$, the Frobenius group of order $20$, which is solvable (composition series $\{e\} \trianglelefteq C_5 \trianglelefteq F_5$ with quotient $C_4$), so its roots can be expressed using radicals, though the formulas are complicated.

Radical tower construction for solvable Galois groups proceeds by adjoining $n$-th roots layer by layer, following the composition series. If $G$ has a normal subgroup $N$ with $G/N$ cyclic of order $m$, then the fixed field $K = E^N$ satisfies $\text{Gal}(K/F) \cong \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$, so $K = F(\alpha)$ where $\alpha^m \in F$, a radical extension. Iterating this for each step in the composition series produces a radical tower.

### Computational Verification of the Fundamental Theorem

To verify the Galois correspondence computationally, we construct explicit intermediate fields and subgroups and check the bijection. For $E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})$ with $G \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, the subgroups of order $2$ are $\langle \sigma_2 \rangle, \langle \sigma_3 \rangle, \langle \sigma_4 \rangle$. The fixed field of $\langle \sigma_2 \rangle$ (where $\sigma_2$ negates $\sqrt{3}$) consists of elements $a + b\sqrt{2}$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$, so $E^{\langle \sigma_2 \rangle} = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$. Similarly, $E^{\langle \sigma_3 \rangle} = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})$ and $E^{\langle \sigma_4 \rangle} = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{6})$ (as $\sigma_4$ negates both $\sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{3}$, so it fixes $\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{3} = \sqrt{6}$).

We verify dimensions: $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 2 = |G|/|\langle \sigma_2 \rangle| = 4/2$. The lattice of subgroups matches the lattice of intermediate fields under inclusion-reversing correspondence. The trivial subgroup $\{e\}$ corresponds to $E$ itself, and the whole group $G$ corresponds to $F = \mathbb{Q}$.

For non-Galois extensions, the correspondence fails. In $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$, the Galois group is trivial, but there are no nontrivial intermediate fields, so the correspondence is vacuous. To apply Galois theory, we first pass to the Galois closure, the splitting field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$, and analyze the correspondence there. The intermediate field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ corresponds to a subgroup of order $2$ in $S_3$, and the three such subgroups correspond to $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$, $\mathbb{Q}(\omega\sqrt[3]{2})$, and $\mathbb{Q}(\omega^2\sqrt[3]{2})$, the three conjugate subfields.

The Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory asserts that for $E/F$ Galois with group $G$, the maps $K \mapsto \text{Gal}(E/K)$ and $H \mapsto E^H$ are inverse bijections between intermediate fields and subgroups of $G$. Verifying this in practice requires computing both directions and checking that compositions are identity maps: $E^{\text{Gal}(E/K)} = K$ and $\text{Gal}(E/E^H) = H$. The first equality follows from the linear independence of characters: if $\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_n$ are distinct automorphisms, then they are linearly independent as functions $E \to E$. The second equality uses the orbit-counting formula: $|H| = [E:E^H]$ and $|\text{Gal}(E/E^H)| = [E:E^H]$, so $H = \text{Gal}(E/E^H)$ by cardinality and containment.

This completes the mechanical dissection of field theory and Galois theory, establishing the rigorous framework for polynomial solvability, algebraic number theory, and cryptographic field arithmetic.
