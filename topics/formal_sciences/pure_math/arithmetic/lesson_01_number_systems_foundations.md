# Lesson 1: Number Systems Foundations

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The axiomatic construction of number systems represents one of the most profound achievements in pure mathematics, transforming intuitive counting operations into rigorous logical structures. Where ancient civilizations manipulated numbers through geometric and algorithmic procedures, modern mathematics demands that we construct each number system from foundational axioms, prove that arithmetic operations satisfy precise algebraic properties, and establish the completeness theorems that distinguish real numbers from rationals. This lesson dissects the hierarchical tower of number systems—natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$, integers $\mathbb{Z}$, rationals $\mathbb{Q}$, reals $\mathbb{R}$, and complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$—through explicit constructions, field axioms, ordering properties, and closure theorems.

The natural numbers emerge from the Peano axioms, a minimal set of logical statements that characterize the structure of counting through successor operations and mathematical induction. Giuseppe Peano formalized these axioms in 1889, providing the first rigorous foundation for arithmetic that eliminated reliance on geometric intuition. The Peano axioms stipulate the existence of a distinguished element zero, a successor function that generates each natural number from its predecessor, and an induction principle that allows proof by recursive argument. From these sparse axioms, we derive addition and multiplication through recursive definitions, prove their associative and commutative properties, and establish the well-ordering principle that every nonempty subset of natural numbers contains a least element.

The integers extend the natural numbers by introducing additive inverses, solving the equation $x + a = 0$ for every natural number $a$. This construction proceeds through equivalence classes of ordered pairs, where the pair $(a, b)$ represents the formal difference $a - b$. Two pairs $(a, b)$ and $(c, d)$ are equivalent if $a + d = b + c$, a definition that circumvents the circularity of defining subtraction before integers exist. The quotient set $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N} / \sim$ inherits addition and multiplication from the natural numbers, and the resulting structure $\mathbb{Z}$ forms a commutative ring with unity: addition yields an abelian group, multiplication distributes over addition, and the multiplicative identity one exists. Yet integers lack multiplicative inverses for nonzero elements, motivating the construction of rational numbers.

Rational numbers emerge by forming the field of fractions of the integral domain $\mathbb{Z}$, solving equations of the form $bx = a$ for nonzero integers $b$. We construct $\mathbb{Q}$ as equivalence classes of ordered pairs $(a, b)$ where $b \neq 0$, with $(a, b) \sim (c, d)$ if and only if $ad = bc$. This equivalence relation captures the fundamental property of fractions: $\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}$ precisely when cross-multiplication yields equality. Arithmetic operations on $\mathbb{Q}$ follow the familiar fraction rules, and the resulting structure satisfies all field axioms. Every nonzero rational possesses a multiplicative inverse $\frac{b}{a}$ when $\frac{a}{b}$ is given, closure under addition and multiplication holds, and the distributive law $a(b + c) = ab + ac$ governs the interaction between operations.

The rationals, however, exhibit a critical incompleteness: they contain gaps corresponding to irrational numbers like $\sqrt{2}$. The Pythagorean discovery that no rational number satisfies $x^2 = 2$ revealed the first crisis in Greek mathematics, forcing the abandonment of the belief that all magnitudes are commensurable. A rigorous proof proceeds by contradiction: assume $\sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q}$ where $p$ and $q$ are coprime integers. Squaring both sides yields $2q^2 = p^2$, implying $p^2$ is even, hence $p$ itself is even. Write $p = 2k$ for some integer $k$, substitute into the equation to obtain $2q^2 = 4k^2$, simplify to $q^2 = 2k^2$, and conclude that $q^2$ is even, hence $q$ is even. This contradicts the assumption that $p$ and $q$ share no common factors, completing the proof. The existence of irrational numbers forces the construction of the real numbers, a system complete with respect to Cauchy sequences and Dedekind cuts.

The real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ are constructed through either Dedekind cuts or equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences of rationals, both approaches yielding an ordered field satisfying the completeness axiom. A Dedekind cut partitions $\mathbb{Q}$ into two nonempty sets $A$ and $B$ such that every element of $A$ is less than every element of $B$, and $A$ contains no greatest element. Each cut represents a unique real number: cuts corresponding to rationals have the form $A = \{x \in \mathbb{Q} : x < r\}$ for some $r \in \mathbb{Q}$, while irrational cuts lack a rational boundary. The alternative Cauchy sequence construction defines a real number as an equivalence class of rational sequences $(a_n)$ satisfying the Cauchy criterion: for every $\epsilon > 0$, there exists $N$ such that $|a_m - a_n| < \epsilon$ whenever $m, n > N$. Two Cauchy sequences are equivalent if their difference converges to zero, and the quotient space $\mathbb{R} = \text{Cauchy}(\mathbb{Q}) / \sim$ inherits field operations from $\mathbb{Q}$ through componentwise limits.

The completeness axiom distinguishes $\mathbb{R}$ from $\mathbb{Q}$ and provides the foundation for real analysis. Every nonempty subset of $\mathbb{R}$ that is bounded above possesses a least upper bound (supremum), and dually, every nonempty subset bounded below possesses a greatest lower bound (infimum). This least upper bound property implies the convergence of Cauchy sequences, the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem asserting that every bounded sequence contains a convergent subsequence, and the Heine-Borel theorem characterizing compact subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ as closed and bounded sets. Completeness also underlies the intermediate value theorem: if $f : [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous and $f(a) < 0 < f(b)$, then there exists $c \in (a, b)$ such that $f(c) = 0$. This theorem fails over $\mathbb{Q}$ because the function $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ satisfies $f(1) < 0 < f(2)$ but possesses no rational root.

Complex numbers extend the reals by introducing a square root of negative one, solving polynomial equations that lack real solutions. The complex field $\mathbb{C}$ consists of ordered pairs $(a, b)$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, where addition is componentwise and multiplication is defined by $(a, b)(c, d) = (ac - bd, ad + bc)$. This multiplication rule ensures that $(0, 1)^2 = (-1, 0)$, identifying $(0, 1)$ with the imaginary unit $i$ and justifying the familiar notation $a + bi$. The complex numbers form a field: every nonzero element $z = a + bi$ possesses a multiplicative inverse $z^{-1} = \frac{a - bi}{a^2 + b^2}$, derived by multiplying numerator and denominator of $\frac{1}{z}$ by the complex conjugate $\bar{z} = a - bi$. The modulus $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ satisfies the triangle inequality $|z + w| \leq |z| + |w|$ and the multiplicative property $|zw| = |z||w|$, establishing $\mathbb{C}$ as a complete metric space under the distance function $d(z, w) = |z - w|$.

The fundamental theorem of algebra asserts that every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients possesses at least one complex root, equivalently, that $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed. This theorem admits several proofs: Gauss's original argument used geometry and continuity, Liouville's proof invoked complex analysis and the fact that bounded entire functions are constant, and algebraic proofs exist using field theory and Galois extensions. A constructive proof proceeds by induction on degree: for a polynomial $p(z) = a_n z^n + \cdots + a_1 z + a_0$ with $a_n \neq 0$, consider the minimum value of $|p(z)|$ as $z$ ranges over $\mathbb{C}$. The function $|p(z)|$ tends to infinity as $|z| \to \infty$, hence achieves its minimum at some point $z_0 \in \mathbb{C}$. If $p(z_0) \neq 0$, we construct a nearby point $z_1$ with $|p(z_1)| < |p(z_0)|$ by Taylor expansion and choosing $z_1 - z_0$ to align with the dominant term, yielding a contradiction. Thus $p(z_0) = 0$, and factoring out $(z - z_0)$ reduces the degree, completing the induction.

The algebraic closure of $\mathbb{C}$ implies that every polynomial of degree $n$ possesses exactly $n$ complex roots, counted with multiplicity. Factoring polynomials over $\mathbb{C}$ reduces to finding roots and applying the factor theorem: if $p(\alpha) = 0$, then $(z - \alpha)$ divides $p(z)$. Over the reals, polynomials factor into linear and irreducible quadratic terms: every real polynomial of odd degree has at least one real root by the intermediate value theorem, and complex roots occur in conjugate pairs $\alpha$ and $\bar{\alpha}$ since coefficients are real. The product $(z - \alpha)(z - \bar{\alpha}) = z^2 - 2\text{Re}(\alpha)z + |\alpha|^2$ yields a quadratic polynomial with real coefficients, establishing the complete factorization over $\mathbb{R}$.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The architectural hierarchy of number systems follows a universal construction pattern: each extension introduces new elements to solve previously unsolvable equations while preserving the algebraic structure of the base system through embeddings and compatibility axioms. The natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$ embed into the integers $\mathbb{Z}$ via the identification $n \mapsto (n, 0)$ in the equivalence class construction, the integers embed into the rationals $\mathbb{Q}$ through $m \mapsto \frac{m}{1}$, the rationals embed into the reals $\mathbb{R}$ as equivalence classes of constant Cauchy sequences, and the reals embed into the complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$ through $x \mapsto (x, 0)$. Each embedding is injective and preserves addition and multiplication, ensuring that the arithmetic of smaller systems remains valid in larger ones. This tower exhibits strict containment:

$$\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R} \subset \mathbb{C}$$

The Peano axioms provide the foundational layer, stating that $\mathbb{N}$ consists of a set equipped with a distinguished element $0$ and a successor function $S : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ satisfying three conditions. First, zero is not the successor of any natural number: for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $S(n) \neq 0$. Second, the successor function is injective: if $S(m) = S(n)$, then $m = n$. Third, the induction axiom holds: if a property $P$ satisfies $P(0)$ and $P(n) \implies P(S(n))$ for all $n$, then $P(n)$ holds for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$. From these axioms, addition is defined recursively by $m + 0 = m$ and $m + S(n) = S(m + n)$, multiplication by $m \cdot 0 = 0$ and $m \cdot S(n) = m \cdot n + m$. Proving associativity of addition requires induction on the third variable: the base case $a + (b + 0) = a + b = (a + b) + 0$ is trivial, and the inductive step assumes $a + (b + n) = (a + b) + n$ and derives $a + (b + S(n)) = a + S(b + n) = S(a + (b + n)) = S((a + b) + n) = (a + b) + S(n)$ using the recursive definition and inductive hypothesis.

The construction of $\mathbb{Z}$ from $\mathbb{N}$ follows the general principle of forming a Grothendieck group, the universal solution to adding inverses to a commutative monoid. Given the additive monoid $(\mathbb{N}, +, 0)$, we form the Cartesian product $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ and define an equivalence relation $(a, b) \sim (c, d)$ if $a + d = b + c$. This relation is reflexive since $a + b = b + a$, symmetric since equality is symmetric, and transitive: if $(a, b) \sim (c, d)$ and $(c, d) \sim (e, f)$, then $a + d = b + c$ and $c + f = d + e$, so adding yields $a + d + c + f = b + c + d + e$, canceling $c + d$ gives $a + f = b + e$, confirming $(a, b) \sim (e, f)$. The quotient set $\mathbb{Z} = (\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}) / \sim$ inherits addition by $[(a, b)] + [(c, d)] = [(a + c, b + d)]$, well-defined since if $(a, b) \sim (a', b')$ and $(c, d) \sim (c', d')$, then $a + b' = a' + b$ and $c + d' = c' + d$, adding these equations yields $(a + c) + (b' + d') = (a' + c') + (b + d)$, hence $(a + c, b + d) \sim (a' + c', b' + d')$. The additive identity is $[(0, 0)]$, and the additive inverse of $[(a, b)]$ is $[(b, a)]$ since $[(a, b)] + [(b, a)] = [(a + b, b + a)] = [(0, 0)]$. Multiplication extends by $[(a, b)] \cdot [(c, d)] = [(ac + bd, ad + bc)]$, derived by requiring $(a - b)(c - d) = ac + bd - ad - bc$ to hold formally.

The field of fractions construction generalizes the formation of $\mathbb{Q}$ from $\mathbb{Z}$ to any integral domain. An integral domain is a commutative ring with unity where the product of nonzero elements is nonzero: if $ab = 0$ in the ring, then either $a = 0$ or $b = 0$. Given an integral domain $R$, we form the set $R \times (R \setminus \{0\})$ of pairs $(a, b)$ with $b \neq 0$, and define $(a, b) \sim (c, d)$ if $ad = bc$ in $R$. The condition $b \neq 0$ ensures that the equivalence relation is well-defined: if $(a, b) \sim (c, d)$ and $(c, d) \sim (e, f)$, then $ad = bc$ and $cf = de$, multiplying the first by $f$ and the second by $b$ gives $adf = bcf$ and $bcf = bde$, hence $adf = bde$, canceling $d \neq 0$ yields $af = be$, confirming $(a, b) \sim (e, f)$. The cancellation property relies critically on $R$ being an integral domain: if $dx = dy$ with $d \neq 0$, then $d(x - y) = 0$ implies $x = y$. The quotient field $\text{Frac}(R) = (R \times (R \setminus \{0\})) / \sim$ inherits addition by $\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad + bc}{bd}$ and multiplication by $\frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ac}{bd}$, with multiplicative inverses given by $\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{-1} = \frac{b}{a}$ for $a \neq 0$.

The Dedekind cut construction of $\mathbb{R}$ partitions $\mathbb{Q}$ into lower and upper sets, capturing the notion of an irrational number as the boundary between two rational intervals. A Dedekind cut is a pair $(A, B)$ where $A, B \subset \mathbb{Q}$, both nonempty, $A \cup B = \mathbb{Q}$, $A \cap B = \emptyset$, every element of $A$ is strictly less than every element of $B$, and $A$ contains no greatest element. The condition that $A$ lacks a greatest element distinguishes irrational cuts from rational ones: if $A$ had a maximum $r$, the cut would correspond to the rational number $r$ with $A = \{x \in \mathbb{Q} : x < r\}$ and $B = \{x \in \mathbb{Q} : x \geq r\}$. An irrational cut like $\sqrt{2}$ has $A = \{x \in \mathbb{Q} : x < 0 \text{ or } x^2 < 2\}$ and $B = \{x \in \mathbb{Q} : x > 0 \text{ and } x^2 \geq 2\}$. For any $a \in A$ with $a > 0$, we construct a larger element still in $A$ by setting $a' = \frac{a + 2/a}{2}$, the arithmetic mean of $a$ and $2/a$. Squaring yields $(a')^2 = \frac{a^2 + 4 + 4/a^2}{4} < \frac{a^2 + 2a^2 + 4}{4} = \frac{3a^2 + 4}{4} < 2$ for sufficiently small $a$, confirming $a' \in A$ and $a' > a$.

Addition of Dedekind cuts $(A_1, B_1) + (A_2, B_2)$ produces the cut $(A, B)$ where $A = \{r_1 + r_2 : r_1 \in A_1, r_2 \in A_2\}$ and $B = \mathbb{Q} \setminus A$. This definition ensures that every element of $A$ is a sum of elements from $A_1$ and $A_2$, hence less than any sum from $B_1$ and $B_2$, and if $A$ had a maximum $m = r_1 + r_2$, we could choose $r_1' > r_1$ in $A_1$ to produce $r_1' + r_2 > m$, contradicting maximality. Multiplication of positive cuts requires care: for $(A_1, B_1)$ and $(A_2, B_2)$ both representing positive reals, define $A = \{r_1 r_2 : r_1 \in A_1^+, r_2 \in A_2^+\} \cup \{x \in \mathbb{Q} : x \leq 0\}$ where $A_i^+ = A_i \cap \mathbb{Q}_{>0}$. The resulting cut $(A, B)$ represents the product, with $B = \mathbb{Q} \setminus A$. Extending multiplication to all reals requires defining signs and invoking the rule $(-x)(-y) = xy$.

The Cauchy sequence construction provides an alternative path to $\mathbb{R}$ using convergence rather than order. A sequence $(a_n)$ of rationals is Cauchy if for every rational $\epsilon > 0$, there exists a natural number $N$ such that $|a_m - a_n| < \epsilon$ for all $m, n \geq N$. This condition captures the intuition that terms become arbitrarily close as the sequence progresses, without requiring a limit to exist within $\mathbb{Q}$. Two Cauchy sequences $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$ are equivalent if $(a_n - b_n) \to 0$, that is, for every $\epsilon > 0$, there exists $N$ such that $|a_n - b_n| < \epsilon$ for all $n \geq N$. The equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive: transitivity follows from the triangle inequality $|a_n - c_n| \leq |a_n - b_n| + |b_n - c_n|$, and if both terms on the right are less than $\epsilon/2$ for large $n$, their sum is less than $\epsilon$. The set of equivalence classes $\mathbb{R} = \text{Cauchy}(\mathbb{Q}) / \sim$ inherits addition and multiplication componentwise: $[(a_n)] + [(b_n)] = [(a_n + b_n)]$ and $[(a_n)] \cdot [(b_n)] = [(a_n b_n)]$. Verifying that the product of Cauchy sequences is Cauchy requires showing that $(a_n b_n)$ satisfies the Cauchy criterion: since $(a_n)$ is Cauchy, it is bounded, say $|a_n| \leq M$ for all $n$, and similarly $|b_n| \leq M'$. Then $|a_m b_m - a_n b_n| = |a_m(b_m - b_n) + b_n(a_m - a_n)| \leq |a_m||b_m - b_n| + |b_n||a_m - a_n| < M \epsilon/(2M) + M' \epsilon/(2M') < \epsilon$ for sufficiently large $m, n$, confirming $(a_n b_n)$ is Cauchy.

The completeness of $\mathbb{R}$ follows from either construction. In the Dedekind cut framework, every nonempty set $S$ of cuts that is bounded above possesses a least upper bound: define $A_{\sup} = \bigcup_{(A, B) \in S} A$, the union of all lower sets in $S$. This union satisfies the cut axioms since it is nonempty (every cut in $S$ contributes elements to $A_{\sup}$), bounded above by the upper bound of $S$, and contains no maximum (if $r \in A_{\sup}$, then $r \in A$ for some $(A, B) \in S$, and since $A$ has no maximum, there exists $r' > r$ in $A \subset A_{\sup}$). The cut $(A_{\sup}, \mathbb{Q} \setminus A_{\sup})$ is the least upper bound of $S$ since it contains all elements less than any element of $S$, and any smaller cut would miss elements from some $(A, B) \in S$. In the Cauchy sequence construction, completeness asserts that every Cauchy sequence of reals converges: given a Cauchy sequence of equivalence classes $([(a_n^{(k)})])$, we construct a limit by diagonalizing. Choose representatives $a_n^{(k)}$ such that $|a_m^{(k)} - a_n^{(k)}| < 1/k$ for $m, n \geq k$, and define the diagonal sequence $b_k = a_k^{(k)}$. This sequence is Cauchy in $\mathbb{Q}$: for $m, n \geq N$, we have $|b_m - b_n| \leq |b_m - a_N^{(m)}| + |a_N^{(m)} - a_N^{(n)}| + |a_N^{(n)} - b_n| < 1/m + \epsilon + 1/n < 3\epsilon$ for sufficiently large $N$, confirming convergence.

The complex field $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}^2$ inherits vector addition from the plane and equips the space with a multiplication law that makes $(0, 1)$ a square root of $(-1, 0)$. The multiplication $(a, b)(c, d) = (ac - bd, ad + bc)$ derives from requiring distributivity and the identity $(0, 1)^2 = (-1, 0)$: if we write $(a, b) = (a, 0) + (0, 1)(b, 0)$ identifying $(a, 0)$ with $a$ and $(0, 1)$ with $i$, then $(a + bi)(c + di) = ac + adi + bci + bdi^2 = ac + (ad + bc)i + bd(-1) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i$. This multiplication is commutative, associative, and distributes over addition. The multiplicative identity is $(1, 0)$, and the multiplicative inverse of $(a, b) \neq (0, 0)$ is $\left(\frac{a}{a^2 + b^2}, \frac{-b}{a^2 + b^2}\right)$, verified by direct calculation: $(a, b) \cdot \left(\frac{a}{a^2 + b^2}, \frac{-b}{a^2 + b^2}\right) = \left(\frac{a^2 + b^2}{a^2 + b^2}, \frac{-ab + ab}{a^2 + b^2}\right) = (1, 0)$.

The polar form $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = re^{i\theta}$ provides geometric insight into complex multiplication, leveraging Euler's formula $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$. Multiplying two complex numbers in polar form yields $r_1 e^{i\theta_1} \cdot r_2 e^{i\theta_2} = r_1 r_2 e^{i(\theta_1 + \theta_2)}$, revealing that multiplication scales moduli and adds arguments. This representation proves de Moivre's theorem $(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos(n\theta) + i\sin(n\theta)$ by induction, since $(e^{i\theta})^n = e^{in\theta}$ and equating real and imaginary parts yields the result. De Moivre's theorem allows extraction of $n$th roots: the solutions to $z^n = w$ for $w = re^{i\theta}$ are $z_k = r^{1/n} e^{i(\theta + 2\pi k)/n}$ for $k = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1$, giving $n$ distinct roots equally spaced around a circle of radius $r^{1/n}$.

The fundamental theorem of algebra admits a proof using Liouville's theorem from complex analysis: a bounded entire function (holomorphic on all of $\mathbb{C}$) is constant. Suppose $p(z)$ is a nonconstant polynomial with no roots. Then $f(z) = 1/p(z)$ is entire, since $p(z) \neq 0$ for all $z$ eliminates singularities. As $|z| \to \infty$, the dominant term $a_n z^n$ of $p(z)$ dominates, so $|p(z)| \geq |a_n||z|^n - \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} |a_k||z|^k \to \infty$, implying $|f(z)| = 1/|p(z)| \to 0$. Hence $f(z)$ is bounded on large circles $|z| = R$, and by the maximum modulus principle, $f(z)$ is bounded on all of $\mathbb{C}$. Liouville's theorem forces $f(z)$ to be constant, hence $p(z)$ is constant, contradicting the assumption that $p(z)$ is nonconstant. Thus $p(z)$ must have at least one root.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Computational arithmetic over different number systems exhibits distinct algorithmic complexities and numerical stability characteristics. Natural number arithmetic via Peano successor operations yields exponential time complexity for repeated addition: computing $m + n$ requires $n$ recursive calls to the successor function, and multiplication $m \cdot n$ requires $n$ additions, totaling $O(n)$ additions and $O(n^2)$ successor operations. Practical implementations use binary or decimal positional notation, reducing addition to $O(\log n)$ digit operations and multiplication to $O((\log n)^2)$ via grade-school algorithms or $O(n \log n)$ using fast Fourier transform (FFT) techniques for large integers. The Karatsuba algorithm achieves $O(n^{\log_2 3}) \approx O(n^{1.585})$ by splitting numbers into halves and reducing three recursive multiplications instead of four, exploiting the identity $(a \cdot 2^{n/2} + b)(c \cdot 2^{n/2} + d) = ac \cdot 2^n + ((a+b)(c+d) - ac - bd) \cdot 2^{n/2} + bd$.

Rational arithmetic suffers from denominator blowup: adding $\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad + bc}{bd}$ produces denominators growing multiplicatively with each operation. Without reduction to lowest terms via the greatest common divisor (GCD), denominators explode exponentially. The Euclidean algorithm computes $\gcd(m, n)$ in $O(\log \min(m, n))$ iterations by iteratively replacing $(m, n)$ with $(n, m \mod n)$ until the remainder vanishes. Extended Euclidean algorithm computes integers $x, y$ such that $mx + ny = \gcd(m, n)$, enabling efficient reduction of fractions $\frac{a}{b}$ to $\frac{a/g}{b/g}$ where $g = \gcd(a, b)$. Fraction-free algorithms maintain integer arithmetic by clearing denominators, multiplying through by least common multiples, and deferring division until final results.

Real number computation requires fixed-precision or arbitrary-precision floating-point arithmetic. The IEEE 754 standard encodes reals as sign-exponent-mantissa triples, storing a number as $(-1)^s \cdot 1.f \cdot 2^{e - 127}$ in single precision (32 bits) or $(-1)^s \cdot 1.f \cdot 2^{e - 1023}$ in double precision (64 bits). Floating-point arithmetic introduces rounding errors: operations like $a + b$ are computed as $\text{fl}(a + b) = (a + b)(1 + \delta)$ where $|\delta| \leq \epsilon_{\text{mach}}$, the machine epsilon. For double precision, $\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \approx 2.22 \times 10^{-16}$. Catastrophic cancellation occurs when subtracting nearly equal numbers: if $a \approx b$, then $a - b$ loses significant digits, amplifying relative error. Computing $\sqrt{x^2 + 1} - \sqrt{x^2}$ for large $x$ suffers cancellation; multiplying by the conjugate yields $\frac{(\sqrt{x^2 + 1} - \sqrt{x^2})(\sqrt{x^2 + 1} + \sqrt{x^2})}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1} + \sqrt{x^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1} + \sqrt{x^2}} \approx \frac{1}{2x}$, a stable form. Summation algorithms like Kahan summation compensate for roundoff by maintaining a running correction term, reducing accumulated error from $O(n\epsilon)$ to $O(\epsilon)$ for $n$ additions.

Arbitrary-precision libraries like GMP (GNU Multiple Precision) represent reals as variable-length mantissas with adjustable precision, enabling exact symbolic computation at the cost of memory and time. Interval arithmetic tracks lower and upper bounds $[a^-, a^+]$ for each quantity, propagating intervals through operations: $[a^-, a^+] + [b^-, b^+] = [a^- + b^-, a^+ + b^+]$ with directed rounding ensures the true result lies within the computed interval. Interval arithmetic provides rigorous error bounds but suffers from interval overestimation: repeated operations widen intervals excessively unless sophisticated splitting and midpoint refinement techniques are employed.

Complex arithmetic inherits floating-point instabilities and introduces phase ambiguities. Computing $(a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i$ in floating-point incurs four multiplications and two additions, each introducing error. For modulus and argument, $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ may overflow if $|a|$ or $|b|$ exceeds $\sqrt{\text{MAX\_FLOAT}}$; the stable form $|z| = |a|\sqrt{1 + (b/a)^2}$ for $|a| \geq |b|$ prevents overflow by scaling. Argument $\theta = \text{atan2}(b, a)$ is multivalued modulo $2\pi$; implementations return principal values in $(-\pi, \pi]$. Branch cuts in complex functions like $\log z = \log|z| + i\arg(z)$ and $\sqrt{z}$ require careful handling: $\log z$ is discontinuous across the negative real axis, jumping by $2\pi i$ when crossing from below to above. Numerical root-finding for complex polynomials via Newton's method $z_{n+1} = z_n - p(z_n)/p'(z_n)$ converges quadratically when starting near a root, but basin boundaries exhibit fractal structure, complicating initial guess selection.

The Wilkinson polynomial $(x - 1)(x - 2) \cdots (x - 20)$ illustrates conditioning issues: expanding to $x^{20} - 210x^{19} + \cdots + 20!$ and perturbing the coefficient of $x^{19}$ by $10^{-8}$ shifts some roots by up to $0.7$, a billion-fold amplification. The condition number of polynomial root-finding grows exponentially with degree, making high-degree polynomial roots extremely sensitive to coefficient perturbations. Eigenvalue algorithms like QR iteration compute roots of characteristic polynomials indirectly through iterative matrix transformations, achieving numerical stability despite underlying polynomial conditioning.

Completeness failures in $\mathbb{Q}$ manifest algorithmically: iterative methods like Newton's method for $\sqrt{2}$ generate Cauchy sequences of rationals that do not converge to a rational limit. The iteration $x_{n+1} = \frac{1}{2}(x_n + 2/x_n)$ started at $x_0 = 1$ produces $x_1 = 3/2$, $x_2 = 17/12$, $x_3 = 577/408$, converging to $\sqrt{2}$ in $\mathbb{R}$ but lacking a rational limit. Algorithms requiring completeness, such as bisection methods for continuous functions, fail over $\mathbb{Q}$: bisecting intervals to locate a root of $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ produces nested rational intervals whose intersection is irrational.

Algebraic number fields $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ extend $\mathbb{Q}$ by adjoining roots of polynomials, providing intermediate structures between $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}$. Computing in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) = \{a + b\sqrt{2} : a, b \in \mathbb{Q}\}$ uses exact rational arithmetic for coefficients $a, b$, avoiding floating-point errors. Addition and multiplication reduce to operations on $a, b$ coefficients: $(a_1 + b_1\sqrt{2}) + (a_2 + b_2\sqrt{2}) = (a_1 + a_2) + (b_1 + b_2)\sqrt{2}$ and $(a_1 + b_1\sqrt{2})(a_2 + b_2\sqrt{2}) = (a_1 a_2 + 2b_1 b_2) + (a_1 b_2 + a_2 b_1)\sqrt{2}$. Inverses exist since $(a + b\sqrt{2})^{-1} = \frac{a - b\sqrt{2}}{a^2 - 2b^2}$ with denominator $a^2 - 2b^2 \neq 0$ for $(a, b) \neq (0, 0)$. Galois theory classifies such extensions through automorphism groups, characterizing solvability of polynomial equations by radicals via solvable Galois groups.

Constructive mathematics rejects the law of excluded middle, requiring existence proofs to provide explicit constructions. In constructive analysis, the real numbers are defined as Cauchy sequences or computable reals, and the intermediate value theorem becomes an algorithm: given a continuous function $f : [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ with $f(a) < 0 < f(b)$, bisection produces a sequence of nested intervals $[a_n, b_n]$ with $f(a_n) < 0 < f(b_n)$, converging to a root. The completeness axiom holds constructively for computable reals, but classical proofs invoking suprema of arbitrary sets fail without explicit construction algorithms. Computable real arithmetic uses representations like signed-digit streams or interval sequences with modulus-of-convergence functions, ensuring that every algorithm produces approximations with error bounds computable from the input precision.

Numerical linear algebra over $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$ dominates computational mathematics, with matrix factorizations (LU, QR, SVD) providing stable algorithms for solving linear systems, least squares problems, and eigenvalue computations. Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting achieves backward stability: the computed solution $\tilde{x}$ satisfies $(A + \delta A)\tilde{x} = b$ where $\|\delta A\| = O(\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \|A\|)$, meaning the computed solution is exact for a slightly perturbed problem. Ill-conditioned matrices amplify errors: the condition number $\kappa(A) = \|A\| \|A^{-1}\|$ bounds relative error amplification $\frac{\|\delta x\|}{\|x\|} \leq \kappa(A) \frac{\|\delta A\|}{\|A\|}$. Regularization techniques like Tikhonov regularization replace $Ax = b$ with $(A^T A + \lambda I)x = A^T b$, trading bias for reduced variance in solutions.

Fast Fourier transform (FFT) exploits algebraic structure of complex roots of unity to compute discrete Fourier transforms in $O(n \log n)$ time, down from $O(n^2)$ for naive matrix multiplication. The FFT recursively splits the DFT into even and odd indexed terms, leveraging the identity $\omega_n^{k + n/2} = -\omega_n^k$ where $\omega_n = e^{2\pi i/n}$ to combine results. Number-theoretic transforms replace $\omega_n$ with primitive roots of unity modulo prime $p$, enabling exact FFT-based convolution over finite fields for applications in algebraic coding theory and symbolic computation.

Computational algebraic geometry uses Gröbner basis algorithms to solve polynomial systems over $\mathbb{Q}$ and algebraic extensions, reducing nonlinear systems to triangular form via Buchberger's algorithm. Gröbner bases compute ideal membership, elimination ideals, and solution sets, but exhibit doubly exponential worst-case complexity in the number of variables and degree. Symbolic-numeric hybrid methods initialize symbolic computation with numerical approximations, refining solutions via exact arithmetic to certify correctness.

Transcendental extensions $\mathbb{R}(e, \pi)$ adjoin numbers like $e$ and $\pi$ that are not algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$, introducing infinite-dimensional vector spaces over $\mathbb{Q}$ without minimal polynomials. Computing with transcendentals requires either numerical approximation or formal symbolic manipulation. Richardson's theorem proves that determining whether an expression involving $\pi$, $e$, $\log$, $\sin$, and arithmetic equals zero is undecidable, imposing fundamental limits on symbolic simplification. Practical computer algebra systems use rewrite rules and heuristics, detecting zero equivalence for restricted subclasses while leaving general cases unresolved.

Error propagation in multi-step algorithms compounds through the chain rule: if $y = f(x)$ and $z = g(y)$, then $\frac{\delta z}{z} \approx \frac{g'(y)}{g(y)} \frac{f'(x)}{f(x)} \frac{\delta x}{x}$, showing relative errors multiply by condition numbers at each stage. Backward error analysis reverses this: instead of tracking forward error $\|z - \tilde{z}\|$, compute the smallest perturbation $\delta x$ such that $\tilde{z} = g(f(x + \delta x))$, interpreting the computed result as exact for perturbed input. Stable algorithms satisfy $\|\delta x\| = O(\epsilon_{\text{mach}}\|x\|)$, ensuring computed answers are exact for nearly correct inputs.

Verification of numerical computations employs certified arithmetic: libraries like MPFR provide correctly rounded arbitrary-precision operations, and proof assistants like Coq and Isabelle formalize real analysis theorems, enabling machine-checked verification of numerical algorithms. Formal verification extends to hardware: Intel's division bug (Pentium FDIV) missed an error in a lookup table, causing $1/824633702441$ to return an incorrect result. Formal methods now verify floating-point units against IEEE 754 specifications, preventing such failures.

The hierarchy $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R} \subset \mathbb{C}$ embeds algorithmically: integers use arbitrary-precision libraries, rationals maintain numerator-denominator pairs, reals use floating-point or interval arithmetic, complexes use pairs of reals. Type systems in programming languages enforce these embeddings: Haskell's type classes distinguish `Integer`, `Rational`, `Double`, and `Complex Double`, coercing between them explicitly. Dynamically typed languages like Python unify numeric types through polymorphic operators, automatically promoting integers to rationals or reals as needed, at the cost of hidden performance penalties and precision loss.
