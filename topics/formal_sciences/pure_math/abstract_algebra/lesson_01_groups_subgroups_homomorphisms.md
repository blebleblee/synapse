# Lesson 1: Groups, Subgroups, Homomorphisms

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Group theory emerged from the study of symmetry and solution structures of polynomial equations, crystallizing into one of the most profound unifying frameworks in mathematics. At its core, a group captures the abstract essence of transformation and reversibility: any collection of operations that can be combined, undone, and composed associatively forms the substrate for deep algebraic investigation. The power of group theory lies not in the specific nature of its elements—whether rotations of geometric objects, permutations of finite sets, or invertible matrices—but in the axiomatic skeleton that governs their interactions.

A **group** $(G, \cdot)$ consists of a set $G$ equipped with a binary operation $\cdot : G \times G \to G$ satisfying four fundamental axioms. First, the operation must be **closed**: for all $a, b \in G$, the product $a \cdot b$ remains in $G$. Second, the operation must be **associative**: for all $a, b, c \in G$, we have $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$. Third, there exists an **identity element** $e \in G$ such that $e \cdot a = a \cdot e = a$ for all $a \in G$. Fourth, every element must have an **inverse**: for each $a \in G$, there exists $a^{-1} \in G$ such that $a \cdot a^{-1} = a^{-1} \cdot a = e$.

These axioms, though minimal, generate an astonishingly rich theory. The identity element, for instance, is necessarily unique: if $e$ and $e'$ both serve as identities, then $e = e \cdot e' = e'$ by applying the identity property twice. Similarly, inverses are unique: if $b$ and $c$ are both inverses of $a$, then $b = b \cdot e = b \cdot (a \cdot c) = (b \cdot a) \cdot c = e \cdot c = c$. These seemingly trivial observations establish that the group structure is rigid and well-defined, admitting no ambiguity in its fundamental components.

The multiplicative group of nonzero real numbers $(\mathbb{R}^*, \times)$ provides a canonical example. Closure holds because the product of nonzero reals is nonzero. Associativity follows from the associativity of real multiplication. The identity is $1$, and every nonzero real $x$ has inverse $x^{-1} = 1/x$. In contrast, the set of all real numbers under multiplication fails to form a group precisely because $0$ has no multiplicative inverse, illustrating how the axioms impose genuine structural constraints.

**Cyclic groups** represent the simplest infinite and finite groups, generated by repeated application of a single element. Let $g \in G$ be an arbitrary element. The **cyclic subgroup generated by** $g$ is defined as $\langle g \rangle = \{g^n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$, where $g^n$ denotes the $n$-fold product $g \cdot g \cdot \ldots \cdot g$ for $n > 0$, the identity for $n = 0$, and $(g^{-1})^{|n|}$ for $n < 0$. If $\langle g \rangle = G$, we say $G$ is cyclic with generator $g$. The **order** of $g$, denoted $|g|$ or $\text{ord}(g)$, is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $g^n = e$, or infinity if no such $n$ exists.

The additive group of integers $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is the prototypical infinite cyclic group, generated by $1$ (or equivalently, by $-1$). Every element $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ can be written as $n \cdot 1$, where we adopt additive notation: $n \cdot 1 = \underbrace{1 + 1 + \ldots + 1}_{n \text{ times}}$. The group $\mathbb{Z}_n = \{0, 1, 2, \ldots, n-1\}$ under addition modulo $n$ exemplifies finite cyclic groups. Here, the generator is $1$, and $\text{ord}(1) = n$ since $\underbrace{1 + 1 + \ldots + 1}_{n \text{ times}} \equiv 0 \pmod{n}$.

A fundamental theorem characterizes cyclic groups completely: every cyclic group is either isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$ (if infinite) or to $\mathbb{Z}_n$ for some positive integer $n$ (if finite of order $n$). The proof proceeds by mapping the generator to $1$ and extending by the group operation. If $G = \langle g \rangle$ is infinite, define $\phi : \mathbb{Z} \to G$ by $\phi(k) = g^k$. This map is a homomorphism because $\phi(k + m) = g^{k+m} = g^k \cdot g^m = \phi(k) \cdot \phi(m)$. Injectivity follows from the assumption that $g$ has infinite order: if $\phi(k) = \phi(m)$, then $g^k = g^m$, whence $g^{k-m} = e$, forcing $k = m$. Surjectivity is immediate from the definition of $\langle g \rangle$. For finite cyclic groups of order $n$, the analogous map $\phi : \mathbb{Z}_n \to G$ with $\phi(k) = g^k$ establishes the isomorphism, using the fact that $g^n = e$ to ensure well-definition on equivalence classes modulo $n$.

**Permutation groups** arise naturally from the study of symmetries of finite sets. Let $X$ be a finite set of cardinality $n$. A **permutation** of $X$ is a bijection $\sigma : X \to X$. The set of all permutations of $X$, denoted $S_X$, forms a group under function composition. When $X = \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$, we write $S_n$ for the **symmetric group on** $n$ **letters**, which has order $n!$.

Permutations admit multiple notational conventions. The **two-line notation** writes $\sigma$ as a matrix:

$$
\sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & \ldots & n \\ \sigma(1) & \sigma(2) & \sigma(3) & \ldots & \sigma(n) \end{pmatrix}
$$

More compactly, **cycle notation** represents $\sigma$ by decomposing it into disjoint cycles. A **cycle** $(a_1 \, a_2 \, \ldots \, a_k)$ denotes the permutation that sends $a_1 \mapsto a_2 \mapsto \ldots \mapsto a_k \mapsto a_1$ and fixes all other elements. For example, the permutation in $S_5$ given by $1 \mapsto 3$, $3 \mapsto 1$, $2 \mapsto 4$, $4 \mapsto 5$, $5 \mapsto 2$ is written $(1 \, 3)(2 \, 4 \, 5)$.

Every permutation decomposes uniquely (up to order) into a product of disjoint cycles. To compute this decomposition, start with any element $x$, follow the orbit $x \mapsto \sigma(x) \mapsto \sigma^2(x) \mapsto \ldots$ until returning to $x$, recording the cycle, then repeat with an element not yet processed. Disjoint cycles commute: if $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are disjoint cycles, then $\alpha \beta = \beta \alpha$, because they act on disjoint subsets of $X$.

A cycle of length $2$, denoted $(a \, b)$, is called a **transposition**. Transpositions generate the entire symmetric group: every permutation can be expressed as a product of transpositions. The proof proceeds by induction on the number of non-fixed points. If $\sigma$ moves only $1$ element, then $\sigma = \text{id}$, which is the empty product. Otherwise, express $\sigma$ as a product of disjoint cycles, and decompose each $k$-cycle as $(a_1 \, a_k)(a_1 \, a_{k-1}) \cdots (a_1 \, a_2)$. The decomposition is not unique—$(1 \, 2 \, 3) = (1 \, 3)(1 \, 2) = (2 \, 3)(1 \, 3)$—but the **parity** (even or odd number of transpositions) is an invariant.

To formalize parity, define the **sign homomorphism** $\text{sgn} : S_n \to \{1, -1\}$ by $\text{sgn}(\sigma) = (-1)^m$, where $m$ is the number of transpositions in any decomposition of $\sigma$. This is well-defined because the parity of $m$ is independent of the choice of decomposition. The **alternating group** $A_n = \ker(\text{sgn}) = \{\sigma \in S_n : \text{sgn}(\sigma) = 1\}$ consists of all even permutations and has order $n!/2$ for $n \ge 2$.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The internal structure of groups reveals itself through the notion of **subgroups**. A subset $H \subseteq G$ is a **subgroup** if $H$ is itself a group under the operation inherited from $G$. Equivalently, $H$ is a subgroup if and only if it is nonempty, closed under the group operation, and closed under taking inverses. The one-step subgroup test condenses these conditions: $H \subseteq G$ is a subgroup if and only if $H \ne \emptyset$ and for all $a, b \in H$, we have $ab^{-1} \in H$. This test incorporates closure ($a, b \in H$ implies $a(b^{-1})^{-1} = ab \in H$) and inverse closure ($e, b \in H$ implies $eb^{-1} = b^{-1} \in H$).

Every group $G$ has two trivial subgroups: $\{e\}$ and $G$ itself. Proper nontrivial subgroups often encode essential structural information. In $\mathbb{Z}$, every subgroup has the form $n\mathbb{Z} = \{nk : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ for some $n \ge 0$. To prove this, let $H \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ be a subgroup. If $H = \{0\}$, we are done. Otherwise, $H$ contains positive integers (since $a \in H$ implies $-a \in H$). Let $n$ be the smallest positive integer in $H$. By closure, $n\mathbb{Z} \subseteq H$. Conversely, let $m \in H$. By the division algorithm, $m = qn + r$ with $0 \le r < n$. Then $r = m - qn \in H$ (by closure under subtraction). Since $n$ is minimal and $r < n$, we must have $r = 0$, so $m = qn \in n\mathbb{Z}$. Thus $H = n\mathbb{Z}$.

The **subgroup lattice** of $G$, denoted $\mathcal{L}(G)$, is the partially ordered set of all subgroups of $G$ under inclusion. For finite groups, this lattice visualizes the hierarchical containment structure. In $\mathbb{Z}_12$, the subgroups are $\{0\}$, $\langle 1 \rangle = \mathbb{Z}_{12}$, $\langle 2 \rangle = \{0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10\}$, $\langle 3 \rangle = \{0, 3, 6, 9\}$, $\langle 4 \rangle = \{0, 4, 8\}$, and $\langle 6 \rangle = \{0, 6\}$. The lattice has $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ at the top, $\{0\}$ at the bottom, and intermediate subgroups partially ordered by inclusion:

```
            ⟨1⟩ = ℤ₁₂
           /  |  \
          /   |   \
       ⟨2⟩  ⟨3⟩  (other)
        |  \/  |
        | /  \ |
       ⟨4⟩  ⟨6⟩
         \  |  /
          \ | /
           {0}
```

A fundamental property of subgroups is their ability to partition the group via **cosets**. Let $H$ be a subgroup of $G$ and let $a \in G$. The **left coset** of $H$ by $a$ is $aH = \{ah : h \in H\}$. Similarly, the **right coset** is $Ha = \{ha : h \in H\}$. Cosets are not subgroups in general (unless $a \in H$, in which case $aH = H$), but they partition $G$ into disjoint equivalence classes.

To establish the partition property, we prove two claims. First, every element of $G$ belongs to some coset: $g \in gH$ since $e \in H$. Second, two cosets are either identical or disjoint. Suppose $aH \cap bH \ne \emptyset$. Then there exist $h_1, h_2 \in H$ such that $ah_1 = bh_2$. Multiplying on the right by $h_1^{-1}$ and on the left by $b^{-1}$, we obtain $a = bh_2h_1^{-1}$. Let $c \in aH$, so $c = ah$ for some $h \in H$. Then $c = bh_2h_1^{-1}h = b(h_2h_1^{-1}h) \in bH$ since $H$ is closed. Thus $aH \subseteq bH$. By symmetry, $bH \subseteq aH$, so $aH = bH$.

The cosets $aH$ and $bH$ have the same cardinality for all $a, b \in G$, as witnessed by the bijection $\phi : aH \to bH$ given by $\phi(ah) = bh$. This map is well-defined and injective: if $ah_1 = ah_2$, then $h_1 = h_2$ by left cancellation, so $bh_1 = bh_2$. Surjectivity is immediate. Thus, all cosets of $H$ are equinumerous.

**Lagrange's Theorem** now follows immediately. Let $G$ be a finite group and $H$ a subgroup. The left cosets of $H$ partition $G$ into disjoint subsets of equal size $|H|$. If there are $k$ distinct cosets, then $|G| = k \cdot |H|$. The integer $k = [G : H]$ is called the **index** of $H$ in $G$. Formally:

$$
|G| = [G : H] \cdot |H|
$$

In particular, $|H|$ divides $|G|$, and $[G : H] = |G| / |H|$. As a corollary, the order of any element $g \in G$ divides $|G|$, since $|g| = |\langle g \rangle|$ and $\langle g \rangle$ is a subgroup.

Lagrange's theorem has immediate computational power. In $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$, subgroups must have order dividing $12$, so possible orders are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$. The element $4 \in \mathbb{Z}_{12}$ has order $3$ because $4 + 4 + 4 = 12 \equiv 0 \pmod{12}$, and indeed $3 \mid 12$. The theorem also implies that groups of prime order $p$ are necessarily cyclic: if $|G| = p$ and $g \in G$ with $g \ne e$, then $|\langle g \rangle| \mid p$, forcing $|\langle g \rangle| = p$, so $G = \langle g \rangle$.

**Homomorphisms** formalize structure-preserving maps between groups. A function $\phi : G \to H$ is a **group homomorphism** if $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$ for all $a, b \in G$. Homomorphisms automatically preserve identities and inverses: $\phi(e_G) = e_H$ and $\phi(a^{-1}) = \phi(a)^{-1}$. To prove the first, observe $\phi(e_G) = \phi(e_G e_G) = \phi(e_G)\phi(e_G)$. Multiplying both sides by $\phi(e_G)^{-1}$ yields $e_H = \phi(e_G)$. For the second, $\phi(a)\phi(a^{-1}) = \phi(aa^{-1}) = \phi(e_G) = e_H$, so $\phi(a^{-1})$ is the inverse of $\phi(a)$.

The **kernel** of $\phi$ is $\ker(\phi) = \{g \in G : \phi(g) = e_H\}$, and the **image** is $\text{im}(\phi) = \{\phi(g) : g \in G\}$. Both are subgroups: $\ker(\phi) \subseteq G$ and $\text{im}(\phi) \subseteq H$. The kernel measures the failure of injectivity: $\phi$ is injective if and only if $\ker(\phi) = \{e_G\}$. Indeed, if $\phi(a) = \phi(b)$, then $\phi(ab^{-1}) = \phi(a)\phi(b)^{-1} = e_H$, so $ab^{-1} \in \ker(\phi)$. If $\ker(\phi) = \{e_G\}$, then $ab^{-1} = e_G$, whence $a = b$.

The **isomorphism** concept captures the notion of identical group structure under relabeling. A homomorphism $\phi : G \to H$ is an **isomorphism** if it is bijective. Two groups $G$ and $H$ are **isomorphic**, written $G \cong H$, if there exists an isomorphism between them. Isomorphic groups are indistinguishable from the perspective of group theory: they have the same order, the same number of elements of each order, and the same subgroup lattice structure.

For example, the Klein four-group $V_4 = \{e, a, b, c\}$ with relations $a^2 = b^2 = c^2 = e$ and $ab = c$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$. The map $\phi : V_4 \to \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$ defined by $\phi(e) = (0, 0)$, $\phi(a) = (1, 0)$, $\phi(b) = (0, 1)$, $\phi(c) = (1, 1)$ is an isomorphism, verifiable by checking that $\phi$ preserves the group operation and is bijective.

**Normal subgroups** are distinguished subgroups for which left and right cosets coincide. A subgroup $N \subseteq G$ is **normal**, written $N \trianglelefteq G$, if $gNg^{-1} = N$ for all $g \in G$, where $gNg^{-1} = \{gng^{-1} : n \in N\}$. Equivalently, $N$ is normal if and only if $gN = Ng$ for all $g \in G$. Normal subgroups are precisely the kernels of homomorphisms: if $\phi : G \to H$ is a homomorphism, then $\ker(\phi) \trianglelefteq G$. Conversely, if $N \trianglelefteq G$, there exists a homomorphism with kernel $N$ (the quotient map, discussed below).

To prove that kernels are normal, let $N = \ker(\phi)$ and let $g \in G$, $n \in N$. Then $\phi(gng^{-1}) = \phi(g)\phi(n)\phi(g)^{-1} = \phi(g) e_H \phi(g)^{-1} = e_H$, so $gng^{-1} \in N$. Thus $gNg^{-1} \subseteq N$. By replacing $g$ with $g^{-1}$, we obtain $g^{-1}Ng \subseteq N$, which rearranges to $N \subseteq gNg^{-1}$. Hence $gNg^{-1} = N$.

In abelian groups, every subgroup is normal since $gng^{-1} = gg^{-1}n = n$ for all $g, n$. In nonabelian groups, normality is a nontrivial constraint. The alternating group $A_n \trianglelefteq S_n$ is normal because it is the kernel of the sign homomorphism. However, not all subgroups of $S_n$ are normal: the subgroup $\langle (1 \, 2) \rangle \subset S_3$ generated by the transposition $(1 \, 2)$ is not normal, since $(1 \, 3) \langle (1 \, 2) \rangle (1 \, 3)^{-1} = \langle (2 \, 3) \rangle \ne \langle (1 \, 2) \rangle$.

Given a normal subgroup $N \trianglelefteq G$, we construct the **quotient group** $G/N$, whose elements are the cosets of $N$. Define the operation on $G/N$ by $(aN)(bN) = (ab)N$. This operation is well-defined precisely because $N$ is normal: if $a_1N = a_2N$ and $b_1N = b_2N$, then $a_1 = a_2n_1$ and $b_1 = b_2n_2$ for some $n_1, n_2 \in N$. Then $a_1b_1 = a_2n_1b_2n_2 = a_2b_2(b_2^{-1}n_1b_2)n_2$. Since $N$ is normal, $b_2^{-1}n_1b_2 \in N$, and by closure, $(b_2^{-1}n_1b_2)n_2 \in N$. Thus $a_1b_1N = a_2b_2N$. The quotient group $G/N$ inherits associativity from $G$, has identity $eN = N$, and each coset $aN$ has inverse $a^{-1}N$.

The **canonical projection** $\pi : G \to G/N$ defined by $\pi(g) = gN$ is a surjective homomorphism with kernel $N$. Indeed, $\pi(ab) = (ab)N = (aN)(bN) = \pi(a)\pi(b)$, and $\ker(\pi) = \{g \in G : gN = N\} = N$.

The **First Isomorphism Theorem** establishes a fundamental correspondence between homomorphisms and quotient groups. Let $\phi : G \to H$ be a homomorphism. Then:

$$
G / \ker(\phi) \cong \text{im}(\phi)
$$

The proof constructs an explicit isomorphism $\bar{\phi} : G/\ker(\phi) \to \text{im}(\phi)$ defined by $\bar{\phi}(g\ker(\phi)) = \phi(g)$. This map is well-defined: if $g_1\ker(\phi) = g_2\ker(\phi)$, then $g_1 = g_2k$ for some $k \in \ker(\phi)$, so $\phi(g_1) = \phi(g_2k) = \phi(g_2)\phi(k) = \phi(g_2) e_H = \phi(g_2)$. The map $\bar{\phi}$ is a homomorphism because $\bar{\phi}((g_1\ker(\phi))(g_2\ker(\phi))) = \bar{\phi}(g_1g_2\ker(\phi)) = \phi(g_1g_2) = \phi(g_1)\phi(g_2) = \bar{\phi}(g_1\ker(\phi))\bar{\phi}(g_2\ker(\phi))$. Injectivity follows from $\ker(\bar{\phi}) = \{\ker(\phi)\} = \{e_{G/\ker(\phi)}\}$, and surjectivity is immediate from the definition of $\text{im}(\phi)$.

The **Second Isomorphism Theorem** (Diamond Isomorphism Theorem) states that if $H$ is a subgroup and $N$ is a normal subgroup of $G$, then:

$$
H / (H \cap N) \cong HN / N
$$

where $HN = \{hn : h \in H, n \in N\}$. The proof defines $\phi : H \to HN/N$ by $\phi(h) = hN$. This is a homomorphism with kernel $H \cap N$, and the First Isomorphism Theorem yields the result.

The **Third Isomorphism Theorem** addresses iterated quotients. If $N, K \trianglelefteq G$ with $N \subseteq K$, then $K/N \trianglelefteq G/N$ and:

$$
(G/N) / (K/N) \cong G/K
$$

The map $(gN)(K/N) \mapsto gK$ provides the isomorphism. These isomorphism theorems reduce many structural questions about groups to questions about quotients and kernels, enabling powerful algebraic manipulations.

**Group actions** formalize the notion of groups acting as symmetries on sets. A **group action** of $G$ on a set $X$ is a function $\cdot : G \times X \to X$, written $g \cdot x$, satisfying: (i) $e \cdot x = x$ for all $x \in X$, and (ii) $(gh) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x)$ for all $g, h \in G$ and $x \in X$. Equivalently, a group action is a homomorphism $\phi : G \to \text{Sym}(X)$, where $\text{Sym}(X)$ is the group of all bijections $X \to X$.

Given an action, the **orbit** of $x \in X$ is $\text{Orb}(x) = \{g \cdot x : g \in G\}$, and the **stabilizer** of $x$ is $\text{Stab}(x) = \{g \in G : g \cdot x = x\}$. The stabilizer is a subgroup of $G$, and the **Orbit-Stabilizer Theorem** relates their sizes:

$$
|\text{Orb}(x)| \cdot |\text{Stab}(x)| = |G|
$$

The proof constructs a bijection between the orbit $\text{Orb}(x)$ and the set of left cosets $G / \text{Stab}(x)$ via $g \cdot x \leftrightarrow g \text{Stab}(x)$. Lagrange's theorem then yields the result.

The symmetric group $S_n$ acts naturally on $\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$ by $\sigma \cdot i = \sigma(i)$. The orbit of $1$ under the subgroup generated by $(1 \, 2 \, 3)$ is $\{1, 2, 3\}$, and the stabilizer of $1$ is $\{e\}$, consistent with the orbit-stabilizer theorem: $3 \cdot 1 = 3 = |\langle (1 \, 2 \, 3) \rangle|$.

**Cayley's Theorem** asserts that every group is isomorphic to a subgroup of some symmetric group. Specifically, let $G$ be a group of order $n$. Define the **left regular action** $\lambda : G \to \text{Sym}(G)$ by $\lambda(g)(h) = gh$. This is a homomorphism: $\lambda(g_1g_2)(h) = (g_1g_2)h = g_1(g_2h) = \lambda(g_1)(\lambda(g_2)(h)) = (\lambda(g_1) \circ \lambda(g_2))(h)$. The map $\lambda$ is injective: if $\lambda(g_1) = \lambda(g_2)$, then $g_1h = g_2h$ for all $h \in G$, in particular for $h = e$, yielding $g_1 = g_2$. Thus $G \cong \text{im}(\lambda) \subseteq \text{Sym}(G) \cong S_n$.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Computational group theory confronts significant complexity barriers when scaling to large groups. The fundamental decision problems—membership testing, order computation, and isomorphism testing—exhibit exponential worst-case behavior in the group's presentation size. Optimizing group-theoretic algorithms requires exploiting structural properties: abelian groups admit polynomial-time algorithms via Smith normal form; solvable groups decompose into iterated extensions by cyclic groups; and simple groups reduce to classification-theorem lookups.

For **subgroup enumeration**, naive generation of all subsets and subgroup testing requires exponential time. The **Lattice-Theoretic Optimization** exploits the fact that subgroups form a lattice under inclusion: generate subgroups incrementally by computing meets and joins, pruning redundant branches. For cyclic groups $\mathbb{Z}_n$, subgroups correspond bijectively to divisors of $n$, so enumeration reduces to integer factorization. For arbitrary finite groups, the **Schreier's Subgroup Lemma** bounds the index of subgroups: if $|G| = p_1^{e_1} \cdots p_k^{e_k}$, then subgroups of index $m$ correspond to homomorphisms $G \to S_m$, constraining the search space.

**Coset enumeration algorithms** (Todd-Coxeter) compute the index $[G : H]$ and construct the coset table for a finitely presented group. The algorithm maintains a partially defined action of generators on cosets, iteratively filling gaps by applying relators. Optimizations include **HLT strategy** (Havas-Leon-Torvonen): prioritize cosets with the most defined entries to accelerate convergence. The worst-case complexity remains exponential in the presentation length, but practical implementations handle groups of order $10^{12}$ by exploiting sparsity.

**Homomorphism computation** for finitely presented groups reduces to solving word equations in the target group. If $G = \langle x_1, \ldots, x_n \mid r_1, \ldots, r_m \rangle$ and $H$ is given computationally, a homomorphism $\phi : G \to H$ is determined by the images $\phi(x_i) \in H$, subject to the constraints $\phi(r_j) = e_H$. For abelian groups, this linearizes to a system of congruences solved via the Chinese Remainder Theorem. For nonabelian groups, constraint satisfaction often requires backtracking search.

**Failure mode**: **non-finitely-presented groups**. Not all groups admit finite presentations. The additive group $\mathbb{Q}$ of rationals has no finite generating set, and the group of continuous functions $C(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R})$ under pointwise addition is uncountable. Algorithms assuming finite presentations fail silently on such inputs, returning incorrect results or diverging. Mitigation requires type-system enforcement: restrict input types to finitely generated or finitely presented groups, rejecting general algebraic structures.

**Failure mode**: **computational indeterminacy of quotient groups**. Given a finitely presented group $G$ and a normal subgroup $N$ (specified by generators), the quotient $G/N$ may not be effectively computable. The **word problem** in $G/N$ asks whether two words represent the same coset, equivalent to testing membership in $N$. This is undecidable in general (by reduction from the word problem in $G$). Practical systems (GAP, Magma) detect undecidability and either restrict to solvable cases (nilpotent, polycyclic groups) or employ probabilistic methods with bounded error.

**Isomorphism testing** for finite groups remains an open complexity question. The best deterministic algorithms run in time $\exp(\sqrt{n \log n})$ for groups of order $n$. For specific classes, polynomial algorithms exist: abelian groups reduce to Smith normal form; $p$-groups use the Frattini subgroup. **Graph isomorphism** reduces to group isomorphism via automorphism groups, so recent quasi-polynomial-time graph isomorphism algorithms (Babai) suggest analogous improvements for group isomorphism. Practical systems use invariants (order, exponent, derived series) to prune the search space before invoking expensive canonical-form algorithms.

**Boundary case**: **trivial group homomorphisms**. The zero map $\phi : G \to H$ defined by $\phi(g) = e_H$ is always a homomorphism, regardless of $G$ and $H$. This degeneracy can mask implementation errors: a buggy homomorphism implementation that accidentally returns the identity passes trivial test cases. Robust testing requires non-degenerate inputs: verify $\phi$ on generators, check kernel and image cardinalities, and test that $\phi$ distinguishes non-identity elements.

**Boundary case**: **quotient by entire group**. The quotient $G/G$ is the trivial group $\{e\}$. All elements collapse to the single coset $G$. This edge case triggers division-by-zero errors in index calculations if $[G : G] = 1$ is hardcoded incorrectly. Defensive implementations check for $N = G$ before computing quotients.

**Optimization strategy**: **Cayley table caching**. For small groups ($|G| < 256$), precompute and cache the full multiplication table. Lookup dominates repeated multiplication, reducing group operations from $O(\log |G|)$ (for power-of-two cyclic groups) to $O(1)$. Memory cost is $O(|G|^2)$, acceptable for groups of order $\le 10^6$. For larger groups, cache only frequently used subgroup tables or rely on implicit representation via generators.

**Optimization strategy**: **Schreier-Sims algorithm for permutation groups**. Compute a **strong generating set** and **base** for a permutation group $G \subseteq S_n$ in polynomial time $O(n^5)$. The base $B = \{b_1, \ldots, b_k\}$ and stabilizer chain $G = G^{(1)} \supseteq G^{(2)} \supseteq \ldots \supseteq G^{(k+1)} = \{e\}$ enable $O(nk)$ membership testing and $O(n^2)$ group order computation via $|G| = \prod_{i=1}^k [G^{(i)} : G^{(i+1)}]$. Modern implementations (GAP's Orb package) achieve $O(n^3 \log |G|)$ by exploiting orbit structures.

**Optimization strategy**: **Lazard correspondence for $p$-groups**. For a prime $p$, the category of finite $p$-groups of nilpotency class $< p$ is equivalent to the category of finite $\mathbb{Z}_p$-Lie algebras. This correspondence linearizes group operations: compute Lie brackets instead of group commutators. The Hall polynomial encodes the class-2 structure, enabling $O(n^2)$ multiplication for class-2 groups. Extensions to higher nilpotency classes remain active research.

**Debugging protocol**: **Subgroup verification**. Claimed subgroups $H \subseteq G$ must satisfy closure and inverse closure. Compute the smallest subgroup containing $H$ via **Breadth-First Search** from generators: initialize $S = H$, then iteratively add $\{ab, a^{-1} : a, b \in S\}$ until closure. If $S \ne H$, the claim is invalid. For large groups, probabilistically sample random products and inverses, accepting with confidence $1 - \delta$ after $O(\log(1/\delta))$ trials.

**Debugging protocol**: **Homomorphism validation**. To verify $\phi : G \to H$, check $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$ for all generator pairs $(a, b)$ in $G$. If $G = \langle x_1, \ldots, x_n \rangle$, test all pairs $(x_i, x_j)$ and $(x_i, x_i^{-1})$. For finitely presented groups, additionally verify $\phi(r) = e_H$ for all relators $r$. If $\phi$ fails on any input, output the counterexample and reject.

**Resource scaling**: **memory versus computation tradeoff**. Full Cayley table storage requires $O(|G|^2)$ space but enables $O(1)$ multiplication. Generator-based representation requires $O(|G|)$ space but incurs $O(|G|)$ multiplication cost (word rewriting). For groups of order $10^6$, Cayley tables consume $10^{12}$ bytes (1 TB), prohibitive for commodity hardware. Hybrid strategies cache partial tables for frequently accessed cosets, evicting cold entries via LRU.

**Failure mitigation**: **timeout bounds on undecidable problems**. Algorithms for the word problem, conjugacy problem, and isomorphism problem do not always terminate. Production systems impose strict time limits (e.g., 60 seconds) and return "unknown" on timeout, preserving system responsiveness. For critical applications (cryptography, formal verification), restrict to decidable subclasses (free groups, nilpotent groups) where termination is guaranteed.
