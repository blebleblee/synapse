# Lesson 4: Separation Axioms, Urysohn's Lemma, Metrization

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The fundamental question that animates the study of separation axioms is both simple and profound: to what extent can a topology distinguish between distinct points, or between points and closed sets, or between disjoint closed sets? This question emerges naturally from the observation that some topologies are pathologically coarse, collapsing distinct geometric entities into indistinguishable structures, while others possess sufficient refinement to separate objects cleanly. The hierarchy of separation axioms—denoted $T_0$ through $T_4$ in the Tikhonov classification scheme—formalizes this intuitive notion of "how separated" the points and sets of a topological space can be made using only the tools provided by the open set structure.

Consider first the trivial topology on any set $X$ containing more than one point: here every pair of distinct points is topologically indistinguishable, since the only open sets are $\emptyset$ and $X$ itself. No continuous function into a Hausdorff space can separate distinct points in this topology, and indeed no meaningful geometric analysis can proceed. At the opposite extreme, metric spaces automatically satisfy strong separation properties: given any two distinct points $x \neq y$ in a metric space $(X,d)$, the open balls $B(x, d(x,y)/3)$ and $B(y, d(x,y)/3)$ provide disjoint open neighborhoods, cleanly partitioning the space around each point. The separation axioms interpolate between these extremes, establishing a hierarchy of progressively stronger requirements that capture different degrees of topological refinement.

The Kolmogorov or $T_0$ axiom represents the weakest meaningful separation property: a space $X$ is $T_0$ if for every pair of distinct points $x \neq y$, there exists an open set containing one but not the other. This minimal requirement ensures that the topology can distinguish between any two points using at least one open set, though it imposes no symmetry requirement—perhaps $x$ lies in an open set avoiding $y$, but every open set containing $y$ also contains $x$. The finite topologies studied in algebraic combinatorics, such as the Alexandrov topology on a poset where open sets correspond to upper sets, frequently satisfy $T_0$ while failing stronger separation axioms.

The Fréchet or $T_1$ axiom strengthens this by requiring symmetry: a space is $T_1$ if for every pair of distinct points $x \neq y$, each point has an open neighborhood not containing the other. Equivalently, $X$ is $T_1$ if and only if every singleton set $\{x\}$ is closed, a characterization that immediately reveals the algebraic content of the axiom. In a $T_1$ space, finite point sets are always closed, and the closure operator behaves more intuitively: $\overline{A}$ consists precisely of those points $x$ such that every open neighborhood of $x$ intersects $A$, without the pathology of accumulation points that are already in $A$ as isolated points.

The Hausdorff or $T_2$ axiom represents the first genuinely robust separation property and serves as the foundation for most of modern analysis and geometry. A space $X$ is Hausdorff if for every pair of distinct points $x \neq y$, there exist disjoint open neighborhoods $U$ of $x$ and $V$ of $y$ such that $U \cap V = \emptyset$. This axiom ensures that limits of sequences and nets are unique when they exist, that compact sets are closed, and that continuous functions into Hausdorff spaces are determined by their values on dense subsets. The real line $\mathbb{R}$ with its standard topology is Hausdorff, as is every metric space, and the majority of spaces studied in differential geometry, functional analysis, and algebraic topology satisfy this axiom automatically.

Beyond the Hausdorff property lie three progressively stronger separation axioms that govern the separation of points from closed sets and of closed sets from each other. A space is regular (or $T_3$ when combined with $T_1$) if whenever $F$ is closed and $x \notin F$, there exist disjoint open sets $U \ni x$ and $V \supseteq F$ with $U \cap V = \emptyset$. A space is normal (or $T_4$ when combined with $T_1$) if whenever $A$ and $B$ are disjoint closed sets, there exist disjoint open sets $U \supseteq A$ and $V \supseteq B$ with $U \cap V = \emptyset$. The distinction between regularity and normality captures a fundamental asymmetry: separating a point from a closed set is generally easier than separating two closed sets, and indeed there exist regular spaces that fail to be normal, demonstrating that the hierarchy is strict.

The central theorem governing these separation properties is Urysohn's lemma, a profound existential result asserting that in normal spaces, topological separation can be quantified continuously. Specifically, if $X$ is a normal space and $A, B$ are disjoint closed subsets, then there exists a continuous function $f : X \to [0,1]$ such that $f(A) = \{0\}$ and $f(B) = \{1\}$. This function interpolates smoothly between the two closed sets, assigning intermediate values to points that lie between them in a topological sense. The construction of this Urysohn function proceeds through an ingenious recursive definition of nested open sets indexed by the dyadic rationals in $[0,1]$, a technique that exploits normality at every stage to construct finer and finer separations.

Urysohn's lemma unlocks a cascade of powerful consequences. The Tietze extension theorem asserts that every continuous real-valued function defined on a closed subset of a normal space extends to a continuous function on the entire space, establishing that normal spaces possess sufficient "room" to interpolate functions without obstruction. The Urysohn metrization theorem provides a converse direction: every second-countable regular $T_3$ space is metrizable, meaning its topology arises from some metric, even if no canonical metric is initially apparent. This result transforms abstract topological problems into concrete metric geometry, where distances, convergence, and completeness can be analyzed quantitatively.

The metrization problem—determining which topological spaces admit a compatible metric—has driven much of 20th-century point-set topology. While the Urysohn metrization theorem handles the second-countable case, the Nagata–Smirnov metrization theorem extends this to the more general class of regular spaces that are paracompact, a covering property that generalizes compactness while preserving many of its useful consequences. The Bing metrization theorem characterizes metrizable spaces through the existence of a $\sigma$-discrete base, connecting metric structure to purely combinatorial properties of the open set lattice.

From a computational and algorithmic perspective, separation axioms impose definite constraints on the complexity of topological decision problems. Determining whether a finitely presented space is Hausdorff, regular, or normal can range from decidable to undecidable depending on the presentation scheme, and the construction of Urysohn functions, while existential in the abstract, admits effective approximation schemes in computationally tractable settings such as finite simplicial complexes. In theoretical computer science, domain theory exploits $T_0$ spaces (specifically, continuous lattices and Scott topologies) to model computation and recursion, while the Hausdorff property appears naturally in the semantics of concurrency and in the study of convergence structures for probabilistic computation.

The separation axioms also interact richly with other topological properties. Every compact Hausdorff space is normal, a classical theorem that follows from the tube lemma and the finite subcover property, establishing that compactness provides enough "control" to force strong separation even without assuming it axiomatically. Conversely, metrizability imposes strong constraints: every metrizable space is paracompact, normal, and first-countable, revealing that metric structure is topologically rigid. The interplay between separation and countability axioms (first-countable, second-countable, separable) forms a delicate web of implications and counterexamples that has been mapped exhaustively in reference works such as Steen and Seebach's *Counterexamples in Topology*.

Understanding the separation hierarchy requires mastery of both positive constructions and negative counterexamples. The Sorgenfrey line (the real line with the lower-limit topology generated by half-open intervals $[a,b)$) is a non-metrizable Hausdorff space that is hereditarily Lindelöf and hereditarily separable, yet whose square is not normal. The cofinite topology on an infinite set is $T_1$ but not Hausdorff. The Zariski topology on affine space $\mathbb{A}^n$ over an algebraically closed field is $T_0$ but not $T_1$ (irreducible closed sets correspond to prime ideals, and generic points are not closed), yet plays a central role in algebraic geometry precisely because its coarse structure encodes polynomial equations efficiently.

The machinery developed in this lesson—separation axioms, Urysohn's lemma, and metrization theorems—serves as the foundation for functional analysis, where the interplay between topological and linear structures depends critically on Hausdorffness and metrizability. In the theory of topological groups, subgroups and quotients inherit separation properties in controlled ways, and the existence of Haar measure on locally compact groups relies on regularity. In algebraic topology, the Hausdorff axiom ensures that homotopy equivalences and covering space theory behave cleanly, without the pathologies that arise in non-Hausdorff settings such as the line with two origins.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The internal architecture of the separation axiom hierarchy rests on precise logical relationships between topological predicates. We establish these relationships through explicit constructions and prove the key implications that connect the axioms into a coherent lattice structure.

### The Separation Axiom Hierarchy: Definitions and Immediate Implications

A topological space $(X, \mathcal{T})$ satisfies the **Kolmogorov axiom** ($T_0$) if for all distinct $x, y \in X$ with $x \neq y$, there exists an open set $U \in \mathcal{T}$ such that $U$ contains exactly one of $x$ or $y$. Formally, $X$ is $T_0$ when

$$\forall x, y \in X \colon x \neq y \implies \exists U \in \mathcal{T} \colon (x \in U \land y \notin U) \lor (y \in U \land x \notin U).$$

The $T_0$ property ensures that the topology detects distinct points but imposes no symmetry. In the specialization preorder defined by $x \leq y \iff x \in \overline{\{y\}}$, a space is $T_0$ if and only if this preorder is antisymmetric, making it a partial order. This connection makes $T_0$ spaces central to domain theory and the semantics of computation.

A space satisfies the **Fréchet axiom** ($T_1$) if for all distinct $x, y \in X$, there exist open sets $U, V \in \mathcal{T}$ with $x \in U$, $y \notin U$, $y \in V$, and $x \notin V$. Equivalently, $X$ is $T_1$ if and only if every singleton $\{x\}$ is closed. This equivalence follows immediately: if $\{x\}$ is closed, then $X \setminus \{x\}$ is an open set containing every $y \neq x$ but not $x$. Conversely, if $X$ is $T_1$, then for each $y \neq x$ there exists an open set $U_y \ni y$ with $x \notin U_y$, so $\{x\}^c = \bigcup_{y \neq x} U_y$ is open, making $\{x\}$ closed.

The $T_1$ property implies $T_0$ trivially, and the implication is strict: the Sierpiński space $\{0,1\}$ with topology $\{\emptyset, \{1\}, \{0,1\}\}$ is $T_0$ but not $T_1$, since $\{0\}$ is not closed. In a $T_1$ space, every finite set is closed (as a finite union of closed singletons), and the closure of a set $A$ consists precisely of the points $x$ such that every open neighborhood of $x$ intersects $A$, without the phenomenon of isolated points belonging to their own closures.

A space satisfies the **Hausdorff axiom** ($T_2$) if for all distinct $x, y \in X$, there exist disjoint open neighborhoods: there exist $U, V \in \mathcal{T}$ with $x \in U$, $y \in V$, and $U \cap V = \emptyset$. The Hausdorff property implies $T_1$, since if $x \neq y$ and $U, V$ are disjoint open neighborhoods of $x$ and $y$, then $U$ is an open neighborhood of $x$ not containing $y$, and vice versa. The implication is strict: the cofinite topology on an infinite set $X$ is $T_1$ but not Hausdorff, since any two nonempty open sets have cofinite complements and thus intersect.

The Hausdorff axiom has profound consequences for convergence and compactness. In a Hausdorff space, limits of sequences and nets are unique when they exist: if $x_\alpha \to x$ and $x_\alpha \to y$ in a Hausdorff space, then $x = y$. The proof is immediate: if $x \neq y$, disjoint open neighborhoods $U \ni x$ and $V \ni y$ contradict eventual membership in both. Every compact subset of a Hausdorff space is closed: if $K$ is compact and $x \notin K$, then for each $k \in K$, choose disjoint open sets $U_k \ni k$ and $V_k \ni x$. The sets $U_k$ cover $K$, so finitely many $U_{k_1}, \ldots, U_{k_n}$ cover $K$. Then $V = \bigcap_{i=1}^n V_{k_i}$ is an open neighborhood of $x$ disjoint from $K$, so $K^c$ is open.

A space is **regular** if whenever $F \subseteq X$ is closed and $x \notin F$, there exist disjoint open sets $U \ni x$ and $V \supseteq F$ with $U \cap V = \emptyset$. A space is called **$T_3$** if it is both regular and $T_0$. Many authors require $T_1$ instead of $T_0$ in the definition of $T_3$, and since regularity combined with $T_0$ implies $T_1$, the distinction is minor in practice. The key observation is that regularity provides a local separation property: neighborhoods can be "shrunk" to avoid closed sets.

Every regular $T_1$ space is Hausdorff: if $x \neq y$ in a regular $T_1$ space, then $\{y\}$ is closed (by $T_1$), so there exist disjoint open sets $U \ni x$ and $V \supseteq \{y\}$, establishing the Hausdorff property. However, the converse fails: there exist Hausdorff spaces that are not regular. The standard counterexample is the **$K$-topology** on $\mathbb{R}$, generated by the standard open sets together with sets of the form $(a,b) \setminus K$ where $K = \{1/n : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$. This space is Hausdorff but not regular: the point $0$ and the closed set $K$ cannot be separated by disjoint open sets.

A space is **normal** if whenever $A, B \subseteq X$ are disjoint closed sets, there exist disjoint open sets $U \supseteq A$ and $V \supseteq B$ with $U \cap V = \emptyset$. A space is called **$T_4$** if it is both normal and $T_1$. Normality represents a global separation property: it applies to arbitrary closed sets, not just singletons or points and closed sets. Every normal $T_1$ space is regular: if $F$ is closed and $x \notin F$, then $\{x\}$ and $F$ are disjoint closed sets (using $T_1$), so they can be separated by disjoint open sets.

The chain of implications is now complete:

$$T_4 \implies T_3 \implies T_2 \implies T_1 \implies T_0.$$

Each implication is strict, as demonstrated by standard counterexamples. Normality does not generally descend to subspaces (closed subspaces of normal spaces are normal, but arbitrary subspaces need not be), and not every regular space is normal. The Sorgenfrey plane $\mathbb{R}_\ell \times \mathbb{R}_\ell$ is Hausdorff and hereditarily Lindelöf but not normal, as the diagonal $\Delta = \{(x,x) : x \in \mathbb{R}\}$ and the antidiagonal $\Gamma = \{(x,-x) : x \in \mathbb{R}\}$ are closed but cannot be separated by disjoint open sets.

### Urysohn's Lemma: Construction of Continuous Separating Functions

Urysohn's lemma transforms the abstract topological separation afforded by normality into a concrete analytical tool: the existence of continuous real-valued functions that interpolate between disjoint closed sets. The theorem states that if $X$ is a normal space and $A, B$ are disjoint closed subsets, then there exists a continuous function $f : X \to [0,1]$ such that $f(A) = \{0\}$ and $f(B) = \{1\}$. The function $f$ is called a **Urysohn function** for the pair $(A,B)$.

The construction proceeds through an intricate recursive scheme that exploits normality at countably many stages. Let $\mathcal{D} = \{r_1, r_2, r_3, \ldots\}$ be an enumeration of the dyadic rationals in $[0,1]$—those rationals of the form $k/2^n$ for integers $0 \leq k \leq 2^n$. We construct a family of open sets $\{U_r : r \in \mathcal{D}\}$ indexed by dyadic rationals such that:

1. $A \subseteq U_r$ for all $r \in \mathcal{D}$.
2. $\overline{U_r} \cap B = \emptyset$ for all $r \in \mathcal{D}$.
3. If $r < s$ in $\mathcal{D}$, then $\overline{U_r} \subseteq U_s$.

Begin by setting $U_1 = X \setminus B$, which is open since $B$ is closed, and which contains $A$ since $A \cap B = \emptyset$. Next, apply normality to the disjoint closed sets $A$ and $X \setminus U_1 = B$ (wait, we already have $U_1$), so instead apply normality to $A$ and $B$ directly: there exist disjoint open sets $U_0 \supseteq A$ and $V_1 \supseteq B$. Set $U_1 = X \setminus B$ as before. Now apply normality to $\overline{U_0}$ and $B$: there exist disjoint open sets $W \supseteq \overline{U_0}$ and $V \supseteq B$. Set $U_{1/2}$ to be an open set containing $\overline{U_0}$ and whose closure avoids $B$.

More precisely, the recursive construction operates as follows. Initialize with $r_1 = 1$, and set $U_1 = X \setminus B$. This satisfies $A \subseteq U_1$ and $\overline{U_1} \cap B = \emptyset$ (since $\overline{U_1} \subseteq X$ and $U_1 \cap B = \emptyset$, so $B \subseteq X \setminus U_1$ closed, hence $B \subseteq X \setminus \overline{U_1}$ is not automatic; we need to be more careful).

Let us restart with the correct construction. Set $U_1 = X \setminus B$, which is open and contains $A$. Note that $\overline{U_1}$ may intersect $B$, so we need a different approach. Instead, use normality directly: since $A$ and $B$ are disjoint closed sets, there exist disjoint open sets $U_0 \supseteq A$ and $V_0 \supseteq B$ with $U_0 \cap V_0 = \emptyset$. Since $V_0$ is open and $B \subseteq V_0$, we have $\overline{U_0} \cap V_0 = \emptyset$ would imply $\overline{U_0} \subseteq X \setminus V_0$, which is closed, but this need not hold. The issue is that $\overline{U_0}$ may intersect $V_0$.

The correct initialization is this: since $X$ is normal and $A, B$ are disjoint closed sets, there exist disjoint open sets $U'_0 \supseteq A$ and $W'_1 \supseteq B$. Now apply normality again to $\overline{U'_0}$ and $B$: these are closed (closure is closed, $B$ is closed) and disjoint (since $\overline{U'_0} \subseteq X \setminus W'_1$... no, this isn't immediate either).

The cleanest approach uses the following lemma: in a normal space, if $A$ is closed and $U \supseteq A$ is open, then there exists an open set $V$ with $A \subseteq V \subseteq \overline{V} \subseteq U$. To prove this, note that $A$ and $X \setminus U$ are disjoint closed sets, so by normality there exist disjoint open sets $V \supseteq A$ and $W \supseteq X \setminus U$. Then $\overline{V} \cap W = \emptyset$, so $\overline{V} \subseteq X \setminus W$. Since $X \setminus U \subseteq W$, we have $X \setminus W \subseteq U$, hence $\overline{V} \subseteq U$.

Now the construction proceeds cleanly. Start with $U_1 = X \setminus B$ (open, $A \subseteq U_1$, $B \subseteq X \setminus U_1$, so $U_1 \cap B = \emptyset$). Apply the lemma to $A$ (closed) and $U_1$ (open, $A \subseteq U_1$): there exists an open set $U_0$ with $A \subseteq U_0 \subseteq \overline{U_0} \subseteq U_1$. In particular, $\overline{U_0} \subseteq U_1 = X \setminus B$, so $\overline{U_0} \cap B = \emptyset$. Also, $\overline{U_0} \subseteq U_1$ implies $U_0 \subseteq \overline{U_0} \subseteq U_1$.

Now recursively construct $U_r$ for dyadic rationals. Suppose we have constructed $U_r$ for all dyadic rationals $r$ of the form $k/2^n$ with $n < m$, satisfying the three properties. Consider a dyadic rational $s = k/2^m$ not yet assigned. Let $r_-$ be the largest dyadic rational less than $s$ for which $U_{r_-}$ has been constructed, and let $r_+$ be the smallest dyadic rational greater than $s$ for which $U_{r_+}$ has been constructed (such rationals exist by the density of constructed dyadics and the fact that $0, 1$ are in the construction). We have $\overline{U_{r_-}} \subseteq U_{r_+}$ by the inductive hypothesis.

Apply the lemma to $\overline{U_{r_-}}$ (closed) and $U_{r_+}$ (open, $\overline{U_{r_-}} \subseteq U_{r_+}$): there exists an open set $U_s$ with $\overline{U_{r_-}} \subseteq U_s \subseteq \overline{U_s} \subseteq U_{r_+}$. This preserves the three properties: $A \subseteq U_0 \subseteq \overline{U_0} \subseteq U_{r_-} \subseteq \overline{U_{r_-}} \subseteq U_s$, so $A \subseteq U_s$. Also, $\overline{U_s} \subseteq U_{r_+} \subseteq U_1 = X \setminus B$, so $\overline{U_s} \cap B = \emptyset$. Finally, if $t < s$ is dyadic with $U_t$ constructed, then either $t \leq r_-$ (so $\overline{U_t} \subseteq \overline{U_{r_-}} \subseteq U_s$) or $r_- < t < s$ (which is impossible by the maximality of $r_-$). Similarly, if $s < t$ is dyadic with $U_t$ constructed, then $r_+ \leq t$, so $\overline{U_s} \subseteq U_{r_+} \subseteq U_t$.

After constructing $U_r$ for all dyadic rationals in $[0,1]$, define $f : X \to [0,1]$ by

$$f(x) = \inf\{r \in \mathcal{D} : x \in U_r\}.$$

For $x \in A$, we have $x \in U_r$ for all $r \in \mathcal{D}$ (since $A \subseteq U_r$ for all $r$), so $f(x) = \inf \mathcal{D} = 0$. For $x \in B$, we have $x \notin \overline{U_r}$ for all $r \in \mathcal{D}$, which implies $x \notin U_r$ (since $U_r \subseteq \overline{U_r}$... no, $U_r \subseteq \overline{U_r}$ is true but doesn't give $x \notin U_r$). Instead, note that if $x \in B$ and $x \in U_r$, then $x \in \overline{U_r}$ (since $U_r \subseteq \overline{U_r}$), contradicting $\overline{U_r} \cap B = \emptyset$. Thus $x \in B$ implies $x \notin U_r$ for all $r \in \mathcal{D}$, so $f(x) = \inf \emptyset = 1$ (by convention, the infimum of the empty set of reals bounded below is the supremum of the lower bounds, which is $1$ in $[0,1]$).

Continuity of $f$ follows from the open set characterization. For $a < b$ in $[0,1]$, consider $f^{-1}([0,a))$ and $f^{-1}((b,1])$. We have $f(x) < a$ if and only if there exists $r \in \mathcal{D}$ with $r < a$ and $x \in U_r$ (since $f(x) = \inf\{r : x \in U_r\}$). Thus $f^{-1}([0,a)) = \bigcup_{r \in \mathcal{D}, r < a} U_r$, which is open as a union of open sets. Similarly, $f(x) > b$ if and only if $x \notin U_r$ for all $r \in \mathcal{D}$ with $r \leq b$, which means $x \notin \bigcup_{r \in \mathcal{D}, r \leq b} \overline{U_r}$... but this is not obviously open. Instead, use the fact that $f(x) > b$ if and only if for all $r \in \mathcal{D}$ with $r \leq b$, we have $x \notin U_r$. Equivalently, $x \in \bigcap_{r \in \mathcal{D}, r \leq b} (X \setminus U_r)$... which is an intersection of closed sets, hence closed, so $f^{-1}((b,1])$ is closed. But we want to show $f$ is continuous, so we need $f^{-1}((b,1])$ to be... well, the preimage of a closed set should be closed, which is what we have.

Alternatively, verify continuity directly by showing preimages of basis elements are open. The sets $[0,a)$ and $(b,1]$ for $a, b \in [0,1]$ form a subbasis for the standard topology on $[0,1]$. We computed $f^{-1}([0,a)) = \bigcup_{r < a, r \in \mathcal{D}} U_r$, which is open. For $f^{-1}((b,1])$, note that $f(x) > b$ if and only if $x \notin U_s$ for all $s \in \mathcal{D}$ with $s \leq b$. Choose dyadic rationals $s_n \to b$ from above, i.e., $s_n > b$ and $s_n \searrow b$. Then $f(x) > b$ if and only if $x \notin U_{s_n}$ for some $n$ (since if $x \in U_s$ for some $s > b$, then... actually, this is getting complicated).

The cleanest proof of continuity uses the characterization that $f^{-1}((c, \infty)) = \bigcup_{r > c, r \in \mathcal{D}} (X \setminus \overline{U_r})$ and $f^{-1}((-\infty, c)) = \bigcup_{r < c, r \in \mathcal{D}} U_r$, both of which are open. Since intervals of these forms generate the topology on $\mathbb{R}$, the function $f$ is continuous. Verifying these preimage formulas is an exercise in unwinding the definition of $f$ and the properties of $U_r$.

The Urysohn function $f$ achieves $f(A) = \{0\}$ and $f(B) = \{1\}$, and takes all intermediate values in $[0,1]$ depending on the position of points relative to the nested family $U_r$. This construction is the canonical example of a transfinite recursive definition driven by a countable dense subset, and the technique generalizes to numerous other existence theorems in topology and analysis.

### Consequences: Tietze Extension Theorem and Complete Regularity

The existence of Urysohn functions immediately implies a rich supply of continuous real-valued functions on normal spaces, which in turn enables functional-analytic techniques. The **Tietze extension theorem** asserts that every continuous function $g : A \to \mathbb{R}$ defined on a closed subset $A$ of a normal space $X$ extends to a continuous function $\tilde{g} : X \to \mathbb{R}$. If $g$ is bounded, say $g : A \to [a,b]$, then the extension $\tilde{g}$ can be chosen to satisfy $\tilde{g}(X) \subseteq [a,b]$ as well.

The proof proceeds by iterative approximation. Assume without loss of generality that $g : A \to [-1, 1]$. Define $A_+ = g^{-1}([1/3, 1])$ and $A_- = g^{-1}([-1, -1/3])$, which are closed in $A$, hence closed in $X$ (since $A$ is closed in $X$). These sets are disjoint, so by Urysohn's lemma there exists a continuous function $f_1 : X \to [-1/3, 1/3]$ with $f_1(A_+) = \{1/3\}$ and $f_1(A_-) = \{-1/3\}$. Define $g_1 = g - f_1|_A : A \to \mathbb{R}$. Then $|g_1(a)| \leq 2/3$ for all $a \in A$: if $g(a) \geq 1/3$, then $f_1(a) = 1/3$, so $g_1(a) = g(a) - 1/3 \leq 1 - 1/3 = 2/3$; if $g(a) \leq -1/3$, then $f_1(a) = -1/3$, so $g_1(a) = g(a) + 1/3 \geq -1 + 1/3 = -2/3$; and if $-1/3 < g(a) < 1/3$, then $|f_1(a)| \leq 1/3$, so $|g_1(a)| \leq |g(a)| + |f_1(a)| \leq 1/3 + 1/3 = 2/3$.

Repeat this process: apply the construction to $g_1$ (scaled to $[-1,1]$ if necessary) to obtain $f_2 : X \to [-2/9, 2/9]$ such that $g_2 = g_1 - f_2|_A$ satisfies $|g_2(a)| \leq (2/3)^2$ for all $a \in A$. Continuing, construct $f_n : X \to [-(2/3)^{n-1}/3, (2/3)^{n-1}/3]$ such that $g_n = g_{n-1} - f_n|_A$ satisfies $|g_n(a)| \leq (2/3)^n$.

Define $\tilde{g} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n$. The series converges uniformly on $X$ since $|f_n(x)| \leq (2/3)^{n-1}/3$ and $\sum (2/3)^{n-1}/3 = (1/3) \cdot 3 = 1 < \infty$. Thus $\tilde{g} : X \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous (uniform limit of continuous functions). For $a \in A$, we have $g(a) = f_1(a) + g_1(a) = f_1(a) + f_2(a) + g_2(a) = \cdots = \sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n(a)$ (since $g_n(a) \to 0$), so $\tilde{g}(a) = g(a)$.

The Tietze extension theorem has numerous applications: proving that $C(X)$, the space of continuous real-valued functions on a compact Hausdorff space $X$, separates points and closed sets; establishing the existence of partitions of unity subordinate to open covers in paracompact spaces; and enabling the use of functional-analytic techniques (Riesz representation theorem, duality theory) in topological settings.

A space $X$ is **completely regular** (or **Tychonoff**) if it is $T_1$ and for every closed set $F$ and point $x \notin F$, there exists a continuous function $f : X \to [0,1]$ with $f(x) = 0$ and $f(F) = \{1\}$. Complete regularity lies strictly between regularity and normality: every completely regular space is regular (separate $x$ and $F$ using $f^{-1}([0, 1/3))$ and $f^{-1}((2/3, 1])$), and every normal space is completely regular (by Urysohn's lemma). However, there exist completely regular spaces that are not normal.

### The Urysohn Metrization Theorem: Embedding into Hilbert Cubes

The culminating result of this lesson is the **Urysohn metrization theorem**, which asserts that every second-countable regular $T_3$ space is metrizable. This theorem transforms abstract topological properties—countability and separation—into concrete metric geometry. The proof proceeds by embedding the space into the Hilbert cube $[0,1]^\mathbb{N}$ with its product topology, which is metrizable, and showing that the subspace topology induced on the image coincides with the original topology.

Let $X$ be a second-countable regular $T_3$ space. Let $\mathcal{B} = \{B_n : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$ be a countable basis for the topology. For each $n$, and each $x \in B_n$, the set $\overline{\{x\}}$ is closed (by $T_1$, which follows from regular $T_3$) and contained in $B_n$ (wait, not necessarily; we need to be more careful). Instead, use regularity: for each $x \in B_n$, there exists an open set $U$ with $x \in U \subseteq \overline{U} \subseteq B_n$ (by regularity applied to the closed set $X \setminus B_n$ and point $x \notin X \setminus B_n$). Since $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis, there exists $B_m \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B_m \subseteq U \subseteq \overline{U} \subseteq B_n$, so $x \in B_m \subseteq \overline{B_m} \subseteq B_n$.

Thus for each $n$, and each $x \in B_n$, there exists $m$ (depending on $x$ and $n$) such that $x \in B_m \subseteq \overline{B_m} \subseteq B_n$. Enumerate all pairs $(n, m)$ such that $\overline{B_m} \subseteq B_n$ as $(n_k, m_k)$ for $k \in \mathbb{N}$. For each $k$, apply Urysohn's lemma (which applies because $X$ is normal, a consequence of regular $T_3$ plus second-countable... wait, regular plus second-countable implies normal? No, that's not generally true. The issue is that we need $X$ to be normal to apply Urysohn's lemma, but we only have regularity.

The resolution is that we use regularity to construct Urysohn-like functions directly. For a regular space and closed set $F$, point $x \notin F$, we get disjoint open sets $U \ni x$ and $V \supseteq F$, but not necessarily a continuous function. However, for regular spaces we can construct the function differently: we need to ensure normality or use a weaker construction.

Actually, the correct approach is to note that every second-countable regular space is normal. This is a standard theorem: if $X$ is regular and second-countable, then $X$ is Lindelöf (every open cover has a countable subcover), and every regular Lindelöf space is normal. The proof proceeds by taking disjoint closed sets $A, B$, and for each $a \in A$, using regularity to find open $U_a \ni a$ and $V_a \supseteq B$ disjoint. The sets $U_a$ cover $A$, and since $X$ is Lindelöf, countably many suffice. Similarly for $B$. Then construct disjoint open sets around $A$ and $B$ using a "shrinking" argument.

Alternatively, proceed directly with the embedding. For each pair $(n, m)$ with $\overline{B_m} \subseteq B_n$, apply Urysohn's lemma (using normality of $X$, which follows from second-countable regular) to the disjoint closed sets $X \setminus B_n$ and $\overline{B_m}$: there exists a continuous function $f_{n,m} : X \to [0,1]$ with $f_{n,m}(X \setminus B_n) = \{0\}$ and $f_{n,m}(\overline{B_m}) = \{1\}$.

Enumerate these functions as $f_1, f_2, f_3, \ldots$ and define the embedding $\Phi : X \to [0,1]^\mathbb{N}$ by $\Phi(x) = (f_1(x), f_2(x), f_3(x), \ldots)$. Each coordinate function $f_k$ is continuous, so $\Phi$ is continuous (by the definition of the product topology). To show $\Phi$ is an embedding, we must show $\Phi$ is injective and a homeomorphism onto its image.

Injectivity follows from the separation property: if $x \neq y$, then since $X$ is Hausdorff (a consequence of regular $T_1$), there exist disjoint open sets $U \ni x$ and $V \ni y$. Since $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis, there exist $B_n, B_m \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B_n \subseteq U$ and $y \in B_m \subseteq V$. By regularity, there exists $B_p$ with $x \in B_p \subseteq \overline{B_p} \subseteq B_n$, so $(n, p)$ is one of the enumerated pairs and $f_{n,p}(x) = 1$ while $f_{n,p}(y) = 0$ (since $y \notin B_n$). Thus $\Phi(x) \neq \Phi(y)$.

To show $\Phi$ is a homeomorphism onto its image, we must show that for every open set $U$ in $X$, the set $\Phi(U)$ is open in $\Phi(X)$ with the subspace topology. Equivalently, for every $x \in U$, there exists a basis open set in the product topology that contains $\Phi(x)$ and is contained in $\Phi(U)$. Since $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis, there exists $B_n$ with $x \in B_n \subseteq U$. By regularity, there exists $B_m$ with $x \in B_m \subseteq \overline{B_m} \subseteq B_n \subseteq U$, so $(n, m)$ is an enumerated pair. Then $f_{n,m}(x) = 1$ and $f_{n,m}(X \setminus U) = \{0\}$ (since $X \setminus U \subseteq X \setminus B_n$).

Consider the basis open set $W = \pi_k^{-1}((1/2, 1]) \cap [0,1]^\mathbb{N}$ where $k$ is the index such that $f_k = f_{n,m}$. Then $\Phi(x) \in W$ (since $f_k(x) = 1 > 1/2$), and $\Phi^{-1}(W) = f_k^{-1}((1/2, 1]) = \{z \in X : f_k(z) > 1/2\} \subseteq B_m \subseteq U$ (since $f_k = 1$ on $\overline{B_m}$ and $f_k = 0$ outside $B_n$, so $f_k > 1/2$ implies... actually, $f_k$ is continuous and takes value $1$ on $\overline{B_m}$ and $0$ on $X \setminus B_n$, so by continuity $f_k^{-1}((1/2, 1])$ is open and contains $\overline{B_m}$, but we need to show it's contained in $B_n$).

The key is that $f_k = 0$ on $X \setminus B_n$, so $f_k^{-1}((1/2, 1]) \subseteq B_n$. Thus $\Phi^{-1}(W) \subseteq B_n \subseteq U$, so $W \cap \Phi(X) \subseteq \Phi(U)$. This shows $\Phi(U)$ is open in the subspace topology on $\Phi(X)$.

Since $[0,1]^\mathbb{N}$ with the product topology is metrizable (via the metric $d((x_n), (y_n)) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty 2^{-n} |x_n - y_n|$, which induces the product topology), and $\Phi(X)$ is a subspace, the subspace topology on $\Phi(X)$ is also metrizable. Since $\Phi : X \to \Phi(X)$ is a homeomorphism, $X$ is metrizable.

The Urysohn metrization theorem is optimal in a sense: second-countability cannot be weakened to separability without additional hypotheses, and regularity cannot be weakened to Hausdorff (there exist second-countable Hausdorff spaces that are not metrizable). The theorem also highlights the power of functional separation: the ability to construct continuous real-valued functions (via regularity and Urysohn's lemma) suffices to embed abstract topological spaces into concrete metric spaces.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Working effectively with separation axioms, Urysohn functions, and metrization theorems requires understanding the edge cases where standard intuitions fail, the boundary conditions where implications reverse or become independent, and the computational strategies for verifying or constructing separating functions in practice.

### Counterexamples and Strictness of the Separation Hierarchy

The separation axiom hierarchy is strict in the sense that each implication $T_{n+1} \implies T_n$ cannot be reversed. Constructing explicit counterexamples that demonstrate these gaps is essential for developing intuition about which properties follow from which axioms.

The Sierpiński space $S = \{0, 1\}$ with topology $\{\emptyset, \{1\}, \{0,1\}\}$ is $T_0$ but not $T_1$: the set $\{0\}$ is not open, and there is no open set containing $0$ but not $1$ (since the only open set containing $0$ is $\{0,1\}$, which also contains $1$). This space appears ubiquitously in domain theory as the simplest non-trivial $T_0$ space, and its function space structure models computation.

The cofinite topology on an infinite set $X$ (where open sets are $\emptyset$ and sets with finite complement) is $T_1$ but not Hausdorff: every singleton is closed (its complement is cofinite, hence open), but any two nonempty open sets intersect (since their complements are finite, the intersection of two cofinite sets is cofinite, hence nonempty). This topology is compact but non-Hausdorff, demonstrating that compactness alone does not imply separation properties.

The $K$-topology on $\mathbb{R}$ (generated by the standard open sets plus sets of the form $(a,b) \setminus K$ where $K = \{1/n : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$) is Hausdorff but not regular: points are separated, but the closed set $K$ and the point $0$ cannot be separated by disjoint open sets. Every open set containing $0$ in the $K$-topology contains a standard open interval $(-\epsilon, \epsilon)$, which intersects every open set containing $K$ (since every such set must avoid $K$ "from both sides," but the topology has insufficient structure to do so cleanly).

The Sorgenfrey line $\mathbb{R}_\ell$ (the real line with the topology generated by half-open intervals $[a, b)$) is Hausdorff, regular, and even hereditarily normal (every subspace is normal). However, its square $\mathbb{R}_\ell \times \mathbb{R}_\ell$ (with the product topology) is not normal: the diagonal $\Delta = \{(x,x) : x \in \mathbb{R}\}$ and the antidiagonal $\Gamma = \{(x, -x) : x \in \mathbb{R}\}$ are closed but cannot be separated by disjoint open sets. This example shows that normality is not preserved under finite products, a striking failure of robustness.

The Niemytzki plane (also called Moore plane) consists of the upper half-plane $\{(x,y) : y > 0\}$ with the Euclidean topology, together with the $x$-axis $\{(x, 0) : x \in \mathbb{R}\}$ where a neighborhood of $(a, 0)$ consists of $(a,0)$ together with an open disk tangent to the $x$-axis at $(a, 0)$. This space is Hausdorff and regular but not normal: the closed set consisting of the $x$-axis and the closed set consisting of points $(n, 1/n)$ for $n \in \mathbb{N}$ cannot be separated.

Understanding these counterexamples requires careful verification of open set containments and closure properties. In computational topology and formal verification, these counterexamples serve as test cases: automated theorem provers must handle the delicate distinctions between $T_2$ and $T_3$, and between $T_3$ and $T_4$.

### Computational Construction of Urysohn Functions

In finite simplicial complexes, finite CW complexes, and other combinatorially tractable topological spaces, Urysohn functions can be constructed algorithmically. The key observation is that normality for finite spaces reduces to a combinatorial condition on the partially ordered set of closed sets, and the recursive construction of $U_r$ becomes a finite algorithm.

For a finite $T_1$ space $X$ (so every singleton is closed), normality is equivalent to the existence of disjoint open neighborhoods for every pair of disjoint closed sets. Since $X$ is finite, the lattice of open sets is finite, and one can check normality by exhaustive search over all pairs of disjoint closed sets. If $X$ is normal, the Urysohn function construction can be implemented directly: enumerate dyadic rationals, construct the nested open sets $U_r$ using the finite lattice of open sets, and define $f(x) = \inf\{r : x \in U_r\}$.

For simplicial complexes, Urysohn functions arise naturally from piecewise-linear interpolation. If $K$ is a simplicial complex embedded in $\mathbb{R}^n$, and $A, B$ are disjoint closed subsets of the geometric realization $|K|$, one can construct a piecewise-linear function $f : |K| \to [0,1]$ with $f(A) = 0$ and $f(B) = 1$ by setting values at vertices and extending linearly over simplices. The resulting function is continuous in the CW topology and provides an explicit, computable Urysohn function.

In the context of domain theory and Scott topologies on partially ordered sets, Urysohn-like separation is achieved through order-theoretic constructions. For a continuous lattice $L$ with the Scott topology, separating a point from a closed set reduces to finding order-theoretic witnesses (elements that separate via the order relation), and the analog of Urysohn's lemma becomes a statement about the density of continuous real-valued Scott-continuous functions.

### Metrization Beyond Second-Countability: Nagata–Smirnov and Bing Theorems

The Urysohn metrization theorem applies to second-countable regular spaces, but many topological spaces of interest—particularly infinite-dimensional function spaces and non-separable spaces—fail to be second-countable. The Nagata–Smirnov metrization theorem extends the Urysohn result to the broader class of regular spaces that are paracompact.

A space $X$ is **paracompact** if every open cover admits a locally finite refinement: given any open cover $\mathcal{U}$, there exists an open cover $\mathcal{V}$ such that each $V \in \mathcal{V}$ is contained in some $U \in \mathcal{U}$, and every point $x \in X$ has a neighborhood that intersects only finitely many sets in $\mathcal{V}$. Paracompactness generalizes compactness (every finite subcover property implies local finiteness) and is preserved under closed subspaces and certain products.

The **Nagata–Smirnov metrization theorem** asserts that a topological space is metrizable if and only if it is regular and has a $\sigma$-locally finite basis: a basis $\mathcal{B} = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \mathcal{B}_n$ where each $\mathcal{B}_n$ is locally finite (every point has a neighborhood intersecting only finitely many sets in $\mathcal{B}_n$). The proof embeds the space into a product of countably many metric spaces using partitions of unity subordinate to locally finite covers.

The **Bing metrization theorem** provides a different characterization: a space is metrizable if and only if it is regular and has a $\sigma$-discrete basis: a basis $\mathcal{B} = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty \mathcal{B}_n$ where each $\mathcal{B}_n$ is discrete (every point has a neighborhood intersecting at most one set in $\mathcal{B}_n$). This theorem is particularly useful in infinite-dimensional topology and the theory of Banach spaces, where bases with discrete structure arise naturally.

These metrization theorems reveal that the essence of metrizability lies in combinatorial properties of the open set lattice—local finiteness and discreteness—rather than global countability. From a computational perspective, verifying paracompactness or the existence of $\sigma$-locally finite bases is generally undecidable for finitely presented spaces, but in specific concrete cases (such as CW complexes or spaces with explicit atlas structures), these properties can be verified through finite computations.

### Failure Modes: Non-Normal Regular Spaces and Non-Metrizable Manifolds

Understanding when separation properties fail is crucial for diagnosing topological pathologies. A regular space that fails to be normal cannot support the full power of Urysohn's lemma and Tietze extension: there exist disjoint closed sets $A, B$ with no continuous function separating them. This failure obstructs functional-analytic techniques and prevents the construction of partitions of unity, rendering many standard tools of analysis unavailable.

The classic example is the Sorgenfrey plane, where the failure of normality arises from the interaction between the product topology and the unusual base topology. In this space, attempting to separate the diagonal and antidiagonal leads to a contradiction: any open set containing the diagonal must contain "strips" around each point, and these strips inevitably intersect any open set containing the antidiagonal.

In algebraic geometry, the Zariski topology on $\mathbb{A}^n$ is $T_0$ but not $T_1$ (generic points corresponding to prime ideals are not closed), and not Hausdorff (the only way to separate points would contradict irreducibility of closed sets). This failure of separation is essential for encoding polynomial constraints efficiently: the coarse topology captures algebraic dependencies that a finer Hausdorff topology would obscure.

Non-metrizable spaces arise naturally in functional analysis: the weak topology on an infinite-dimensional Banach space $X$ (the coarsest topology making all linear functionals in $X^*$ continuous) is Hausdorff but not metrizable when $X$ is infinite-dimensional. The failure of metrizability reflects the fact that weak convergence cannot be characterized by a single metric, and the topology requires examining convergence with respect to all functionals simultaneously.

In the theory of topological groups, locally compact groups that are not metrizable (such as the product $\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}$ with pointwise addition) appear naturally. These groups are regular and Hausdorff but fail second-countability, and their topology encodes the interaction between group operations and convergence in a way that resists metric description.

### Verification Strategies and Proof Techniques

Verifying that a given space satisfies a separation axiom or constructing explicit Urysohn functions requires systematic proof techniques tailored to the axiom's structure.

For $T_0$ and $T_1$, verification reduces to checking closure properties: enumerate all singletons and verify that each is closed (for $T_1$) or that for each pair of distinct points, the closure of one does not contain the other (for $T_0$). In finite spaces, this is a finite computation. In infinite spaces, one typically argues by contradiction using the defining axiom of the topology.

For Hausdorff, the standard technique is to construct explicit disjoint open neighborhoods. In metric spaces, use open balls of radius less than $d(x,y)/3$. In product spaces, use the product of open neighborhoods in each factor. In quotient spaces, verify that the equivalence relation is closed (the graph $R = \{(x,y) : x \sim y\}$ is closed in $X \times X$), which ensures that the quotient is Hausdorff when $X$ is.

For regularity, use local constructions: given $x \notin F$ closed, find an open $U \ni x$ with $\overline{U} \subseteq X \setminus F$. In metric spaces, this is automatic. In more general spaces, exploit the basis structure or use specific properties of the topology (such as locally compact Hausdorff spaces being regular).

For normality, the standard technique is to construct disjoint open neighborhoods using induction or transfinite recursion. In compact Hausdorff spaces, for disjoint closed $A, B$, use the Hausdorff property to construct disjoint open neighborhoods around each point of $A$ and each point of $B$, then apply compactness to extract finitely many neighborhoods covering $A$ and $B$ separately. In paracompact spaces, use partitions of unity subordinate to covers separating $A$ and $B$.

Constructing explicit Urysohn functions follows the recursive algorithm described earlier: enumerate dyadic rationals, build nested open sets using normality, and define $f(x) = \inf\{r : x \in U_r\}$. In concrete cases (finite spaces, simplicial complexes), this construction is algorithmic. In abstract settings, the existence is non-constructive, relying on the axiom of choice through the enumeration of dyadic rationals and the iterative application of normality.

Verifying metrizability requires either constructing an explicit metric or applying a metrization theorem. For second-countable regular spaces, the Urysohn embedding into the Hilbert cube provides a canonical construction. For paracompact regular spaces, the Nagata–Smirnov theorem applies, requiring verification of the existence of a $\sigma$-locally finite basis. For spaces arising from manifolds, atlases, or other geometric structures, explicit metrics can often be written down using Riemannian geometry or coordinate charts.

The interaction between separation axioms and other topological properties—compactness, connectedness, completeness—provides a rich source of optimization strategies. Compact Hausdorff spaces are automatically normal, so Urysohn's lemma applies without separate verification. Complete metric spaces support powerful analytical tools (Baire category theorem, Banach fixed point theorem) that leverage both metrizability and completeness. Understanding these interactions allows the topologist to select the minimal hypotheses needed for a given theorem and to construct counterexamples that delineate the boundaries of validity precisely.
