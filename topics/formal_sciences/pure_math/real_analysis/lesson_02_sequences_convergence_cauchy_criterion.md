# Lesson 2: Sequences in ℝ — Convergence, Cauchy Criterion, Subsequences

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The study of sequences forms the foundational infrastructure upon which all of real analysis is constructed. Where calculus provides intuitive machinery for differentiation and integration, real analysis demands rigorous verification of limiting behavior through quantified proximity arguments. A sequence $(a_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ in $\mathbb{R}$ is simply a function $f : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$, assigning to each positive integer $n$ a real number $a_n$. The central question animating this lesson is deceptively simple: when does a sequence approach a limiting value, and how do we formalize the notion of "approach" without relying on vague geometric intuition or hand-waving about "getting arbitrarily close"?

The epsilon-delta machinery that Cauchy and Weierstrass developed in the nineteenth century provides the answer. A sequence $(a_n)$ converges to a limit $L \in \mathbb{R}$ if, for every tolerance threshold $\varepsilon > 0$, no matter how microscopically small, we can identify a stage $N \in \mathbb{N}$ beyond which all subsequent terms lie within an $\varepsilon$-neighborhood of $L$. Formally, we write $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L$ if and only if for all $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all $n \ge N$ we have $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$. This definition encodes the intuitive notion of "eventually staying close" into a precise logical quantifier structure: universal quantification over tolerances, followed by existential witness of a threshold index, followed by universal verification beyond that threshold.

The power of this definition lies in its constructive verifiability. Given a proposed limit $L$ and a challenge tolerance $\varepsilon$, we must produce an explicit threshold $N(\varepsilon)$ as a function of $\varepsilon$. This is not a probabilistic statement or an asymptotic heuristic—it is a deterministic, quantified assertion that can be verified algebraically. The definition immediately yields several critical invariants. First, limits are unique: if $(a_n)$ converges to both $L$ and $M$, then $L = M$. The proof is direct: assume $L \ne M$ and set $\varepsilon = |L - M|/2 > 0$. By convergence to $L$, there exists $N_1$ such that $n \ge N_1$ implies $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$. By convergence to $M$, there exists $N_2$ such that $n \ge N_2$ implies $|a_n - M| < \varepsilon$. Let $N = \max\{N_1, N_2\}$. Then for $n \ge N$, the triangle inequality gives $|L - M| \le |L - a_n| + |a_n - M| < 2\varepsilon = |L - M|$, a contradiction. Hence $L = M$.

Second, convergent sequences are bounded. If $(a_n) \to L$, then for $\varepsilon = 1$ there exists $N$ such that $n \ge N$ implies $|a_n - L| < 1$, hence $|a_n| < |L| + 1$ for $n \ge N$. The sequence is therefore bounded by $M = \max\{|a_1|, |a_2|, \ldots, |a_{N-1}|, |L| + 1\}$. This result is foundational: unbounded sequences cannot converge, though boundedness alone is insufficient for convergence (consider $(a_n) = (-1)^n$, which is bounded but oscillates).

Third, the algebra of limits respects the field operations. If $(a_n) \to L$ and $(b_n) \to M$, then $(a_n + b_n) \to L + M$, $(a_n b_n) \to LM$, and if $M \ne 0$ and $b_n \ne 0$ for all $n$, then $(a_n / b_n) \to L/M$. These results follow from careful epsilon management using the triangle inequality and field axiom manipulations. For instance, to prove $(a_n + b_n) \to L + M$, let $\varepsilon > 0$. Choose $N_1$ such that $n \ge N_1$ implies $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon/2$, and $N_2$ such that $n \ge N_2$ implies $|b_n - M| < \varepsilon/2$. Let $N = \max\{N_1, N_2\}$. Then for $n \ge N$, we have $|(a_n + b_n) - (L + M)| \le |a_n - L| + |b_n - M| < \varepsilon/2 + \varepsilon/2 = \varepsilon$, as required.

The monotone convergence theorem provides a powerful bridge between order structure and topological convergence. A sequence $(a_n)$ is monotone increasing if $a_n \le a_{n+1}$ for all $n$, and monotone decreasing if $a_n \ge a_{n+1}$ for all $n$. The theorem states that every bounded monotone sequence converges. The proof leverages the completeness axiom of $\mathbb{R}$, specifically the least upper bound property. Suppose $(a_n)$ is monotone increasing and bounded above. Let $L = \sup\{a_n : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$, which exists by the completeness axiom. Given $\varepsilon > 0$, the number $L - \varepsilon$ is not an upper bound for the set $\{a_n\}$, so there exists $N$ such that $a_N > L - \varepsilon$. Since $(a_n)$ is increasing, for all $n \ge N$ we have $L - \varepsilon < a_N \le a_n \le L < L + \varepsilon$, hence $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$. Thus $(a_n) \to L = \sup_n a_n$. The analogous result holds for bounded decreasing sequences, which converge to their infimum. This theorem is the workhorse for establishing convergence of recursively defined sequences, iteration schemes, and fixed-point algorithms.

The Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem asserts that every bounded sequence in $\mathbb{R}$ contains a convergent subsequence. A subsequence $(a_{n_k})_{k=1}^\infty$ is obtained by selecting an increasing sequence of indices $n_1 < n_2 < n_3 < \ldots$ and considering the terms $a_{n_k}$. The proof of Bolzano-Weierstrass proceeds by bisection. Suppose $(a_n)$ is bounded, say $a_n \in [a, b]$ for all $n$. Bisect $[a, b]$ into $[a, (a+b)/2]$ and $[(a+b)/2, b]$. At least one of these intervals contains infinitely many terms of the sequence; call it $I_1$. Select an index $n_1$ such that $a_{n_1} \in I_1$. Now bisect $I_1$ and repeat: one of the halves, call it $I_2$, contains infinitely many terms. Choose $n_2 > n_1$ such that $a_{n_2} \in I_2$. Continuing inductively, we construct nested intervals $I_1 \supseteq I_2 \supseteq I_3 \supseteq \ldots$ with lengths $|I_k| = (b - a)/2^k \to 0$, and a subsequence $(a_{n_k})$ with $a_{n_k} \in I_k$ for all $k$. By the nested interval theorem (a corollary of completeness), there exists a unique $L \in \bigcap_{k=1}^\infty I_k$. Given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $K$ such that $|I_K| < \varepsilon$. Then for all $k \ge K$, both $L$ and $a_{n_k}$ lie in $I_K$, hence $|a_{n_k} - L| < |I_K| < \varepsilon$. Thus $(a_{n_k}) \to L$.

Cauchy sequences introduce a fundamentally different perspective on convergence. A sequence $(a_n)$ is Cauchy if its terms eventually cluster arbitrarily tightly among themselves, independent of any external limiting value. Formally, $(a_n)$ is Cauchy if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all $m, n \ge N$ we have $|a_m - a_n| < \varepsilon$. This definition is intrinsic to the sequence itself and does not reference a limit. The completeness axiom of $\mathbb{R}$ is equivalent to the assertion that every Cauchy sequence in $\mathbb{R}$ converges. This equivalence distinguishes $\mathbb{R}$ from $\mathbb{Q}$: there exist Cauchy sequences in $\mathbb{Q}$ (for instance, the decimal approximations to $\sqrt{2}$) that do not converge to a rational limit. The proof that every convergent sequence is Cauchy is straightforward: if $(a_n) \to L$, then for $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $N$ such that $n \ge N$ implies $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon/2$. Then for $m, n \ge N$, we have $|a_m - a_n| \le |a_m - L| + |L - a_n| < \varepsilon$. The converse—that every Cauchy sequence converges—requires the completeness of $\mathbb{R}$. The proof proceeds in two stages: first show that every Cauchy sequence is bounded (choosing $\varepsilon = 1$, all but finitely many terms lie in a unit interval, so the entire sequence is bounded), then apply Bolzano-Weierstrass to extract a convergent subsequence, and finally show that the entire sequence converges to the same limit by leveraging the Cauchy property.

The concepts of limit superior and limit inferior provide a comprehensive classification of sequence behavior, including oscillatory and divergent cases. For a bounded sequence $(a_n)$, define $b_k = \sup\{a_n : n \ge k\}$ and $c_k = \inf\{a_n : n \ge k\}$. The sequence $(b_k)$ is monotone decreasing and bounded below (by any lower bound of $(a_n)$), hence converges by the monotone convergence theorem. We define $\limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n = \lim_{k \to \infty} b_k = \inf_{k \ge 1} \sup_{n \ge k} a_n$. Similarly, $(c_k)$ is monotone increasing and bounded above, so we define $\liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n = \lim_{k \to \infty} c_k = \sup_{k \ge 1} \inf_{n \ge k} a_n$. The limit superior captures the largest accumulation point of the sequence, while the limit inferior captures the smallest. A sequence converges if and only if $\limsup a_n = \liminf a_n$, and in this case the common value is the limit. If $\limsup a_n > \liminf a_n$, the sequence oscillates and does not converge. For unbounded sequences, we extend these definitions by allowing $\pm \infty$ as limit superior or inferior values.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The epsilon-N definition encodes convergence as a nested quantifier structure that can be visualized as a game between a challenger and a defender. The challenger selects an arbitrary tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, attempting to disprove convergence by choosing an impossibly small threshold. The defender must respond with a threshold index $N(\varepsilon)$ such that all terms beyond $N$ lie within the $\varepsilon$-band around the proposed limit. If the defender can win this game for every challenge $\varepsilon$, the sequence converges. This game-theoretic interpretation clarifies why the definition requires universal quantification over $\varepsilon$ followed by existential provision of $N$.

Consider the sequence $a_n = 1/n$. We claim $\lim_{n \to \infty} 1/n = 0$. Let $\varepsilon > 0$ be given. We must produce $N$ such that $n \ge N$ implies $|1/n - 0| < \varepsilon$, that is, $1/n < \varepsilon$. By the Archimedean property of $\mathbb{R}$, there exists $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $N > 1/\varepsilon$, equivalently $1/N < \varepsilon$. Then for all $n \ge N$, we have $1/n \le 1/N < \varepsilon$, verifying convergence. The threshold function $N(\varepsilon) = \lceil 1/\varepsilon \rceil$ is explicit and constructive.

For the sequence $a_n = (2n + 3)/(5n - 7)$, we claim convergence to $L = 2/5$. We compute:

$$|a_n - L| = \left| \frac{2n + 3}{5n - 7} - \frac{2}{5} \right| = \left| \frac{5(2n + 3) - 2(5n - 7)}{5(5n - 7)} \right| = \left| \frac{10n + 15 - 10n + 14}{5(5n - 7)} \right| = \frac{29}{5|5n - 7|}.$$

For $n \ge 2$, we have $5n - 7 \ge 3$, hence $|5n - 7| = 5n - 7 \ge 3$. Thus $|a_n - L| \le 29/(5 \cdot 3) = 29/15$ for $n \ge 2$. More precisely, for $n \ge 2$, we have $|a_n - L| = 29/(5(5n - 7)) < 29/(25n - 35)$. To ensure this is less than $\varepsilon$, we require $29/(25n - 35) < \varepsilon$, equivalently $n > (29 + 35\varepsilon)/(25\varepsilon)$. Setting $N = \max\{2, \lceil (29 + 35\varepsilon)/(25\varepsilon) \rceil\}$ suffices.

The monotone convergence theorem transforms the topological question of convergence into an order-theoretic question of boundedness. Consider the sequence defined recursively by $a_1 = 1$ and $a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n}$. We claim $(a_n)$ converges. First, we show by induction that $(a_n)$ is increasing and bounded above by 2. The base case $a_1 = 1 < 2$ is immediate. If $a_n < 2$, then $a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n} < \sqrt{2 + 2} = 2$. Furthermore, $a_{n+1} - a_n = \sqrt{2 + a_n} - a_n$. We verify $\sqrt{2 + a_n} \ge a_n$ by squaring (valid since both sides are positive): this requires $2 + a_n \ge a_n^2$, equivalently $a_n^2 - a_n - 2 \le 0$, which factors as $(a_n - 2)(a_n + 1) \le 0$. Since $a_n > 0$ and $a_n \le 2$, this inequality holds. Thus $(a_n)$ is increasing and bounded, hence converges to some $L \le 2$. Taking limits in the recurrence relation $a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n}$ yields $L = \sqrt{2 + L}$, hence $L^2 = 2 + L$, giving $L^2 - L - 2 = 0$. Factoring, $(L - 2)(L + 1) = 0$, so $L = 2$ or $L = -1$. Since $a_n > 0$ for all $n$, we have $L = 2$.

The bisection proof of Bolzano-Weierstrass reveals a nested interval construction that is central to many existence arguments in analysis. The key structural invariant is that nested intervals with vanishing lengths isolate a unique point, and any sequence with one term in each interval must converge to that point. This technique extends to the construction of the Cantor set, proofs of the intermediate value theorem, and fixed-point theorems.

To formalize the Cauchy criterion equivalence with convergence, we prove that every Cauchy sequence in $\mathbb{R}$ converges. Let $(a_n)$ be Cauchy. First, $(a_n)$ is bounded: for $\varepsilon = 1$, there exists $N$ such that $m, n \ge N$ implies $|a_m - a_n| < 1$. In particular, for all $n \ge N$, we have $|a_n - a_N| < 1$, hence $|a_n| \le |a_N| + 1$. Thus $(a_n)$ is bounded by $M = \max\{|a_1|, \ldots, |a_{N-1}|, |a_N| + 1\}$. By Bolzano-Weierstrass, $(a_n)$ has a convergent subsequence $(a_{n_k}) \to L$. We claim the entire sequence $(a_n) \to L$. Let $\varepsilon > 0$. Since $(a_n)$ is Cauchy, there exists $N_1$ such that $m, n \ge N_1$ implies $|a_m - a_n| < \varepsilon/2$. Since $(a_{n_k}) \to L$, there exists $K$ such that $k \ge K$ implies $|a_{n_k} - L| < \varepsilon/2$. Choose $k \ge K$ such that $n_k \ge N_1$ (this is possible since $n_k \to \infty$). Then for all $n \ge N_1$, we have $|a_n - L| \le |a_n - a_{n_k}| + |a_{n_k} - L| < \varepsilon/2 + \varepsilon/2 = \varepsilon$, establishing convergence.

The limit superior and limit inferior satisfy several key structural relations. For any bounded sequence $(a_n)$, we have $\liminf a_n \le \limsup a_n$. If $L$ is an accumulation point of $(a_n)$ (meaning there exists a subsequence converging to $L$), then $\liminf a_n \le L \le \limsup a_n$. The characterization of convergence via limsup and liminf is sharp: $(a_n) \to L$ if and only if $\limsup a_n = \liminf a_n = L$.

To compute $\limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n$ for $a_n = \sin(n)$, we observe that the sequence $(\sin(n))$ is dense in $[-1, 1]$ due to the irrationality of $\pi$ and Weyl's equidistribution theorem, hence the set of accumulation points is the entire interval $[-1, 1]$. Thus $\limsup_{n \to \infty} \sin(n) = 1$ and $\liminf_{n \to \infty} \sin(n) = -1$. For $a_n = (-1)^n (1 + 1/n)$, the subsequence $a_{2k} = 1 + 1/(2k) \to 1$, while $a_{2k-1} = -(1 + 1/(2k-1)) \to -1$. Hence $\limsup a_n = 1$ and $\liminf a_n = -1$.

The algebraic structure of convergence extends to the comparison and squeeze theorems. If $(a_n) \to L$, $(b_n) \to M$, and $a_n \le b_n$ for all $n \ge N_0$, then $L \le M$. The proof is by contradiction: if $L > M$, set $\varepsilon = (L - M)/2 > 0$. For sufficiently large $n$, we have $a_n > L - \varepsilon$ and $b_n < M + \varepsilon$, hence $a_n > L - \varepsilon = (L + M)/2 = M + \varepsilon > b_n$, contradicting $a_n \le b_n$. The squeeze theorem states that if $a_n \le c_n \le b_n$ for all $n \ge N_0$, and $(a_n) \to L$ and $(b_n) \to L$, then $(c_n) \to L$. This follows directly from the epsilon-N definition: given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $N_1, N_2$ such that $n \ge N_1$ implies $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$ and $n \ge N_2$ implies $|b_n - L| < \varepsilon$. Let $N = \max\{N_0, N_1, N_2\}$. Then for $n \ge N$, we have $L - \varepsilon < a_n \le c_n \le b_n < L + \varepsilon$, hence $|c_n - L| < \varepsilon$.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

The practical verification of convergence often hinges on the efficient construction of explicit threshold functions $N(\varepsilon)$. For sequences defined by rational expressions, algebraic simplification can yield sharp bounds. For recursively defined sequences, monotonicity arguments combined with boundedness checks enable application of the monotone convergence theorem without explicitly solving the recurrence relation. When direct verification is intractable, comparison with known convergent or divergent sequences via the squeeze theorem or tail estimates can provide resolution.

A common failure mode is the confusion between boundedness and convergence. The sequence $a_n = (-1)^n$ is bounded but does not converge, illustrating that boundedness is necessary but not sufficient. Another failure mode is the conflation of convergence of subsequences with convergence of the entire sequence. The sequence $a_n = (-1)^n$ has convergent subsequences (all even-indexed terms converge to 1, all odd-indexed terms converge to -1), yet the sequence itself diverges. The Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem guarantees existence of a convergent subsequence for any bounded sequence, but it does not imply convergence of the full sequence unless additional structure (such as the Cauchy property) is present.

The Cauchy criterion provides a powerful computational tool for proving convergence without explicitly identifying the limit. For example, consider the decimal expansion sequence $a_n = \sum_{k=1}^n d_k / 10^k$ where $d_k \in \{0, 1, \ldots, 9\}$. To show $(a_n)$ converges, we verify it is Cauchy: for $m > n$, we have $|a_m - a_n| = \left| \sum_{k=n+1}^m d_k / 10^k \right| \le \sum_{k=n+1}^m 9 / 10^k < \sum_{k=n+1}^\infty 9 / 10^k = (9/10^{n+1}) / (1 - 1/10) = 10/10^{n+1} = 1/10^n$, which tends to 0 as $n \to \infty$. Thus $(a_n)$ is Cauchy and hence converges in $\mathbb{R}$. This argument establishes convergence without computing the limit explicitly.

Edge cases in the epsilon-N game arise when sequences converge slowly. For $a_n = 1/\ln(n)$, the sequence converges to 0, but the threshold function $N(\varepsilon) = e^{1/\varepsilon}$ grows exponentially in $1/\varepsilon$, making direct verification computationally expensive for small $\varepsilon$. For $a_n = 1/\ln(\ln(n))$, convergence is even slower, and threshold functions become doubly exponential. These slow convergence rates have implications for numerical analysis and algorithm design, where iterative methods must balance accuracy against computational cost.

Divergence to infinity is formalized analogously to convergence: $(a_n) \to \infty$ if for every $M > 0$ there exists $N$ such that $n \ge N$ implies $a_n > M$. Similarly, $(a_n) \to -\infty$ if for every $M < 0$ there exists $N$ such that $n \ge N$ implies $a_n < M$. The sequence $a_n = n^2$ diverges to infinity, while $a_n = -n^2$ diverges to negative infinity. Oscillatory divergence, exemplified by $a_n = n(-1)^n$, involves unbounded oscillation without a limiting infinity. Such sequences have no limit, finite or infinite, and are characterized by $\limsup a_n = \infty$ and $\liminf a_n = -\infty$.

The ratio test and root test, while traditionally framed for series convergence, have direct applications to sequence analysis. For a sequence $(a_n)$ with positive terms, if $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_{n+1}/a_n = r < 1$, then $\limsup a_n^{1/n} = r < 1$, and the sequence converges to 0 at a geometric rate. For $a_n = n^k / c^n$ with $c > 1$, the ratio $a_{n+1}/a_n = ((n+1)/n)^k / c \to 1/c < 1$, confirming exponential decay to 0. For $a_n = n!$, the ratio $a_{n+1}/a_n = n + 1 \to \infty$, confirming superexponential divergence.

Subsequence extraction is a fundamental technique for analyzing oscillatory or complex sequences. Given a sequence $(a_n)$, the extraction of even-indexed and odd-indexed subsequences $(a_{2k})$ and $(a_{2k-1})$ can reveal periodic structure. For $a_n = \sin(n\pi/2)$, the subsequences $a_{4k} = 0$, $a_{4k+1} = 1$, $a_{4k+2} = 0$, $a_{4k+3} = -1$ exhibit periodic behavior modulo 4. More generally, if every subsequence of $(a_n)$ has a further subsequence converging to the same limit $L$, then $(a_n) \to L$. This result follows from the epsilon-N definition: if $(a_n)$ did not converge to $L$, there would exist $\varepsilon_0 > 0$ and a subsequence $(a_{n_k})$ with $|a_{n_k} - L| \ge \varepsilon_0$ for all $k$, contradicting the hypothesis that every subsequence has a further subsequence converging to $L$.

The completeness of $\mathbb{R}$ distinguishes it from incomplete metric spaces such as $\mathbb{Q}$. The sequence $a_n = (1 + 1/n)^n$ converges in $\mathbb{R}$ to the transcendental number $e \approx 2.71828$, but this limit is irrational and does not exist in $\mathbb{Q}$. The sequence is Cauchy in $\mathbb{Q}$ but does not converge to a rational limit, demonstrating the incompleteness of $\mathbb{Q}$. The completion of $\mathbb{Q}$ via equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences recovers $\mathbb{R}$, providing a constructive foundation for the real number system.

Nested interval sequences provide a dual perspective on completeness. A sequence of nested closed intervals $I_1 \supseteq I_2 \supseteq I_3 \supseteq \ldots$ with $|I_n| \to 0$ has a unique common point $\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty I_n = \{L\}$. If $I_n = [a_n, b_n]$, then $(a_n)$ is increasing and $(b_n)$ is decreasing, with $a_n \le b_n$ for all $n$. By the monotone convergence theorem, $(a_n) \to L_a$ and $(b_n) \to L_b$. Since $|I_n| = b_n - a_n \to 0$, we have $L_a = L_b = L$, and $L \in I_n$ for all $n$. This nested interval property is equivalent to the completeness axiom and provides the foundation for bisection algorithms, interval arithmetic, and fixed-point iteration methods.

The interplay between convergence and algebraic operations extends to power sequences. If $|r| < 1$, then $r^n \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$, proven by setting $r = 1/(1 + h)$ with $h > 0$, so $r^n = 1/(1 + h)^n \le 1/(1 + nh)$ by Bernoulli's inequality, which tends to 0. If $r = 1$, then $r^n = 1$ for all $n$, hence $r^n \to 1$. If $r > 1$, write $r = 1 + h$ with $h > 0$, so $r^n = (1 + h)^n \ge 1 + nh \to \infty$ by Bernoulli's inequality. If $r = -1$, then $r^n = (-1)^n$ oscillates. If $r < -1$, then $r^n$ oscillates unboundedly. These cases partition the behavior of geometric sequences and provide the foundation for the geometric series convergence criterion.

The Cesàro mean provides a smoothing operation that can induce convergence from oscillatory sequences. If $(a_n) \to L$, then the Cesàro mean sequence $b_n = (a_1 + \cdots + a_n)/n$ also converges to $L$. The converse is false: the sequence $a_n = (-1)^n$ diverges, but its Cesàro mean $b_n = (a_1 + \cdots + a_n)/n \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$, since the partial sums oscillate between 0 and $\pm 1$, and division by $n$ forces convergence to 0. This averaging technique is central to ergodic theory and summability methods for divergent series.

The limsup and liminf capture the extremal accumulation behavior of sequences and provide a complete classification of limiting behavior. For a sequence $(a_n)$ of real numbers (not necessarily bounded), we define $\limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n = \lim_{k \to \infty} \sup_{n \ge k} a_n$ and $\liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n = \lim_{k \to \infty} \inf_{n \ge k} a_n$, allowing values in $[-\infty, \infty]$. These extended limits always exist in $[-\infty, \infty]$. The sequence $(a_n)$ converges to a finite limit $L$ if and only if $\limsup a_n = \liminf a_n = L$. It diverges to $\infty$ if and only if $\limsup a_n = \liminf a_n = \infty$. It diverges to $-\infty$ if and only if $\limsup a_n = \liminf a_n = -\infty$. It oscillates if $\limsup a_n > \liminf a_n$.

For the sequence $a_n = n \sin^2(n\pi/6)$, the subsequence $a_{6k} = 0$, while $a_{6k+3} = (6k+3) \cdot 1 \to \infty$. Thus $\liminf a_n = 0$ and $\limsup a_n = \infty$. For $a_n = (1 + 1/n) \cos(n\pi)$, the subsequence $a_{2k} = (1 + 1/(2k)) \to 1$ while $a_{2k-1} = -(1 + 1/(2k-1)) \to -1$. Thus $\limsup a_n = 1$ and $\liminf a_n = -1$.

The relationship between Cauchy sequences and completeness extends to general metric spaces. A metric space $(X, d)$ is complete if every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges to a point in $X$. The real line $\mathbb{R}$ with the Euclidean metric $d(x, y) = |x - y|$ is complete. The rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ with the same metric are not complete. The space of continuous functions $C([0, 1])$ with the supremum metric $d(f, g) = \sup_{x \in [0, 1]} |f(x) - g(x)|$ is complete, while the space of polynomials $\mathcal{P}([0, 1])$ with the same metric is not. Completeness is the topological foundation for fixed-point theorems, contraction mapping principles, and the Baire category theorem, which collectively underpin existence and uniqueness results throughout analysis, differential equations, and optimization theory.
