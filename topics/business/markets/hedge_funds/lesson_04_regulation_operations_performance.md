# Lesson 4: Regulation, Operations, Performance Measurement

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The hedge fund industry exists in a state of perpetual tension between two opposing forces: the economic imperative to generate alpha through sophisticated, often opaque strategies, and the regulatory imperative to protect market integrity and ensure that capital flows to qualified, sophisticated participants who can bear the risks these vehicles entail. Understanding this tension is foundational to understanding why hedge funds are structured the way they are, why their operations are so capital-intensive, and why performance measurement has become simultaneously more rigorous and more contested.

At their core, hedge funds operate under a regulatory exemption. Unlike mutual funds, which are heavily regulated under the Investment Company Act of 1940, hedge funds typically rely on exemptions from registration as investment advisers and investment companies. These exemptions—particularly the "qualified client" exemption under the Advisers Act and the "private fund exemption" under the Investment Company Act—are not granted freely. They come with explicit conditions: the fund may only accept capital from accredited investors or qualified purchasers; the fund's adviser must register with the SEC and comply with heightened operational and disclosure standards; and the fund itself must maintain detailed books and records that regulators can inspect. This regulatory structure reflects a philosophical principle: sophisticated investors with significant capital and financial literacy can self-protect through due diligence and negotiation, and therefore do not require the paternalistic guardrails applied to retail mutual fund investors.

The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) operate as the primary federal regulators of hedge fund activity. The SEC regulates investment advisers and investment company conduct through the Investment Advisers Act of 1940 and maintains jurisdiction over equity and certain derivative markets. The CFTC regulates commodity and futures markets and derivatives trading. Many hedge funds engage in strategies that fall within both regulators' jurisdictions—a multi-strategy fund trading equities, commodity futures, and FX derivatives simultaneously will find itself subject to overlapping oversight from both agencies. This jurisdictional overlap creates complexity: a fund must maintain dual compliance calendars, submit separate regulatory filings, and ensure that operational infrastructure supports the reporting and audit requirements of both agencies.

The foundational invariant underlying all hedge fund regulation is the concept of accreditation. An accredited investor, as defined by the SEC under Regulation D, is an individual with either annual income exceeding $200,000 (or $300,000 for joint filers) for the two preceding years with reasonable expectation of continued income, or net worth exceeding $1 million (excluding primary residence). Alternatively, qualified institutional buyers—such as pensions, endowments, insurance companies, and registered investment advisers—are presumed accredited by virtue of their institutional status and size. This accreditation requirement is not merely administrative compliance; it serves as a legal threshold that removes regulatory liability from the fund and its adviser for certain disclosure failures, under the theory that accredited investors can fend for themselves. When a hedge fund accepts capital from a non-accredited investor—or fails to verify accreditation documentation—it exposes itself to regulatory sanction, potential disgorgement of fees, and private litigation from defrauded investors claiming they were misled about the risks they were taking.

Beyond accreditation lies the concept of the qualified purchaser, which is a higher standard created under the Investment Company Act. A qualified purchaser is an individual with at least $5 million in investable assets, or an institutional investor meeting specific size thresholds. Many hedge funds structure themselves to accept only qualified purchasers, which allows the fund to impose higher leverage, use more complex derivative strategies, and operate with fewer ongoing SEC disclosures. This structural choice reflects a risk calculus: by accepting only the wealthiest and most sophisticated investors, the fund can deploy more aggressive strategies and maintain greater operational secrecy.

The operational infrastructure of a hedge fund is designed to support regulatory compliance while simultaneously maintaining performance and managing risk. This infrastructure comprises four distinct but interconnected systems: custody and asset safekeeping, valuation and pricing, reconciliation and control, and reporting and disclosure. Each system performs a specific function within the regulatory ecosystem, and each system can fail in ways that create both financial and regulatory risk.

Custody is the act of holding securities and cash on behalf of investors. In a properly structured hedge fund, custodial assets are segregated from the fund adviser's own assets; the custodian—typically a large commercial bank or specialized prime broker—maintains physical or electronic control over the securities and cash and is responsible for ensuring they are not comingled or misappropriated. This segregation is not merely prudent; it is mandated by the Advisers Act Rule 206(4)-2, which requires that advisers holding client assets either maintain custody themselves (subject to stringent auditing and bonding requirements) or place assets with a "qualified custodian." The qualified custodian requirement exists because of historical fraud: before this rule was adopted, investment advisers would hold client assets directly, creating temptation and opportunity for misappropriation. Bernie Madoff, operating for decades before his 2008 exposure, relied on poor custody practices and the absence of genuine independent verification to sustain his Ponzi scheme. Modern hedge fund custody practices exist specifically to prevent this class of fraud.

Valuation and pricing are the processes by which a hedge fund determines the value of its portfolio holdings at a given point in time. For exchange-traded securities, this is straightforward: the closing price on the relevant exchange provides an objective, real-time valuation. For illiquid securities, derivatives with no active secondary market, or alternative assets like private equity or real estate held through fund structures, valuation becomes subjective and contested. A hedge fund holding a private company stake must estimate its fair value through comparable company analysis, discounted cash flow projections, or transaction precedents. A hedge fund with exposure to credit default swap indices must mark positions using broker quotes and interpolation models. This subjectivity creates both opportunity for fraud and opportunity for honest error. The SEC and CFTC have issued extensive guidance requiring that fund advisers use "fair value" methodologies that are reasonable, consistently applied, and independently verified where possible. In practice, this means hedge funds employ internal valuation committees—typically comprising portfolio managers, risk officers, and operational controllers—that review illiquid or complex positions quarterly or more frequently, document their assumptions, and reconcile valuations against independent sources such as broker quotes or service provider inputs.

Reconciliation is the systematic comparison of internal records against external sources of truth. A hedge fund's portfolio management system records what positions the fund holds and what prices were paid. The custodian's system records the same positions independently. A reconciliation process compares these two records, identifies discrepancies, investigates root causes, and documents resolution. Reconciliation occurs daily for traded securities (matching trades executed by the fund against custodian confirmations), monthly for positions and cash balances (matching positions and cash held according to the fund's records against the custodian's records), and quarterly for detailed holdings and valuations. Reconciliation failures create audit findings, regulatory red flags, and operational risk: if a fund cannot reconcile its cash balance, it cannot accurately calculate its net asset value per share, cannot accurately pay investors redemptions, and cannot file accurate performance reports.

The invariant underlying all of this operational complexity is the principle of independent verification. Regulators recognize that fund advisers have incentives to overstate asset values (to increase their own fee income based on assets under management), understate liabilities (to present misleadingly favorable performance), or misapply accounting standards to smooth reported returns. To counteract these incentives, hedge funds must have their financial statements audited annually by independent auditors who are not affiliated with the fund adviser, and must maintain operational controls that are reviewed and tested by the auditors. The auditor's role is not merely to check mathematical accuracy; it is to assess whether the fund's valuation methodologies are reasonable, whether controls over pricing and reconciliation are effective, and whether the fund's financial reporting conforms to accounting standards.

Performance measurement sits atop this operational foundation. A hedge fund's performance is its primary marketing tool and the basis on which investors decide to allocate capital and retain their investment. Yet performance metrics—even seemingly objective metrics like returns—can be misleading without proper context and understanding of what is actually being measured and how.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

### Regulatory Filing & Compliance Calendar

The regulatory compliance calendar for a hedge fund is structured around a series of mandatory filings and submission deadlines that occur at different frequencies and to different agencies. Understanding this calendar and its mechanics is essential because missing a deadline, filing incomplete information, or providing inconsistent information across filings creates regulatory exposure.

The foundational filing for all SEC-regulated hedge fund advisers is Form ADV, which must be filed annually by March 31 (or within 90 days of fiscal year end). Form ADV Part 1A is a standardized questionnaire that captures information about the adviser's business, ownership, compensation, regulatory history, conflicts of interest, and assets under management. Form ADV Part 1B is the "brochure," a document that advisers must provide to clients describing the adviser's investment approach, fee structure, conflicts, and risks. Form ADV Part 2A, if filed, contains supplemental information about specific investment strategies. The information in Form ADV is used by the SEC to allocate examination resources and to identify potential regulatory risks. An adviser that misrepresents assets under management on Form ADV, for example, may face enforcement action, because AUM is used to determine whether the adviser meets size thresholds that trigger specific regulatory obligations.

Concurrent with Form ADV, hedge fund advisers that manage $150 million or more in hedge fund assets must file Form PF (Private Fund Report) with the SEC. Form PF is the hedge fund industry's most detailed regulatory submission. It captures a fund-by-fund breakdown of assets, leverage, counterparty exposure, redemption terms, valuation methodologies, and certain performance and risk metrics. Form PF exists because the SEC lacks real-time visibility into the size and leverage of the hedge fund industry and the interconnectedness of hedge funds with prime brokers, custodians, and other financial institutions. During the 2008 financial crisis, the SEC discovered retroactively that hedge funds were far more leveraged and interconnected than previously understood, and that failures of prime brokers cascaded into failures across the hedge fund ecosystem. Form PF was designed to prevent this informational vacuum from recurring.

Form PF is filed on a quarterly basis (31 days after quarter-end) and is highly technical. The form requires advisers to report, fund-by-fund, detailed information about leverage (including off-balance-sheet leverage through derivatives and short sales), counterparty exposure (listing the ten largest counterparties and their credit exposure), liquidity profile (matching redemption frequency against portfolio liquidity), and valuation methodology. Large advisers managing over $1 billion in hedge fund assets file an enhanced Form PF that includes monthly reporting of portfolio holdings for the adviser's largest hedge funds and detailed information about derivative usage, short positions, and the results of stress tests.

For advisers managing commodity-related strategies or trading significant notional amounts of futures and derivatives, the CFTC requires registration as a Commodity Trading Adviser (CTA) or Commodity Pool Operator (CPO). CTAs and CPOs file Form CPO-PQR (Commodity Pool Operator-Pool Questionnaire Report) on a quarterly basis, disclosing similar information about leverage, counterparty risk, and performance. The CFTC's filing requirements overlap significantly with the SEC's Form PF requirements, creating a compliance burden: the same underlying data must be extracted, formatted according to different technical specifications, and submitted to two different agencies on different schedules.

The operational mechanics of these filings are nontrivial. A hedge fund adviser must maintain a data infrastructure that extracts information from portfolio management systems, accounting systems, and custodian statements; transforms this information into the format and definitions required by Form ADV and Form PF; and submits the filings through the SEC's EDGAR filing system or the CFTC's equivalent system. Each field in Form ADV and Form PF has a specific definition—"assets under management" includes only discretionary assets the adviser actually invests, not consulting fees or advisory-only arrangements; "leverage" includes both direct borrowing and notional leverage through derivatives—and misapplication of these definitions creates inaccurate filings.

Many hedge fund advisers outsource this compliance infrastructure to compliance-as-a-service vendors or hire in-house compliance officers who maintain mapping documents from internal data systems to regulatory definitions, run monthly or quarterly reconciliations, and prepare draft filings for adviser review and sign-off. The process typically works as follows: in the first week after quarter-end, portfolio and accounting data are extracted and loaded into a compliance data warehouse; mappings are applied to translate internal accounting codes into regulatory categories; preliminary calculations are performed; internal stakeholders (CIO, COO, CFO, Chief Compliance Officer) review the preliminary results for reasonableness; any discrepancies are investigated and corrected; and the final filing is prepared, reviewed by outside counsel, and submitted by the deadline.

### Accreditation Verification & Investor Onboarding

The mechanics of verifying accreditation and onboarding new investors establish the foundation for the fund's regulatory compliance. A hedge fund adviser has a fiduciary obligation to ensure that the fund only accepts capital from accredited or qualified purchaser investors, as applicable. Failure to verify accreditation exposes the fund to regulatory action and investor claims.

Accreditation verification occurs through documentation: the prospective investor provides either tax returns showing income exceeding $200,000 (or joint income exceeding $300,000) for the two preceding years, or a balance sheet (audited if available) showing net worth exceeding $1 million. The fund's compliance team reviews this documentation, confirms it appears reasonable (tax returns are recent and from an appropriate issuing authority; balance sheets are dated and prepared using GAAP; the stated income or net worth exceeds the threshold), and maintains the documentation in a file that can be produced to the SEC in an examination. For institutional investors, accreditation is typically documented through a representation letter from the investor certifying that it meets the qualified institutional buyer definition.

The investor onboarding process typically encompasses the following steps: initial contact and interest expression by the prospective investor; delivery of the fund's offering document (private placement memorandum or PPM), a comprehensive document describing the fund's strategy, risks, fee structure, and terms; completion by the prospective investor of an investor suitability questionnaire confirming accreditation status and investment objectives; execution of a subscription agreement, the legal contract establishing the investor's terms and rights; collection of the initial capital (typically wired to the fund's custodian, not to the fund adviser directly, to maintain segregation); and acknowledgment to the investor of receipt of capital and issuance of partnership units or shares representing the investor's interest.

The PPM is the primary disclosure document and serves as the basis for regulatory compliance with antifraud provisions of the federal securities laws. If a fund omits material risks or misrepresents its strategy, fee structure, or historical performance in the PPM, and an investor relies on these misstatements in deciding to invest, the fund adviser can face liability for securities fraud. Accordingly, PPMs are typically drafted by specialized securities counsel, reviewed by outside compliance consultants, and updated annually or whenever material changes occur.

### Custody, Valuation, and Reconciliation: Operational Workflows

The custody and valuation architecture of a hedge fund establishes both the plumbing through which capital moves and the processes through which asset values are determined. Consider a typical hedge fund that employs multiple prime brokers (perhaps Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley), holds positions in equities, fixed income, and derivatives, and maintains a small amount of cash for redemptions. The operational workflow unfolds as follows.

When a trader at the hedge fund decides to purchase 100,000 shares of ABC Corp at $50 per share, the trader enters the trade order into the fund's portfolio management system (often a commercial platform like Bloomberg, FactSet, or a custom in-house system). The trade is executed through one of the fund's prime brokers, and the prime broker sends a trade confirmation to the fund and to the custodian. The fund's operations team reconciles the trade in the portfolio management system against the prime broker's confirmation, ensuring that quantity, price, and settlement date match. On settlement (typically T+2 for equities in U.S. markets), the fund's cash account at the custodian is debited for the purchase price, and the fund's securities account is credited with the shares. The custodian provides a daily reconciliation report listing all trades and positions. The operations team compares the daily reconciliation against the fund's internal records and investigates any breaks (positions that appear on the custodian's statement but not in the fund's system, or vice versa).

Breaks are common and usually benign: they arise from timing differences (a trade executed late in the day may not settle immediately), data entry errors, or system synchronization delays. When a break occurs, the operations team investigates root cause, documents the resolution, and ensures the break is cleared by the next business day. If a break persists for several days or involves a large notional value, it escalates to senior operational management and triggers a deeper investigation.

Cash reconciliation occurs daily and is particularly critical because cash directly affects the fund's ability to pay investor redemptions. The fund's accounting system shows a calculated cash balance based on all inflows (investor deposits, dividends, interest, proceeds from sales), outflows (investor redemptions, management fees, expense payments), and other movements. The custodian's system independently maintains the fund's cash balance based on its own record of transactions. These two balances should match exactly. If they don't, the operations team investigates: Was a dividend received by the custodian but not yet recorded by accounting? Did the fund initiate an expense payment that hasn't cleared? Did accounting make a data entry error? The cash reconciliation process typically runs nightly, with results reviewed by the morning of the next business day.

Valuation occurs at multiple frequencies depending on the asset class. For exchange-traded securities, positions are marked-to-market daily at 4 p.m. Eastern Time (or market close) using the closing price from the relevant exchange. For positions in foreign exchanges, positions are marked using real-time feeds from those exchanges after their local market close (and before the fund's NAV calculation). For illiquid securities or alternative assets, valuation occurs on a less frequent basis—typically quarterly, or whenever new information becomes available (such as a recent transaction in a comparable company or a financing round for a private company the fund holds).

The valuation methodology for illiquid assets is documented in the fund's valuation procedures, which are typically reviewed and approved by the fund's valuation committee. For a private company held at cost, the valuation procedure might state: "Private investments held at cost unless there is evidence of a significant change in valuation. Valuation changes are triggered by (a) a financing round by the company at a new valuation; (b) a comparable company transaction; (c) a material change in the company's financial condition; or (d) the occurrence of a triggering event such as bankruptcy or acquisition." For a credit derivative position, the valuation procedure might state: "Mark-to-market using Bloomberg valuation service; if Bloomberg valuation diverges from dealer quotes by more than X%, investigate discrepancy and apply judgment."

Once valuations are determined, the fund's NAV (net asset value) is calculated. NAV is the total market value of the fund's assets minus its liabilities, divided by the number of outstanding shares or partnership units. If the fund has $100 million in assets, $5 million in liabilities (perhaps borrowed cash), and 10 million shares outstanding, the NAV per share is ($100 million - $5 million) / 10 million = $9.50. NAV per share is the price at which new investors buy into the fund and the price at which existing investors redeem their shares. Because NAV directly affects investor valuations and management fee calculations, inaccuracy in NAV is not merely an operational nuisance; it is a regulatory and fiduciary violation.

The NAV calculation workflow typically unfolds as follows. At a fixed time each business day (often 4:30 p.m. or 5 p.m. Eastern Time, after market close), the fund's portfolio management and accounting systems generate a NAV package. This package includes: a position-level holdings report; a pricing report showing the price used for each position and the source of the price (exchange, broker quote, internal model); a valuation adjustment report for any illiquid or complex positions that were revalued; a cash reconciliation showing beginning cash balance, all movements during the day, and ending cash balance; and a leverage and borrowing report showing all debt positions, interest costs, and net debt. The NAV is calculated by: summing the market value of all positions (quantity × price), adding accrued income and receivables, subtracting liabilities and accrued expenses, and dividing by shares outstanding.
