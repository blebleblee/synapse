# Lesson 3: Forex Risk Measurement, Hedging, and Position Management

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Currency risk is not a single phenomenon. It is a family of related but mechanically distinct exposures that arise wherever a firm, fund, or portfolio participant operates across monetary jurisdictions. The practitioner who conflates transaction risk with economic risk will misprice hedges, misreport balance-sheet volatility, and misalign the treasury function with the firm's actual commercial strategy. Getting the taxonomy right is therefore the first invariant of professional FX risk management, because the measurement tool you reach for, and the hedging instrument you choose, depends entirely on which kind of exposure you are addressing.

**Transaction exposure** is the most immediate and most legible form of currency risk. It arises the moment a firm commits to a future cash flow denominated in a foreign currency: a receivable from an export sale, a payable to an overseas supplier, a dividend repatriation, or a cross-border acquisition settlement. The exposure exists between the moment of contractual commitment and the moment of cash settlement. A U.S. manufacturer that invoices a German buyer for €10,000,000 payable in 90 days is long euros and short dollars for that period. If EUR/USD moves from 1.0800 to 1.0400 by settlement, the dollar proceeds fall from $10,800,000 to $10,400,000, a $400,000 loss on the underlying commercial transaction. The exposure is defined, dated, and denominated precisely — which makes it the most tractable category for mechanical hedging.

**Translation exposure**, sometimes called accounting exposure, is a different animal entirely. It does not involve an actual cash flow. Instead, it arises when a multinational firm consolidates the financial statements of foreign subsidiaries into its reporting currency. Under ASC 830 (U.S. GAAP) and IAS 21 (IFRS), monetary assets and liabilities of a foreign subsidiary are translated at the balance-sheet date spot rate, while non-monetary items use historical rates. The resulting translation gains and losses flow into Other Comprehensive Income (OCI) on the equity section of the consolidated balance sheet, not through the income statement, unless the foreign operation is in a hyperinflationary economy. A U.S. parent with a sterling-denominated U.K. subsidiary that carries £500,000,000 in net assets is exposed to GBP/USD translation risk on every reporting date. If cable drops from 1.2700 to 1.2200, the consolidated equity line shrinks by $25,000,000 purely through the translation mechanism, with no underlying economic loss having occurred in the subsidiary's local operations. Whether to hedge translation exposure is a contested governance question. Many treasury teams choose not to, arguing that OCI volatility is non-cash and sophisticated investors see through it. Others hedge when leverage covenants or executive compensation benchmarks are sensitive to book equity levels.

**Economic exposure** — also called operating exposure — is the most strategically important and the hardest to quantify. It refers to the effect of unexpected exchange rate changes on the present value of a firm's future free cash flows. Unlike transaction exposure, it encompasses cash flows that have not yet been contracted. A Japanese luxury car exporter is economically exposed to yen appreciation not just because of invoiced receivables, but because a stronger yen raises the dollar price of its vehicles in the American market, compressing volume and market share over a multi-year horizon relative to Detroit and Korean competitors. The economic exposure propagates through revenue, cost structure, competitive positioning, and reinvestment decisions simultaneously. Hedging instruments calibrated for dated transaction flows cannot neutralize this. Managing economic exposure requires operational decisions: invoicing in the buyer's currency to shift risk to the client, shifting production to cost-currency jurisdictions to create a natural hedge, or adjusting pricing elasticity models to absorb partial currency pass-through.

The core invariant that connects all three exposure types is this: **the hedge should match the exposure in currency pair, notional, tenor, and hedge accounting classification**. Mismatches across any of these dimensions create basis risk — the residual risk left after the hedge is applied — which can be as damaging as the original unhedged position if it is not measured and budgeted.

### Value at Risk for FX Positions

Value at Risk (VaR) is the dominant risk quantification framework in institutional FX management, despite its well-documented limitations. At its core, VaR answers a specific probabilistic question: what is the maximum loss on a position or portfolio that will not be exceeded over a given holding period, at a given confidence level, assuming normal market conditions? The practical expression most commonly used in FX desks is the one-day 99% VaR, meaning the desk can expect to lose more than the stated VaR amount on roughly 2.5 trading days per year.

The three methodological approaches to computing VaR each make different assumptions about the distribution of returns and handle tail behavior differently. **Parametric (variance-covariance) VaR** assumes that daily FX log-returns are normally distributed and uses the sample standard deviation of the currency pair as the input. For a single-currency linear position, the formula is:

$$\text{VaR}_{1d,99\%} = W \cdot \sigma_d \cdot z_{0.99}$$

where $W$ is the notional position size in the reporting currency, $\sigma_d$ is the daily standard deviation of log-returns for the currency pair, and $z_{0.99} = 2.326$ is the standard normal quantile at 99% confidence. If a desk is long $50,000,000 in USD/JPY and the daily volatility of the pair is 0.65%, then:

$$\text{VaR} = \$50{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0065 \times 2.326 = \$755{,}950$$

Scaling to a 10-day holding period uses the square root of time, so the 10-day 99% VaR is $\$755{,}950 \times \sqrt{10} \approx \$2{,}390{,}000$. The Basel III regulatory capital framework mandates the 10-day 99% VaR as the basis for market risk capital charges.

The parametric method breaks down at portfolio level because it must account for correlation between currency pairs. The multivariate parametric VaR for a portfolio of $n$ FX positions is:

$$\text{VaR}_p = z_{0.99} \cdot \sqrt{\mathbf{w}^T \Sigma \mathbf{w}}$$

where $\mathbf{w}$ is the vector of position notionals and $\Sigma$ is the covariance matrix of daily log-returns across all pairs in the portfolio. When currency pairs are positively correlated — EUR/USD and GBP/USD share significant comovement driven by the common dollar leg — the portfolio VaR is less than the sum of individual VaRs, reflecting genuine diversification. When a risk-off shock hits global markets, however, those correlations can collapse or invert, turning apparent diversification into correlated drawdown. This is the parametric model's central failure mode: the covariance matrix is estimated from historical data that may not represent the correlation regime prevailing during a stress event.

**Historical simulation VaR** sidesteps the normality assumption by applying actual historical daily return vectors to the current portfolio and sorting the resulting P&L distribution. If 500 trading days of history are used, the 99th percentile loss is simply the fifth-worst P&L observation. This approach captures fat tails and skew as they actually appeared in market data, but its effectiveness depends entirely on whether the historical window contains relevant stress scenarios. A 500-day window estimated in 2006 would not capture the 2008 currency dislocations. A 2010–2015 window underweights the Swiss National Bank's January 2015 EUR/CHF floor removal, when the franc appreciated 20% in minutes — an event that rendered parametric VaR models catastrophically insufficient for any desk short CHF volatility.

**Monte Carlo VaR** generates thousands of simulated return paths using a specified stochastic process — most commonly correlated geometric Brownian motions or a jump-diffusion model — and prices the portfolio under each scenario. This approach handles optionality and non-linear payoff structures that parametric VaR cannot accommodate, since options have gamma and vega exposures that make their P&L profile non-linear with respect to spot moves. The computational cost is proportionally higher, but for portfolios containing FX options, exotic structures, or barrier products, Monte Carlo is the only methodology that accurately represents the full risk profile.

### Correlation Dynamics and Their Failure Modes

Currency pair correlations are not stationary. They are functions of the macroeconomic regime, global risk appetite, and the structural relationships between monetary authorities. The EUR/USD and USD/CHF correlation is reliably negative and close to -1.00 during normal periods because the Swiss franc and euro share deep European economic ties — when EUR/USD falls, USD/CHF rises by nearly the same magnitude, as both reflect a strengthening dollar against European currencies. A trader long EUR/USD and long USD/CHF in equal notionals is nearly perfectly hedged against dollar-directional moves in calm conditions.

But the January 2015 SNB shock demonstrated that structural correlations can break violently and instantaneously when a central bank policy regime changes. Prior to the shock, the EUR/CHF floor at 1.2000 had been in place since September 2011, compressing EUR/CHF implied volatility to near-zero. Options traders pricing EUR/CHF structures used historical correlation and volatility inputs that bore no relationship to the latent risk of the peg removal. When the SNB removed the floor without warning, EUR/CHF collapsed 20%, EUR/USD moved sharply, and the correlated P&L across any portfolio with CHF exposure was nothing like the VaR models had projected.

The practitioner lesson is that correlation-based diversification estimates must be supplemented with stress tests that explicitly assume correlation breakdowns. A robust FX risk framework maintains a regime-conditional correlation library: a "risk-on" matrix estimated from low-volatility trending periods, a "risk-off" matrix from periods like 2008 or 2011, and a "structural break" matrix capturing tail events. VaR is a tool for normal markets; scenario analysis and reverse stress testing are the tools for understanding what breaks the portfolio at the tails.

### Duration and Convexity in FX-Denominated Bond Portfolios

A portfolio manager holding foreign-currency bonds carries two distinct risk dimensions simultaneously: interest rate risk (duration) and currency risk. These dimensions interact in ways that require careful decomposition. The price of a foreign-currency bond denominated in euros, held by a dollar-based investor, moves with both euro interest rates and the EUR/USD exchange rate. The total P&L attribution across a one-day horizon is approximately:

$$\Delta P_{\$} \approx S \cdot \left(-D \cdot P_{€} \cdot \Delta y\right) + P_{€} \cdot \Delta S$$

where $S$ is the spot EUR/USD rate, $D$ is the modified duration of the bond in years, $P_{€}$ is the euro price of the bond, $\Delta y$ is the change in the euro yield, and $\Delta S$ is the change in the exchange rate. The first term captures the interest rate component: a bond with $D = 7.5$ years loses approximately 7.5% of its euro price per 100 basis point rise in euro rates. The second term captures the currency component: if EUR/USD falls 2%, the dollar value of the entire position declines by 2%, regardless of what happens to euro yields.

The cross term — the change in dollar value of the duration P&L caused by simultaneous yield and FX moves — is the convexity correction that matters for large moves. When rates rise sharply and risk-off flows simultaneously pressure the currency (a common combination in emerging market crises), both components of loss reinforce each other, and the aggregate drawdown substantially exceeds what an additive decomposition would suggest. For EM sovereign bonds, this interaction is not second-order: a 300 basis point sovereign yield spike accompanied by a 15% currency depreciation is exactly the scenario in which additive risk decomposition fails. The correct treatment maintains a DV01-equivalent of the full dollar position (the dollar change in portfolio value per basis point move in foreign yields) alongside the FX delta of the same position, and runs correlated stress scenarios across both simultaneously.

### The Hedging Instrument Toolkit

A treasury practitioner or portfolio risk manager has three primary instrument classes for hedging FX exposure: forward contracts, options, and cross-currency swaps. Each occupies a different position in the trade-off space between cost, flexibility, and payoff linearity, and choosing among them is not a stylistic preference but a function of the exposure's cash flow certainty, duration, and directionality.
