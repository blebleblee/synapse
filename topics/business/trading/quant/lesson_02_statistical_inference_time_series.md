# Lesson 2: Statistical Inference for Financial Time Series

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Financial markets generate streams of observational data that superficially resemble the ideal random processes taught in undergraduate statistics courses, yet their internal structure defies nearly every classical assumption. Returns are not normally distributed; volatility clusters and exhibits memory; correlations shift regime unpredictably; and the stationarity assumption that underpins conventional inference collapses under the weight of structural breaks, central bank interventions, and reflexive feedback loops between price discovery and participant behavior. Statistical inference for financial time series therefore demands a complete rethinking of the inferential toolkit, replacing the comforting symmetries of the Gaussian world with robust techniques calibrated to the empirical realities of fat tails, conditional heteroskedasticity, serial dependence, and non-stationarity.

The foundational invariant that separates financial econometrics from classical statistics is the recognition that price processes are not governed by stationary physical laws but by adaptive human institutions operating under bounded rationality, asymmetric information, and structural regime shifts. A physicist measuring the decay rate of a radioactive isotope can assume the underlying process remains constant across experimental runs. A quantitative analyst modeling the mean reversion speed of a currency pair cannot make that assumption; the true data-generating process may have shifted between the estimation window and the live trading period due to central bank policy changes, liquidity shocks, or macroeconomic regime transitions. This fundamental instability forces the practitioner to adopt a posture of perpetual skepticism toward model parameters, treating them not as eternal constants but as local approximations valid only within a narrow spatiotemporal context.

The second invariant concerns the asymmetry between upside and downside risk in financial returns. Textbook treatments of portfolio theory lean heavily on mean-variance optimization, implicitly assuming that returns are symmetric and that investors care equally about deviations above and below the mean. Empirical return distributions, however, exhibit pronounced negative skewness in equity indices and positive excess kurtosis across virtually all asset classes. The left tail is fatter than the right; crashes happen faster and with greater magnitude than rallies; and the fourth moment dominates risk management considerations for any strategy with embedded optionality or leverage. This stylized fact invalidates parametric inference procedures that rely on Gaussian error assumptions and necessitates the use of robust estimators, bootstrapped confidence intervals, and extreme value theory to characterize tail behavior.

The third invariant is the clustering of volatility and the time-varying nature of second moments. Robert Engle's observation that large price changes tend to be followed by large price changes of either sign, and small changes by small changes, gave rise to the autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity framework. This volatility clustering phenomenon reflects the informational and liquidity microstructure of markets: news arrives in bursts, market makers widen spreads during uncertainty, and herding behavior amplifies short-term correlation. The consequence for statistical inference is that the independence assumption underlying ordinary least squares regression is violated; residuals are serially correlated in their squares even when uncorrelated in their levels. Ignoring this structure leads to underestimated standard errors, spurious significance, and catastrophic risk model failures during precisely the stress periods when risk models matter most.

The fourth invariant addresses the distinction between cross-sectional and time-series inference. In cross-sectional studies, each observation is drawn from a separate unit, and the central limit theorem provides asymptotic guarantees as the number of independent units grows. In time series, observations are sequentially dependent, and the effective sample size is often far smaller than the nominal count of data points. A trader with ten years of daily data possesses not 2,500 independent observations but perhaps only a few dozen independent market regimes. This temporal dependence erodes statistical power and inflates the risk of overfitting, particularly when practitioners estimate complex multi-parameter models on finite historical samples. The Bayesian-frequentist divide becomes operationally significant in this context: frequentist p-values and confidence intervals assume infinite hypothetical repetitions of the data-generating process, a fiction that breaks down when the process itself is non-stationary and the estimation window spans multiple structural regimes.

The fifth invariant concerns the problem of multiple hypothesis testing and data mining bias. A trading strategy development workflow typically involves screening thousands of candidate signals, parameter combinations, and asset universes before selecting a final model for deployment. Each statistical test performed increases the probability of discovering a spurious pattern that capitalizes on historical noise rather than structural alpha. The Bonferroni correction and false discovery rate control procedures offer partial protection, but they impose severe power penalties and fail to account for the correlation structure among tested hypotheses. The practitioner must therefore adopt a hierarchical approach to inference, distinguishing between exploratory data analysis used for hypothesis generation and confirmatory testing on hold-out samples, while maintaining rigorous documentation of the complete research path to avoid selection bias.

Maximum likelihood estimation provides the central inferential framework for parametric time series models. The likelihood function quantifies the probability of observing the realized data sequence conditional on a proposed model and parameter vector. Under regularity conditions, the maximum likelihood estimator is consistent, asymptotically normal, and efficient, achieving the Cramér-Rao lower bound on estimator variance. The practical appeal of maximum likelihood extends beyond these asymptotic properties; the framework naturally accommodates complex dynamic structures, censored or missing data, and non-Gaussian innovations through appropriate specification of the conditional density. The log-likelihood also provides a unified objective function for model comparison through information criteria that penalize complexity, such as the Akaike Information Criterion, which trades off in-sample fit against the number of estimated parameters through a bias correction term derived from asymptotic theory.

Hypothesis testing in time series contexts requires careful attention to the null distribution of test statistics under serial dependence. The conventional t-statistic and F-statistic retain their asymptotic validity under heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation when robust standard errors are employed, but their finite-sample properties degrade rapidly as the degree of temporal dependence increases. The Newey-West estimator of the long-run variance-covariance matrix provides a consistent estimator under mild regularity conditions, but the practitioner must select an appropriate lag truncation parameter that balances bias and variance. An alternative approach constructs confidence intervals and hypothesis tests through bootstrap resampling, which preserves the temporal dependence structure of the data without requiring parametric assumptions about the error distribution. The block bootstrap, which resamples contiguous blocks of observations rather than individual data points, maintains short-run temporal dependence while achieving asymptotic refinement over first-order asymptotic approximations.

The distinction between statistical significance and economic significance becomes paramount when deploying quantitative trading strategies at scale. A backtest may demonstrate that a signal predicts returns with a t-statistic exceeding three, satisfying conventional significance thresholds, yet the effect size may be too small to survive transaction costs, market impact, and adverse selection. The practitioner must therefore supplement hypothesis tests with economically grounded metrics: Sharpe ratios, information ratios, maximum drawdown, and profit-and-loss distributions that account for realistic trading frictions. A statistically significant alpha of two basis points per trade becomes economically insignificant when bid-ask spreads consume three basis points and price impact adds another basis point on execution. The inferential workflow must therefore integrate statistical testing within a broader framework of economic viability assessment, recognizing that model selection is ultimately a decision-theoretic problem rather than a purely statistical one.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The autoregressive moving average framework provides the foundational parametric structure for modeling univariate stationary time series. An ARMA(p,q) process expresses the current observation as a linear combination of p lagged values, q lagged error terms, and a white noise innovation. Formally, if we denote the demeaned return series as $r_t$, the ARMA(p,q) model is specified as:

$$r_t = \phi_1 r_{t-1} + \phi_2 r_{t-2} + \cdots + \phi_p r_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1} + \theta_2 \varepsilon_{t-2} + \cdots + \theta_q \varepsilon_{t-q}$$

where $\varepsilon_t$ is a white noise process with mean zero and constant variance $\sigma^2$, the autoregressive coefficients $\phi_i$ govern the persistence of past shocks, and the moving average coefficients $\theta_j$ control the transient response to innovations. The autoregressive component captures direct feedback from past values, reflecting momentum or mean reversion dynamics depending on coefficient signs and magnitudes. The moving average component models the propagation of unobservable shocks through the system, allowing the current observation to depend on past forecast errors.

Identification of the ARMA order requires examination of the sample autocorrelation function and partial autocorrelation function. The autocorrelation at lag k measures the linear correlation between $r_t$ and $r_{t-k}$, providing a signature of the temporal dependence structure. A pure AR(p) process exhibits autocorrelations that decay exponentially or according to damped sinusoids, while the partial autocorrelation function cuts off sharply after lag p. Conversely, a pure MA(q) process shows autocorrelations that terminate after lag q and partial autocorrelations that decay gradually. Mixed ARMA processes exhibit both gradual decay patterns, requiring joint consideration of both functions and information criteria to determine appropriate orders.

Maximum likelihood estimation of ARMA parameters proceeds by constructing the conditional likelihood function from the one-step-ahead prediction errors. Given initial values and parameter guesses, the Kalman filter recursively computes the conditional expectation of each observation given past information and accumulates the sum of squared standardized prediction errors. The log-likelihood is proportional to the negative of this sum, adjusted for the logarithm of the conditional variance. Numerical optimization routines such as quasi-Newton methods or expectation-maximization algorithms search the parameter space to locate the maximum, typically requiring starting values obtained from moment-matching or grid search. The observed information matrix at the maximum provides standard errors through the inverse of the negative Hessian, enabling construction of confidence intervals and hypothesis tests on individual coefficients.

Diagnostic checking of the fitted ARMA model examines whether the standardized residuals behave as white noise, validating the maintained assumption that all temporal structure has been captured. The Ljung-Box Q-statistic tests for remaining autocorrelation in the residuals by comparing the sum of squared sample autocorrelations against a chi-squared reference distribution. Rejection indicates model misspecification, suggesting the need for higher orders or alternative structures. Additional diagnostics include normality tests such as the Jarque-Bera statistic, which jointly tests for zero skewness and excess kurtosis, and heteroskedasticity tests that examine whether the squared residuals exhibit serial dependence, signaling the presence of ARCH effects.

The generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity framework extends ARMA models to accommodate time-varying volatility. Introduced by Tim Bollerslev as a generalization of Engle's ARCH process, the GARCH(p,q) model specifies the conditional variance as a function of past squared innovations and past conditional variances:

$$\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha_1 \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \alpha_2 \varepsilon_{t-2}^2 + \cdots + \alpha_q \varepsilon_{t-q}^2 + \beta_1 \sigma_{t-1}^2 + \beta_2 \sigma_{t-2}^2 + \cdots + \beta_p \sigma_{t-p}^2$$

where $\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t$ and $z_t$ is a standardized innovation drawn from a specified distribution, typically Gaussian or Student's t. The ARCH coefficients $\alpha_i$ measure the immediate impact of past shocks on current volatility, while the GARCH coefficients $\beta_j$ capture persistence in the volatility process. The sum $\alpha_1 + \beta_1$ in the GARCH(1,1) case quantifies the degree of volatility clustering; values near unity indicate that shocks to volatility decay very slowly, producing the long-memory behavior observed empirically in most financial return series.

Estimation of GARCH models follows the maximum likelihood principle, with the log-likelihood constructed from the conditional density of the standardized innovations. Each observation contributes a term involving the log of the conditional variance and the squared standardized residual, and the full sample log-likelihood is the sum across all time periods. Constraints on the parameter space ensure positivity of the conditional variance and stationarity of the volatility process. The GARCH(1,1) specification has become the industry workhorse due to its parsimony and empirical success in capturing volatility dynamics across diverse asset classes. Extensions such as EGARCH allow for asymmetric responses to positive and negative shocks, capturing the leverage effect whereby negative returns increase volatility more than positive returns of equal magnitude.

Cointegration provides the statistical foundation for pairs trading and statistical arbitrage strategies predicated on mean reversion relationships between non-stationary price series. Two integrated series of order one are cointegrated if a linear combination of them is stationary, implying the existence of a long-run equilibrium relationship despite individual trending behavior. The Engle-Granger two-step procedure first estimates the cointegrating vector by regressing one series on the other using ordinary least squares, then tests whether the residuals are stationary using an augmented Dickey-Fuller test with modified critical values. Rejection of the unit root null in the residuals constitutes evidence of cointegration, validating the mean reversion premise underlying the trading strategy.

The Johansen procedure offers a multivariate maximum likelihood framework for testing cointegration among multiple series simultaneously. The method estimates a vector error correction model that decomposes the first differences of the series into a cointegrating component capturing long-run relationships and a short-run dynamic adjustment component. The cointegrating rank, the number of independent cointegrating vectors, is determined through sequential likelihood ratio tests comparing restricted and unrestricted models. The trace statistic tests the null hypothesis that there are at most r cointegrating relationships against the alternative of more than r, while the maximum eigenvalue statistic tests r against r+1. Critical values are tabulated from Monte Carlo simulations under the null of no cointegration, accounting for the non-standard distributions that arise from unit root asymptotics.

Mean reversion strategies exploit temporary deviations from equilibrium relationships, entering positions when the spread widens beyond historical norms and exiting when reversion occurs. The speed of mean reversion, quantified by the autoregressive coefficient in an AR(1) model of the spread, determines the expected holding period and risk-adjusted profitability. A half-life of mean reversion can be computed as $\ln(0.5) / \ln(\phi)$, providing an intuitive metric for the expected time until half the deviation dissipates. Statistical inference on the mean reversion coefficient must account for small-sample bias in autoregressive models; the ordinary least squares estimator is downward-biased in finite samples, understating the degree of persistence and overstating mean reversion speeds. Bias-corrected estimators or bootstrap confidence intervals provide more reliable inference in typical sample sizes encountered in trading applications.

Autocorrelation structure diagnostics extend beyond simple correlogram examination to encompass formal tests for white noise and spectral density estimation. The portmanteau tests of Ljung and Box aggregate information across multiple lags into a single statistic with an asymptotic chi-squared distribution under the white noise null. Spectral density estimation via Fourier transform decomposes the variance of the series into contributions from different frequencies, revealing cyclical patterns and dominant periodicities that may correspond to market microstructure effects, calendar anomalies, or macroeconomic cycles. The periodogram provides a raw spectral estimate subject to high variability, while smoothed spectral estimators such as the Bartlett or Parzen windows trade off resolution against variance reduction.

Regime detection methods recognize that financial markets transition between qualitatively distinct states characterized by different return and volatility dynamics. The Hamilton Markov-switching model posits a latent discrete state variable that evolves according to a first-order Markov chain, with observable returns generated from state-dependent distributions. A two-state specification might distinguish between high-volatility bear markets and low-volatility bull markets, with transition probabilities governing the persistence of each regime and the likelihood of switching. Maximum likelihood estimation employs the EM algorithm, iterating between an expectation step that computes smoothed regime probabilities given current parameter values and a maximization step that updates parameters given the smoothed probabilities. The filtered regime probabilities provide real-time regime classifications, enabling dynamic strategy allocation based on the current market state.

Hidden Markov models extend the basic Markov-switching framework to accommodate multiple states and richer dependence structures within each regime. The Viterbi algorithm computes the most likely sequence of latent states conditional on the full observed data, while the forward-backward algorithm produces smoothed probabilities for each state at each time point. Model selection involves trading off fit against complexity; the Bayesian information criterion penalizes the number of parameters more heavily than the Akaike criterion, favoring parsimony in regime count. Practical implementation requires careful initialization and assessment of identification; label-switching permutations of regime indices produce identical likelihoods, necessitating parameter constraints or post-hoc relabeling based on interpretable state characteristics.

Statistical properties of return distributions deviate systematically from the Gaussian ideal, with fat tails representing the most consequential departure for risk management and derivative pricing. The excess kurtosis of most financial return series exceeds zero, indicating greater probability mass in the tails relative to a normal distribution with matched mean and variance. The Student's t-distribution provides a flexible parametric family that nests the normal as a limiting case while accommodating fat tails through the degrees-of-freedom parameter. Lower degrees of freedom produce heavier tails; values between three and ten typically provide good fits to equity return data. Maximum likelihood estimation of the degrees-of-freedom parameter jointly with location and scale parameters enables formal comparison against the normal null through likelihood ratio tests.

Extreme value theory offers a complementary approach focused explicitly on tail behavior rather than the full distribution. The Pickands-Balkema-de Haan theorem establishes that the distribution of exceedances over a high threshold converges to a generalized Pareto distribution as the threshold increases, with shape parameter governing tail heaviness. The Hill estimator provides a simple method-of-moments estimate of the tail index for Pareto-type tails, computed from the order statistics of the largest observations. Empirical applications typically find tail indices between two and four for daily equity returns, implying finite variance but infinite higher moments. This tail thickness has profound implications for Value-at-Risk calculations; Gaussian VaR estimates understate tail risk by factors of two to five relative to empirically calibrated fat-tailed distributions.

Quantile regression provides a distribution-free approach to characterizing return distributions without imposing parametric functional forms. Instead of modeling the conditional mean as in ordinary least squares, quantile regression estimates conditional quantiles, mapping predictor variables to specific points in the conditional distribution. The 0.05 quantile corresponds to a one-in-twenty downside event, providing a natural estimator of Value-at-Risk. The objective function minimizes a weighted sum of absolute deviations, with asymmetric penalties for positive and negative residuals inducing the desired quantile. Linear programming algorithms efficiently solve the optimization problem even in high dimensions. Inference proceeds via bootstrap or asymptotic theory, with standard errors accounting for heteroskedasticity and serial dependence through robust covariance estimators.

The connection between statistical inference and trading system validation demands rigorous out-of-sample testing protocols that respect the temporal nature of financial data. Walk-forward analysis partitions the historical sample into successive training and testing windows, recursively estimating model parameters on past data and evaluating performance on subsequent unseen periods. This procedure mimics the actual deployment workflow and provides a realistic assessment of degradation from in-sample to live performance. The training window length involves a bias-variance tradeoff; longer windows increase parameter precision but reduce adaptability to regime changes, while shorter windows track non-stationarity at the cost of estimation noise. Typical choices range from one to five years for daily trading strategies, with periodic refitting every month or quarter.

Cross-validation techniques from machine learning must be adapted carefully to avoid lookahead bias in time series contexts. Standard k-fold cross-validation randomly partitions observations into training and validation sets, violating the temporal ordering and inducing spurious performance through information leakage. Time series cross-validation instead creates folds that respect chronological order, always training on past data and validating on future data. The expanding window variant grows the training set with each fold, while the rolling window maintains constant training set size, sliding forward through time. The choice depends on the presumed stationarity of the data-generating process; expanding windows work well for stationary processes, while rolling windows adapt more quickly to structural breaks.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Computational efficiency in maximum likelihood estimation of time series models hinges on exploiting recursive structure in the prediction error decomposition. Naive computation of the likelihood by constructing the full Toeplitz covariance matrix and evaluating the multivariate normal density scales as $O(T^3)$ in the sample size T, rendering estimation infeasible for high-frequency datasets with millions of observations. The Kalman filter provides an $O(T)$ alternative by recursively updating the one-step-ahead prediction and its variance, accumulating the likelihood contribution from each innovation without requiring matrix inversion. State-space formulations cast ARMA and GARCH models in Kalman filter form, enabling fast likelihood evaluation even for complex multivariate specifications.

Numerical optimization of the likelihood function confronts multiple local maxima, flat regions, and ridges in the parameter space, particularly for models with moving average components. The MA coefficients induce non-convexity because changes in their signs can be offset by changes in the signs of the innovations, creating symmetric likelihood ridges. Starting values matter critically; poor initialization can trap quasi-Newton algorithms in local optima far from the global maximum. Grid search over plausible parameter ranges identifies promising regions, which are then refined through gradient-based methods. The expectation-maximization algorithm provides an alternative that monotonically increases the likelihood at each iteration, offering more robust convergence at the cost of slower asymptotic speed relative to Newton methods.

Precision in floating-point arithmetic becomes problematic when computing likelihoods that involve products of many small probabilities or sums of large negative log-densities. Underflow occurs when the conditional density evaluates to a number smaller than the machine epsilon, causing the likelihood to round to zero. Numerically stable implementations work in log-space, accumulating log-likelihoods rather than raw likelihoods and using the log-sum-exp trick when combining probabilities. The R and Python ecosystem libraries such as statsmodels, rugarch, and MFE implement these numerical safeguards, but custom implementations require careful attention to avoid catastrophic cancellation and overflow.

Parameter identification in ARMA models requires invertibility and stationarity constraints to ensure a unique parametrization. The autoregressive polynomial $1 - \phi_1 L - \phi_2 L^2 - \cdots - \phi_p L^p$ must have roots outside the unit circle for stationarity, where L denotes the lag operator. The moving average polynomial must similarly have roots outside the unit circle for invertibility, ensuring that the infinite AR representation converges. Maximum likelihood estimation imposes these constraints through parametrization transformations or constrained optimization. Violations manifest as explosive forecasts, negative conditional variances in GARCH models, or singular Hessian matrices that prevent standard error calculation. Diagnostic checks should verify that estimated roots satisfy the stability conditions within numerical tolerance.

Overfitting represents the central failure mode in time series model selection, arising from the tension between capturing genuine temporal structure and fitting sample-specific noise. Complex models with many parameters achieve lower in-sample residual variance but degrade catastrophically out-of-sample as fitted patterns fail to generalize. Information criteria address this tradeoff by penalizing likelihood improvements according to the number of estimated parameters. The Akaike Information Criterion imposes a penalty of two per parameter, derived from asymptotic bias correction in Kullback-Leibler divergence. The Bayesian Information Criterion uses a stronger penalty of $\ln(T)$ per parameter, asymptotically selecting the true model under correct specification. Practical model selection evaluates multiple criteria alongside out-of-sample forecasting metrics, recognizing that no single criterion dominates uniformly.

Structural breaks and non-stationarity undermine the fundamental premise of statistical inference that parameters remain constant over the estimation window. A mean reversion strategy calibrated during a trending regime will produce catastrophic losses when markets enter a new trending phase with opposite sign. Formal tests for structural breaks such as the Chow test, CUSUM procedure, or Bai-Perron sequential break detection identify discrete shifts in model parameters at unknown dates. Recursive estimation and rolling-window diagnostics visualize parameter stability over time, flagging periods of instability that warrant model recalibration or strategy deactivation. Regime-switching models provide a parametric framework for accommodating non-stationarity, though they introduce additional estimation uncertainty and identification challenges.

Small-sample bias in autoregressive coefficient estimation leads to systematic understatement of unit root and near-unit-root persistence. The ordinary least squares estimator of $\phi$ in an AR(1) model is biased downward, with the bias inversely proportional to sample size and increasing with the true persistence. This bias matters profoundly for mean reversion trading; an estimated half-life of five days may correspond to a true half-life of ten days after bias correction, doubling the expected holding period and risk exposure. Bias-corrected estimators such as the Andrews-Chen median-unbiased estimator or bootstrap percentile intervals provide more reliable inference. Practitioners should routinely report confidence intervals for persistence parameters rather than point estimates alone, acknowledging estimation uncertainty in strategy performance projections.

Data snooping bias arises from the iterative search process inherent in quantitative strategy development. A researcher testing 100 potential signals at the five-percent significance level expects five spurious rejections under the null hypothesis of no predictive power. Selecting the best-performing signal from this set capitalizes on these chance findings, producing inflated backtest performance that evaporates in live trading. The Bonferroni correction divides the nominal significance level by the number of tests, controlling the family-wise error rate but sacrificing statistical power. The false discovery rate framework offers a less conservative alternative, controlling the expected proportion of false positives among all rejections rather than the probability of any false positive. White's reality check and Hansen's superior predictive ability test provide formal hypothesis tests of whether the best-performing model from a candidate set genuinely outperforms a benchmark after accounting for data snooping.

Transaction costs and market microstructure frictions erode the profitability of high-turnover mean reversion strategies, creating a wedge between statistical significance and economic viability. A cointegration relationship may be statistically strong yet too slow to revert or too narrow in spread to overcome bid-ask spreads and commissions. The empirical finance literature distinguishes between statistical arbitrage, which exploits transient mispricings within a risk model, and pure arbitrage, which locks in riskless profits. Most quantitative equity strategies fall into the former category, bearing residual factor exposures and regime risk. Performance evaluation must therefore incorporate realistic cost models that account for bid-ask spreads, exchange fees, market impact as a function of order size and liquidity, and financing costs for short positions. Sharpe ratios computed on frictionless backtest returns can exceed four, while post-cost realized Sharpe ratios collapse below one.

Regime persistence and transition dynamics determine the viability of regime-detection-based trading strategies. If regimes flip rapidly, the smoothed regime probabilities provide little real-time information because the signal lags the true state by several observations. High transition probabilities also increase the probability that regime classifications are spurious artifacts of estimation noise rather than genuine state shifts. Practitioners should validate regime models on hold-out data, confirming that identified regimes exhibit economically interpretable differences in return distributions, volatility, and correlation structures. A two-state model that merely separates slightly above-average and slightly below-average volatility periods offers limited actionable information, whereas a model distinguishing stable trending markets from turbulent mean-reverting markets enables meaningful strategy adaptation.

Tail risk modeling failures manifest most acutely during market dislocations when correlations surge toward one and liquidity evaporates. Gaussian copula models that exhibit acceptable fit during normal periods understate joint tail risk because they assume tail independence after conditioning on marginal distributions. Empirical tail dependence in equity markets is strongly positive; large drawdowns occur simultaneously across assets, invalidating diversification assumptions embedded in Gaussian risk models. Student's t copula provides a more realistic alternative, inducing positive tail dependence through the common degrees-of-freedom parameter. Extreme value theory supplements parametric approaches by fitting separate models to tail exceedances, enabling extrapolation beyond the largest observed losses. Stress testing should complement statistical risk measures by simulating scenarios that are rare under estimated models but plausible given institutional structures and historical precedents.

Computational scalability challenges emerge when extending univariate GARCH models to multivariate portfolio contexts. The number of parameters in a full multivariate GARCH specification grows quadratically in the asset count, quickly exhausting estimation precision and computational feasibility. The DCC-GARCH model of Engle offers a middle ground, estimating univariate GARCH models for each asset and then modeling the time-varying correlations through a separate dynamic process. This two-step procedure reduces parameter count and computation time while capturing the empirically relevant co-volatility dynamics. The BEKK parametrization ensures positive-definite covariance matrices through quadratic forms, while the Cholesky decomposition approach works with the volatility factors directly. Parallel computation exploits independence across assets in the first stage and within MCMC or bootstrapping iterations, mapping naturally to multi-core CPU or GPU architectures.

Hyperparameter tuning for lag order selection, threshold levels in extreme value models, and block length in bootstrap resampling introduces additional degrees of freedom that are often ignored in reported performance statistics. A researcher who tests ARMA orders from zero to five in both AR and MA components has implicitly conducted a model selection procedure that should be reflected in adjusted significance levels. Cross-validation on a hold-out period provides partial protection, but the choice of validation period length and the decision to refit on the full sample post-validation involve additional judgement calls. Preregistration of the analysis plan before examining the data offers the strongest protection against data mining, though it is rarely feasible in commercial trading strategy development where iterative exploration is integral to the research process. Transparency regarding the full research path, including failed approaches and sensitivity analyses, enables more realistic assessment of out-of-sample degradation risk.
