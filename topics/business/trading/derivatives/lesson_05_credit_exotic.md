# Lesson 5: Credit Derivatives, Exotic Structures, Path Dependence

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Credit derivatives emerged in the early 1990s as a mechanism to decouple credit risk from the underlying loan or bond exposure, enabling banks to manage their balance sheet capital requirements without liquidating illiquid corporate debt positions. The canonical instrument—the credit default swap—functions as bilateral insurance, where a protection buyer pays periodic premiums to a protection seller in exchange for compensation upon a credit event such as bankruptcy, failure to pay, or restructuring. Unlike traditional insurance, CDS contracts are tradable, establishing a secondary market for pure credit risk that exists independently of the underlying reference obligation.

The fundamental invariant governing all credit derivatives is the no-arbitrage relationship between the CDS spread, the bond's credit spread over the risk-free rate, and the expected loss given default. If a corporate bond yields 200 basis points over Treasuries and the recovery rate is 40%, the theoretical CDS spread converges to the present value of the default probability multiplied by the loss severity, adjusted for the payment frequency of the premium leg. Market participants exploit deviations from this equilibrium through basis trading, simultaneously buying the bond and purchasing CDS protection when the bond spread exceeds the CDS spread, or executing the reverse when the relationship inverts.

The transition from vanilla credit derivatives to exotic options introduces path dependence, where the payoff depends not merely on the terminal spot price but on the entire trajectory of the underlying asset through the contract's life. A European call option cares only about whether the stock finishes above the strike at expiration; an Asian option averages the spot price over a predetermined sampling window, dampening volatility and reducing option premiums by 20–40% relative to vanilla equivalents. Barrier options embed knockout or knock-in thresholds that either extinguish the contract or activate it upon breaching a specified level, creating discontinuous payoff functions that challenge traditional Black-Scholes delta hedging.

Stochastic volatility models acknowledge that implied volatility itself follows a random process, contradicting the Black-Scholes assumption of constant volatility. The volatility smile—the empirical observation that out-of-the-money puts and calls trade at higher implied volatilities than at-the-money options—cannot be reconciled with lognormal asset returns. Models such as Heston, SABR (Stochastic Alpha Beta Rho), and local volatility surfaces calibrate to the entire term structure of implied volatilities, enabling traders to price exotic options consistently with the observable vanilla option market. The smile's shape encodes the market's expectation of fat tails and crash risk; a pronounced skew in equity index options reflects the higher demand for downside protection following the 1987 crash.

Correlation products such as basket options, worst-of structures, and collateralized debt obligations depend critically on the joint distribution of multiple underlyings. A basket call on ten equities with 50% pairwise correlation behaves fundamentally differently than the same basket with 10% correlation, even if each equity's marginal distribution remains identical. Copula functions—particularly the Gaussian copula popularized in CDO pricing—specify the dependence structure between marginal distributions, but the 2008 financial crisis exposed the fragility of assuming static correlation parameters when tail dependence and contagion dominate during stress events.

The mathematical treatment of path-dependent options requires abandoning the simple terminal payoff function $\max(S_T - K, 0)$ in favor of functionals that integrate over the entire price history. For an arithmetic Asian call, the payoff becomes $\max\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} S_{t_i} - K, 0\right)$, where the summation introduces computational complexity that resists closed-form solutions. Monte Carlo simulation becomes the primary valuation tool, but variance reduction techniques such as antithetic variates, control variates, and quasi-random low-discrepancy sequences are essential to achieve acceptable convergence rates within practical computation budgets.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

### Credit Default Swap Structure and Cash Flow Mechanics

A credit default swap decomposes into two legs: the premium leg, where the protection buyer pays a fixed spread quarterly or semi-annually, and the protection leg, where the protection seller compensates the buyer upon a credit event. The notional principal—typically $10 million to $100 million for single-name corporate CDS—determines the absolute dollar exposure, but the market quotes spreads in basis points per annum. A five-year CDS on a BBB-rated industrial company might trade at 150 basis points, implying quarterly payments of $37,500 on a $10 million notional.

The precise payment mechanics incorporate accrued interest and settlement conventions that evolved from ISDA (International Swaps and Derivatives Association) standardization efforts post-2009. Following the Big Bang protocol, North American corporate CDS standardized on 100 or 500 basis point fixed coupons with an upfront payment adjusting the present value to par. If the market spread for a reference entity is 180 basis points but the contract coupon is fixed at 100 basis points, the protection buyer receives an upfront payment reflecting the present value of the 80 basis point annuity differential. This standardization enhanced liquidity by concentrating trading in fungible contracts rather than bespoke spreads.

The credit event determination follows a defined process where the ISDA Determinations Committee—a supermajority vote of dealer and buy-side representatives—rules on whether a credit event occurred and, if so, the settlement method. Physical settlement requires the protection buyer to deliver bonds or loans with a face value equal to the notional amount, receiving par in return. Cash settlement, more common post-crisis, relies on a dealer poll conducted approximately 30 days after the credit event to establish the recovery rate, with the protection seller paying the notional multiplied by one minus the recovery rate.

The risk-neutral pricing framework values the premium leg as the present value of the spread payments, weighted by the survival probability at each coupon date, and values the protection leg as the present value of the loss given default integrated over the probability density of default timing. Mathematically, for a CDS spread $s$, notional $N$, and continuous hazard rate $\lambda$, the fair spread equates:

$$
s \cdot N \cdot \sum_{i=1}^{n} \tau_i \cdot e^{-r t_i} \cdot e^{-\lambda t_i} = N \cdot (1 - R) \cdot \lambda \int_0^T e^{-(r + \lambda)t} dt
$$

where $\tau_i$ represents the day count fraction between coupon payments, $r$ is the risk-free rate, $R$ is the recovery rate, and $T$ is the contract maturity. Solving for $s$ yields the breakeven spread that equates the present value of both legs under the assumed default intensity.

### Barrier Option Mechanics and Discontinuous Greeks

Barrier options introduce conditional activation or deactivation based on whether the underlying asset breaches a specified barrier level during the contract's life. An up-and-out call with a strike of $100 and a barrier at $120 becomes worthless if the stock ever trades at or above $120, even if it subsequently falls back below that level before expiration. Conversely, an up-and-in call only becomes active if the barrier is breached, otherwise expiring worthless regardless of the terminal spot price.

The payoff discontinuity at the barrier creates challenges for delta hedging, as the option's delta experiences violent swings when the spot price hovers near the barrier. Consider an up-and-out call trading at $105 with the barrier at $110. As the spot approaches $110, the option's value collapses because the probability of knockout surges, driving the delta sharply negative in the final dollars before barrier breach. This behavior contradicts the smooth, monotonic delta profile of vanilla options and forces traders to dynamically adjust hedge ratios with sub-daily frequency when spot prices trade within 2–3% of the barrier.

Closed-form solutions for barrier options exist under Black-Scholes assumptions, leveraging the reflection principle from probability theory. For an up-and-out call with spot $S$, strike $K$, barrier $H > S$, volatility $\sigma$, risk-free rate $r$, and time to expiration $T$, the price decomposes into the vanilla call value minus the probability-weighted value of the reflected path that breaches the barrier:

$$
C_{\text{out}} = C_{\text{vanilla}} - \left(\frac{S}{H}\right)^{2\lambda} C_{\text{reflected}}
$$

where $\lambda = \frac{r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}$ and $C_{\text{reflected}}$ represents a modified call option with adjusted strikes reflecting the barrier breach condition. The formula reveals that barrier options are cheaper than vanilla equivalents, with discounts ranging from 15% to 60% depending on the barrier's proximity to the current spot and the time to expiration.

### Asian Option Valuation and Variance Reduction

Asian options average the underlying asset's price over a specified observation schedule, either arithmetic or geometric. The arithmetic average—common in commodity derivatives where producers hedge against fluctuating spot prices—lacks a closed-form solution under Black-Scholes because the sum of lognormal random variables does not produce a lognormal distribution. The geometric average permits a closed-form approximation since the product of lognormal variables remains lognormal, but this formulation sees limited practical adoption outside academic settings.

Monte Carlo simulation becomes the workhorse for arithmetic Asian option valuation, generating thousands of price paths and computing the average payoff across all scenarios. A naive Monte Carlo implementation requires 10,000 to 100,000 paths to achieve a standard error of estimate below one cent for a $10 strike option, consuming significant computational resources for real-time pricing in a trading environment. Control variate techniques exploit the known closed-form solution for geometric Asians as a correlated proxy, computing both the arithmetic and geometric payoffs along each path, then adjusting the arithmetic estimate by the error observed in the geometric estimate relative to its theoretical value.

The variance reduction from control variates can compress the required path count by an order of magnitude. If the correlation between arithmetic and geometric Asian payoffs exceeds 0.9—typical for moderate volatilities and maturities under one year—a simulation with 5,000 paths using control variates achieves the same accuracy as 50,000 paths without variance reduction. The computational speedup translates directly into trading desk responsiveness, enabling market makers to requote prices within milliseconds as underlying spot prices tick.

The averaging feature reduces the effective volatility exposure, as extreme outcomes in individual observations get smoothed by the averaging process. An at-the-money Asian call typically exhibits 30–40% lower vega than a vanilla call with the same strike and expiration, making Asians attractive for hedging long-dated volatility exposures or for buyers seeking cheaper premium structures when the directional view dominates the volatility view.

### Stochastic Volatility and the Heston Model

The Heston model introduces a second stochastic process governing instantaneous variance, transforming the single-factor Black-Scholes universe into a two-factor framework where both spot and variance evolve according to correlated Brownian motions. The Heston dynamics specify:

$$
dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sqrt{v_t} S_t dW_t^S
$$

$$
dv_t = \kappa(\theta - v_t) dt + \xi \sqrt{v_t} dW_t^v
$$

where $v_t$ represents instantaneous variance, $\kappa$ is the mean reversion speed, $\theta$ is the long-run variance, $\xi$ is the volatility of volatility, and $dW_t^S$ and $dW_t^v$ are Brownian motions with correlation $\rho$. The negative correlation typically observed in equity markets—around -0.6 to -0.8—captures the leverage effect where falling stock prices increase volatility due to rising debt-to-equity ratios and panic selling.

Calibrating Heston parameters to the observable implied volatility surface requires minimizing the squared difference between model-implied and market-implied volatilities across strikes and maturities. The calibration problem is non-convex with multiple local minima, necessitating global optimization algorithms such as differential evolution or simulated annealing. A typical equity index calibration might target 20 to 40 liquid option strikes spanning one month to two years, with weights inversely proportional to bid-ask spreads to prioritize the most liquid instruments.

The Heston model's semi-analytic pricing formula, derived through characteristic function inversion, evaluates European option prices orders of magnitude faster than Monte Carlo simulation, enabling real-time recalibration as market conditions shift. The characteristic function approach transforms the expectation of the option payoff into a Fourier integral, which numerical integration techniques such as adaptive Gauss-Kronrod quadrature solve in microseconds. This computational efficiency makes Heston the industry standard for volatility surface modeling despite the model's failure to capture extreme tail events and volatility clustering observed during crisis periods.

### Smile Dynamics and Sticky Strike vs. Sticky Delta

The volatility smile's behavior under spot price changes profoundly impacts the P&L attribution of exotic option books. Two extreme regimes characterize market behavior: sticky strike, where implied volatilities remain constant for each absolute strike level as spot moves, and sticky delta, where implied volatilities remain constant for each fixed moneyness level (e.g., 25-delta put volatility stays constant as spot changes). Reality occupies an intermediate regime, with equity markets exhibiting sticky strike behavior over short horizons and transitioning toward sticky delta over multi-week periods.

A trader long a knock-out barrier call profits from sticky strike dynamics when spot rallies, as the option's vega exposure decreases while the implied volatility surface remains anchored to absolute strike levels, causing a favorable convexity effect. Conversely, sticky delta dynamics hurt this position because the implied volatility at the new effective moneyness increases, offsetting some of the delta gains. Quantifying this exposure requires computing the vanna (delta sensitivity to volatility changes) and volga (vega sensitivity to volatility changes) Greeks, which capture the second-order cross-effects between spot and volatility.

Empirical studies of smile dynamics reveal that short-dated options exhibit stickier strike behavior, while long-dated options trend toward sticky delta as fundamental uncertainty dominates transient supply-demand imbalances. A one-month S&P 500 option's smile typically remains 70% sticky strike over a 2% spot move, while a one-year option shows only 40% sticky strike behavior. These observations guide traders' hedging strategies, with short-dated exotic books requiring more frequent vega hedging adjustments and long-dated books leaning on static vanna hedges constructed using liquid vanilla options.

### Correlation Trading and Dispersion Strategies

Correlation products exploit the difference between the implied volatility of an index and the weighted average implied volatility of its constituents. An equity index with 30% implied volatility comprises stocks individually trading at 35% implied volatility on average, with the 5-point differential attributable to diversification and less-than-perfect correlation among constituents. Dispersion trades sell index variance swaps (or index straddles) and simultaneously buy variance swaps (or straddles) on the individual components, capturing the correlation risk premium when realized correlations fall below the level implied by the index-to-constituent volatility ratio.

The mathematical relationship linking index volatility $\sigma_{\text{index}}$ to constituent volatilities $\sigma_i$ and pairwise correlations $\rho_{ij}$ follows from portfolio theory:

$$
\sigma_{\text{index}}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i^2 \sigma_i^2 + \sum_{i \neq j} w_i w_j \sigma_i \sigma_j \rho_{ij}
$$

where $w_i$ represents each stock's index weight. Solving for the implied correlation reveals that when index volatility trades at 25% and the average constituent volatility is 35%, with equal weights across ten stocks, the implied correlation is approximately 35%. A trader executing a dispersion trade expects realized correlation to average below 35% over the trade horizon, generating profits as the index underperforms the volatility-weighted sum of constituent moves.

The mechanics of dispersion trading require careful management of multiple leg exposures, as each constituent option position maintains independent delta, vega, and theta profiles. A typical dispersion trade on the S&P 500 or NASDAQ-100 might involve 20 to 30 constituent positions, each hedged dynamically to isolate pure correlation exposure while neutralizing directional and volatility risks. The transaction costs from bid-ask spreads and hedging slippage consume 1–2 volatility points of expected profit, making dispersion trades viable only when the implied-to-realized correlation spread exceeds 5–10 percentage points.

Correlation swaps—bilateral contracts paying the difference between realized and implied correlation—provide a more direct expression of correlation views but suffer from limited liquidity and complex risk management. The realized correlation calculation samples pairwise correlations between constituent returns over the swap's tenor, typically using daily or weekly returns to balance statistical stability against responsiveness to regime changes. Market makers demand wide bid-ask spreads due to the difficulty of hedging correlation exposure, with typical markets quoted 5 to 10 correlation points wide for one-year swaps on major equity indices.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

### CDS Basis Arbitrage and Cheapest-to-Deliver Optionality

The CDS-bond basis—the difference between the CDS spread and the bond's credit spread over swaps—fluctuates around zero under no-arbitrage theory but exhibits persistent deviations driven by funding costs, counterparty risk, and settlement optionality. A negative basis (CDS spread below bond spread) creates an arbitrage opportunity by buying the bond, purchasing CDS protection, and synthetically locking in a risk-free return above the swap rate. However, the trade requires funding the bond position at repo rates, which spiked dramatically during the 2008 crisis as repo markets froze and haircuts surged, converting apparent arbitrages into catastrophic losses.

The cheapest-to-deliver option embedded in physically settled CDS contracts grants the protection buyer the right to deliver any obligation meeting the contract's deliverable specifications, typically any senior unsecured bond or loan. When a credit event occurs, bonds with the lowest market price relative to their face value become the preferred delivery instrument, and protection buyers scramble to source these bonds, often driving prices temporarily higher. This short squeeze dynamic was observed in multiple sovereign restructurings, including Argentina and Greece, where the cheapest-to-deliver bonds traded at substantial premiums to other pari passu instruments for weeks following the credit event declaration.

Market participants mitigate cheapest-to-deliver risk by monitoring the deliverable universe and stress-testing the basis trade's P&L across various recovery rate scenarios. A prudent risk manager assumes recovery rates 10–15 points below historical averages during periods of market stress, recognizing that liquidation dynamics and creditor coordination failures depress actual recoveries relative to pre-default bond prices. The 2020 COVID-19 crisis validated this conservatism, as initial recovery rate estimates for retail and hospitality credits exceeded 50%, only to converge toward 30% as bankruptcy proceedings revealed asset impairments and DIP financing seniority subordinated pre-petition creditors.

### Barrier Option Hedging and Gamma Explosions

Barrier options create concentration risk when spot prices trade near the barrier, as gamma (the rate of delta change with respect to spot) explodes in magnitude. A spot price 1% below a knock-out barrier might exhibit gamma 50 times larger than a vanilla option, forcing a market maker to rehedge every 0.1% spot move to maintain delta neutrality. The hedging frequency increases the transaction cost drag, with slippage and bid-ask spreads eroding profitability and potentially turning a theoretically profitable market-making quote into a realized loss.

Traders employ several techniques to manage barrier risk. Dynamic hedging with vanilla options creates a synthetic barrier payoff by adjusting the hedge ratio continuously as spot evolves, but this approach demands sub-second execution and robust connectivity to exchange infrastructure. Alternatively, static replication constructs a portfolio of vanilla options at various strikes that collectively approximate the barrier option's payoff, avoiding the gamma explosion by distributing exposure across the strike spectrum. Static replication sacrifices some precision—typically introducing 2–5% valuation error—but eliminates the operational risk of failing to rehedge during fast market moves.

The knock-out probability itself becomes a critical risk factor, as underestimating barrier breach likelihood exposes the trader to unexpected losses. Models that assume constant volatility and continuous price diffusion systematically underestimate knock-out probabilities when spot exhibits jump-diffusion behavior or when volatility clusters around the barrier level. Empirical studies show that adding a jump component to the Heston model increases barrier breach probabilities by 10–30% for barriers 10% away from spot, with larger adjustments for closer barriers and higher jump frequencies.

### Asian Option Greeks and Early Exercise Risk

The Greeks of Asian options exhibit non-standard behavior due to the path-dependent averaging mechanism. Delta decreases as the averaging period progresses because each new observation carries diminishing weight in the cumulative average, causing the option's sensitivity to spot moves to decay faster than a vanilla option's time decay. For an Asian option 70% through its averaging period, delta might be only 30% of a comparable vanilla option's delta, forcing hedgers to dynamically reduce their spot hedge as time passes even when spot price and volatility remain constant.

Vega also declines through the averaging period, but the rate of decline depends on the relationship between the current average and the spot price. If spot significantly exceeds the running average for a call option, vega decay accelerates because future observations have diminishing impact on the final payoff—the option is already deep in-the-money based on the accumulated average. Conversely, when spot trades near the running average, vega decay slows because future volatility retains meaningful influence over the final outcome. Risk managers must track the running average alongside spot and recalibrate hedge ratios daily, particularly for long-dated Asian options where the averaging period spans months or years.

American-style Asian options introduce early exercise complexity that resists analytical solutions. The holder's optimal exercise policy depends on the running average, remaining averaging period, and current spot—a three-dimensional problem that Monte Carlo and finite difference methods struggle to solve efficiently. The Longstaff-Schwartz least-squares Monte Carlo method provides a tractable approximation by regressing continuation values against basis functions of the state variables at each exercise opportunity, then working backward to determine the optimal stopping strategy. This technique converges to the true American Asian value within 1–2% using 50,000 paths and polynomial basis functions up to fourth order.

### Stochastic Volatility Model Risk and Calibration Instability

Heston model calibration exhibits sensitivity to parameter initialization and optimization algorithm choice, occasionally converging to spurious local minima that fit the short-dated smile accurately but extrapolate poorly to long-dated options or extreme strikes. A common failure mode occurs when the volatility of volatility parameter $\xi$ calibrates to unrealistically high values (exceeding 2.0) to fit short-dated out-of-the-money puts, generating explosive variance paths that violate the Feller condition $2\kappa\theta > \xi^2$. When this condition fails, the variance process can reach zero and remain absorbed at the boundary, creating mathematical degeneracies in the pricing formula.

Robust calibration practices impose constraints during optimization, bounding $\xi$ below 1.5, enforcing Feller condition compliance, and penalizing parameter sets that generate implied volatilities below realized historical volatility floors. Multi-start optimization from diverse initial parameter guesses—typically 10 to 20 random draws from plausible parameter ranges—mitigates local minima risks, with the final calibration selected based on a combination of fit quality and parameter stability across adjacent calibration dates.

Model risk crystallizes during market dislocations when the Heston framework fails to capture sudden volatility regime shifts or correlation breakdowns. The 2010 Flash Crash saw S&P 500 index volatility surge from 20% to 40% within minutes while constituent stock volatilities increased more modestly, temporarily violating the correlation structure embedded in calibrated Heston parameters. Traders using stale calibrations to price dispersion or basket options experienced significant losses as realized correlation diverged from model-implied values. Post-crisis risk management now incorporates real-time recalibration triggers tied to volatility market changes, automatically flagging positions when model-implied and market-implied volatilities diverge beyond preset thresholds (typically 2–3 volatility points).

### Correlation Estimation and Forecasting Challenges

Realized correlation estimation requires sufficiently long sample windows to achieve statistical significance, creating a trade-off between sample size and responsiveness to regime changes. A 60-day rolling window using daily returns provides approximately 60 × 59 / 2 = 1,770 pairwise correlation observations for a 60-stock portfolio, but this estimate lags structural breaks by weeks. Shortening the window to 20 days improves responsiveness but increases estimation noise, with correlation standard errors reaching 0.15 to 0.20 for typical equity pairs.

Exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) schemes partially resolve this trade-off by assigning higher weights to recent observations while retaining long-term history. The RiskMetrics decay factor of 0.94 for daily returns corresponds to an effective sample size of approximately 33 days, balancing responsiveness and stability. However, EWMA correlation estimates exhibit momentum—past increases in correlation predict future increases—creating challenges for correlation mean-reversion trading strategies that assume correlations oscillate around stable long-run averages.

Machine learning techniques such as LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) neural networks and random forests now augment traditional correlation forecasting, incorporating market microstructure features like order flow imbalance, bid-ask spreads, and volatility smile curvature as predictive inputs. Backtests on equity index constituents demonstrate 5–10% improvement in out-of-sample correlation forecast accuracy relative to EWMA baselines, but these models require extensive feature engineering and risk overfitting to idiosyncratic patterns that fail to generalize across market regimes.

The correlation forecasting challenge compounds in multi-asset portfolios where cross-sectoral and cross-geography correlations exhibit different dynamics. Equity-commodity correlations tend to cluster around zero during calm periods and surge toward -0.5 during risk-off episodes, while intra-sector equity correlations remain elevated across regimes. Risk managers addressing this heterogeneity partition the correlation matrix into blocks—equities, rates, commodities, FX—estimating within-block and across-block correlations separately, then imposing consistency constraints during portfolio risk aggregation to ensure the global correlation matrix remains positive semi-definite.
