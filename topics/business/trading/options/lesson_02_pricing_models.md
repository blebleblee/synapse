# Lesson 2: Pricing Models from Binomial Trees to Black-Scholes

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The history of derivatives pricing is a history of reconciling intuition with arbitrage-free mathematics. Before Fischer Black, Myron Scholes, and Robert Merton published their seminal work in 1973, options traders relied on heuristics, rules of thumb, and seat-of-the-pants intuition to quote prices on exchange floors. The breakthrough insight of modern option pricing was not that markets are efficient or that traders are rational—it was that **arbitrage constraints alone** are sufficient to determine a unique, model-independent price for any derivative security, given a set of observable market prices and a minimal set of assumptions about the underlying asset's stochastic behavior.

The intellectual genealogy runs from Louis Bachelier's 1900 doctoral thesis on Brownian motion in financial markets, through Paul Samuelson's formalization of geometric Brownian motion in the 1960s, to the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein binomial lattice framework in 1979 that provided the discrete-time pedagogical bridge between intuition and the continuous-time elegance of Black-Scholes-Merton. Understanding this progression is not an academic exercise—it is the foundation for every volatility surface calibration, every exotic structure pricing model, and every risk management framework deployed in modern trading operations.

At the core of all derivatives pricing lies a single, inviolable principle: **the law of one price**. If two portfolios produce identical cash flows in every possible future state of the world, they must have identical present values today, or an arbitrageur can construct a costless, riskless profit by selling the expensive portfolio and buying the cheap one. This principle, seemingly trivial, has profound consequences. It implies that the fair value of an option can be computed without making any assumptions about investor risk preferences, expected returns, or market equilibrium. The only inputs required are the risk-free interest rate, the volatility of the underlying asset, and the structural terms of the option contract itself.

The binomial model operationalizes this insight by discretizing time and the state space of possible asset prices into a recombining lattice. At each node, the underlying asset can move to exactly two successor states—an "up" state with probability $p$ and a "down" state with probability $1 - p$. The critical observation is that we can construct a **replicating portfolio** consisting of $\Delta$ shares of the underlying asset and $B$ dollars in a risk-free bond that perfectly replicates the option's payoff at expiration. Since the replicating portfolio and the option produce identical cash flows, they must have identical prices. This construction does not require knowledge of the true probabilities governing the asset's evolution; it requires only that the market be arbitrage-free.

The mathematical machinery that makes this work is the **risk-neutral measure**—a probability distribution under which all assets, when discounted at the risk-free rate, are martingales. Under this measure, the expected return on every asset is the risk-free rate, and option prices are simply discounted expected payoffs computed using risk-neutral probabilities rather than physical probabilities. The risk-neutral probabilities are not forecasts of what will happen; they are pricing constructs that ensure arbitrage-free valuation. This subtle but essential distinction separates quantitative finance from statistical forecasting and explains why implied volatility—the volatility parameter that equates model price to market price—differs systematically from realized volatility measured from historical returns.

The Black-Scholes model emerges as the continuous-time limit of the binomial lattice as the number of time steps approaches infinity and the step size shrinks to zero. Under this limiting construction, the asset price follows a geometric Brownian motion, and the partial differential equation governing the option price can be solved in closed form for European calls and puts. The resulting Black-Scholes formula is one of the most celebrated equations in financial economics, not because it is empirically accurate—it is not—but because it provides a **common language** for quoting and comparing volatility across strikes, expirations, and underlying assets. When a trader quotes "20 vol" for a call option, she is stating the volatility input that, when plugged into the Black-Scholes formula, reproduces the market price. This convention transforms option markets from a cacophony of dollar prices into a standardized volatility market, enabling efficient price discovery and risk management.

The assumptions underpinning Black-Scholes are restrictive and well-documented: constant volatility, continuous trading, no transaction costs, log-normal asset price distributions, and no dividends in the baseline formulation. Each of these assumptions is empirically false. Realized volatility is stochastic and clustered. Trading is discrete and constrained by liquidity. Transaction costs matter, especially for high-frequency delta hedging. Equity returns exhibit fat tails and negative skewness. Dividends are discrete and often uncertain. Yet the model persists because it provides a tractable, internally consistent framework for pricing and hedging, and because market participants have developed systematic adjustments—volatility surfaces, jump-diffusion models, stochastic volatility models—to accommodate deviations from the baseline assumptions.

Understanding both the binomial and Black-Scholes frameworks is essential for practitioners because each serves a distinct purpose. The binomial model handles American exercise, discrete dividends, and path-dependent features with minimal computational overhead and maximum transparency. The Black-Scholes model provides closed-form solutions for European options, enabling rapid calculation of Greeks and facilitating analytic approaches to portfolio risk management. The interplay between discrete and continuous models, between numerical and analytic methods, and between risk-neutral and physical measures forms the intellectual scaffolding upon which all modern derivatives trading rests.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The binomial lattice is a discrete-time, discrete-state model of asset price evolution constructed through recursive subdivision of the time to expiration. Consider a non-dividend-paying stock trading at price $S_0$ today, with a European call option expiring at time $T$ with strike price $K$. We partition the interval $[0, T]$ into $N$ equal subintervals of length $\Delta t = T / N$. At each time step, the stock price can move from its current level $S$ to either $S \cdot u$ (the "up" state) or $S \cdot d$ (the "down" state), where $u > 1$ and $d < 1$ are multiplicative factors calibrated to match the volatility of the underlying asset.

The Cox-Ross-Rubinstein parameterization sets $u = e^{\sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}}$ and $d = e^{-\sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}}$, where $\sigma$ is the annualized volatility of log returns. This parameterization ensures that the lattice recombines—an up move followed by a down move produces the same terminal price as a down move followed by an up move—thereby reducing the number of nodes from exponential to polynomial in the number of steps. After $N$ time steps, there are $N + 1$ terminal nodes rather than $2^N$, making the model computationally tractable even for hundreds of steps.

At expiration, the option payoff is known with certainty at each terminal node. For a European call, the payoff at node $j$ (where $j$ ranges from $0$ to $N$, representing the number of up moves) is $\max(S_0 \cdot u^j \cdot d^{N-j} - K, 0)$. The valuation problem is to work backward through the lattice, computing the option value at each preceding node by discounting the risk-neutral expected value of the two successor nodes.

The risk-neutral probability of an up move, denoted $q$, is determined by the no-arbitrage condition that the expected return on the stock under the risk-neutral measure equals the risk-free rate $r$. Mathematically, this requires $S \cdot e^{r \Delta t} = q \cdot S \cdot u + (1 - q) \cdot S \cdot d$, which solves to $q = \frac{e^{r \Delta t} - d}{u - d}$. This probability has no connection to the actual likelihood of an up move in the physical world; it is purely a pricing artifact that ensures arbitrage-free valuation.

At each node in the lattice, the option value $V$ is computed as $V = e^{-r \Delta t} \cdot [q \cdot V_u + (1 - q) \cdot V_d]$, where $V_u$ and $V_d$ are the option values at the up and down successor nodes. This backward recursion propagates terminal payoffs back to the initial node, yielding the option's present value $V_0$. For American options, an additional check is performed at each node: $V = \max(\text{intrinsic value}, \text{continuation value})$, where the intrinsic value is the payoff from immediate exercise and the continuation value is the discounted risk-neutral expectation computed above. This modification captures the optionality of early exercise, which can be valuable for American puts or calls on dividend-paying stocks.

The binomial lattice also provides a natural framework for computing option sensitivities, or "Greeks." Delta, the sensitivity of the option value to changes in the underlying price, is approximated as $\Delta \approx \frac{V_u - V_d}{S \cdot u - S \cdot d}$ using the values at the up and down nodes immediately following the initial node. Gamma, the second derivative with respect to the underlying price, requires examining nodes two steps into the future. Theta, the sensitivity to time decay, is computed by comparing the option value at the initial node to the value at the next time step, holding the underlying price constant. These discrete approximations converge to the continuous Greeks as the number of time steps increases.

The Black-Scholes model dispenses with the lattice structure entirely by modeling the log price of the underlying asset as a continuous-time stochastic process. Under the risk-neutral measure, the stock price $S_t$ satisfies the stochastic differential equation $dS_t = r S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t$, where $W_t$ is a standard Brownian motion. This equation states that the instantaneous return on the stock consists of a deterministic drift term proportional to the risk-free rate and a stochastic diffusion term proportional to the volatility $\sigma$ and the increment of the Brownian motion $dW_t$.

Applying Itô's lemma—the stochastic calculus analog of the chain rule—to a twice-differentiable function $V(S, t)$ representing the option value yields the Black-Scholes partial differential equation:

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + r S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} = r V$$

This PDE, subject to the terminal condition $V(S, T) = \max(S - K, 0)$ for a call, admits a closed-form solution. The solution is derived by recognizing that the option value can be expressed as a discounted risk-neutral expectation of the terminal payoff, and that the terminal log price is normally distributed under the risk-neutral measure. After integrating against the normal density and simplifying, the Black-Scholes formula for a European call is:

$$C = S_0 \Phi(d_1) - K e^{-r T} \Phi(d_2)$$

where $\Phi$ is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution, $d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0 / K) + (r + \sigma^2 / 2) T}{\sigma \sqrt{T}}$, and $d_2 = d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{T}$. The formula has an elegant interpretation: $S_0 \Phi(d_1)$ is the present value of the stock received at expiration if the option finishes in the money, weighted by the risk-neutral probability (adjusted for correlation between the stock price and the indicator of finishing in the money), and $K e^{-r T} \Phi(d_2)$ is the present value of the strike paid at expiration, weighted by the risk-neutral probability $\Phi(d_2)$ of finishing in the money.

The corresponding formula for a European put, derived either by solving the PDE with the appropriate terminal condition or by invoking put-call parity $P = C - S_0 + K e^{-r T}$, is:

$$P = K e^{-r T} \Phi(-d_2) - S_0 \Phi(-d_1)$$

The Black-Scholes Greeks are obtained by differentiating the pricing formula with respect to its inputs. Delta for a call is $\Delta = \Phi(d_1)$, which ranges from 0 for deep out-of-the-money options to 1 for deep in-the-money options. Gamma is $\Gamma = \frac{\phi(d_1)}{S_0 \sigma \sqrt{T}}$, where $\phi$ is the standard normal density function; gamma is maximized for at-the-money options and decays for options far from the money. Vega, the sensitivity to volatility, is $\nu = S_0 \phi(d_1) \sqrt{T}$, again maximized at the money. Theta for a call is $\Theta = -\frac{S_0 \phi(d_1) \sigma}{2 \sqrt{T}} - r K e^{-r T} \Phi(d_2)$, reflecting time decay and the cost of financing the strike.

When the underlying asset pays continuous dividends at rate $q$, the Black-Scholes formula is modified by replacing $S_0$ with $S_0 e^{-q T}$ in all computations. This adjustment accounts for the fact that the holder of a call option does not receive dividends, so the forward price of the stock (the price for a contract to deliver the stock at expiration) is reduced by the present value of dividends. For discrete dividends, the adjustment is more involved: the stock price is decomposed into a risky component and a deterministic component equal to the present value of dividends. The Black-Scholes formula is applied to the risky component, with the strike adjusted downward by the present value of dividends expected before expiration.

The binomial model handles dividends naturally. For continuous dividends, the risk-neutral probability is recomputed as $q = \frac{e^{(r - q_{\text{div}}) \Delta t} - d}{u - d}$, where $q_{\text{div}}$ is the dividend yield. For discrete dividends, the stock price at each node following an ex-dividend date is reduced by the dividend amount, and the lattice is reconstructed from that point forward. This approach captures the impact of dividends on early exercise decisions for American options, a feature that cannot be addressed within the closed-form Black-Scholes framework.

The convergence of the binomial model to Black-Scholes as $N \to \infty$ can be demonstrated rigorously using the central limit theorem. The sum of $N$ independent up-down moves, each contributing $\ln(u)$ or $\ln(d)$ to the log price, converges in distribution to a normal random variable with mean $(r - \sigma^2 / 2) T$ and variance $\sigma^2 T$, exactly matching the distribution of $\ln(S_T)$ under geometric Brownian motion. In practice, convergence is rapid: binomial prices with 100 time steps typically match Black-Scholes prices to within a fraction of a cent for liquid equity options.

The lattice structure provides transparency that the Black-Scholes formula obscures. Each node in the binomial tree corresponds to a specific price and time, and the option value at that node reflects the risk-neutral expected payoff conditional on reaching that state. Traders can visualize the evolution of the hedge ratio (delta) through time and across price paths, build intuition about early exercise boundaries for American options, and debug pricing anomalies by inspecting individual nodes. This pedagogical clarity makes the binomial model indispensable for training junior traders and for explaining complex structures to clients, even when Black-Scholes or more sophisticated models are used for production pricing.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

The computational efficiency of option pricing models is critical for real-time trading operations. A market maker quoting two-sided markets in hundreds of strikes across multiple expirations must recompute option prices and Greeks thousands of times per second in response to underlying price movements, volatility updates, and order flow. The choice between binomial, Black-Scholes, and more sophisticated models involves explicit tradeoffs between accuracy, speed, and transparency.

For European options on non-dividend-paying stocks, the Black-Scholes closed-form solution is unbeatable: a single call to the cumulative normal distribution function (which can be approximated to machine precision in a few dozen floating-point operations using polynomial or rational approximations) yields the option price in microseconds. Modern implementations precompute and cache Greeks at initialization, updating them incrementally as market inputs change rather than recomputing from scratch. When the underlying price moves by a small amount $\delta S$, the new option price can be approximated as $V_{\text{new}} \approx V_{\text{old}} + \Delta \cdot \delta S + \frac{1}{2} \Gamma \cdot (\delta S)^2$, avoiding the transcendental function calls entirely for small moves.

The binomial model is more computationally intensive, with complexity $O(N^2)$ for a lattice with $N$ time steps. However, the quadratic scaling is benign for typical parameter ranges: a 100-step lattice requires only 5,050 option value computations, which a modern CPU can complete in hundreds of microseconds. The key optimization is to exploit the recombining structure by storing option values in a two-dimensional array indexed by time step and price level, overwriting values in place as the backward recursion proceeds. This approach minimizes memory allocation overhead and maximizes cache locality.

For American options, the binomial model is often the method of choice in production systems because it handles early exercise exactly (up to discretization error) and because the optimal exercise boundary emerges naturally from the max operation at each node. The main alternative, the Barone-Adesi-Whaley analytic approximation, is faster but less accurate and fails near ex-dividend dates. Finite difference methods, which solve the Black-Scholes PDE on a discrete grid, offer comparable accuracy to binomial trees with similar computational cost but require more sophisticated implementation and are less intuitive for traders.

A critical failure mode of the Black-Scholes model is the assumption of constant volatility. Empirically, implied volatility varies systematically across strikes—a phenomenon known as the volatility smile or skew. For equity index options, out-of-the-money puts trade at higher implied volatilities than at-the-money options, reflecting demand for downside protection and the empirical fact that equity returns exhibit negative skewness and excess kurtosis. Ignoring the smile leads to systematic mispricing: a trader who delta-hedges an option using Black-Scholes delta but faces a stochastic volatility environment will realize gains or losses from gamma scalping that differ from the model's prediction.

The practitioner's solution is to replace the single volatility parameter $\sigma$ with a volatility surface $\sigma(K, T)$ that depends on strike and expiration. Each option is priced using the implied volatility corresponding to its strike and expiration, and the Greeks are computed by numerically differentiating the pricing function with respect to spot, volatility, and time. This approach, known as "local volatility" or "sticky strike" modeling, is a pragmatic compromise: it ensures that model prices match market prices by construction, but it does not provide a dynamically consistent framework for hedging or for pricing exotic derivatives that depend on the joint evolution of spot and volatility.

More sophisticated models—stochastic volatility models like Heston, jump-diffusion models like Merton or Kou, and local-stochastic volatility models that blend both features—address these limitations by introducing additional state variables and parameters. These models are calibrated to the entire volatility surface simultaneously, ensuring internal consistency, and they generate more accurate hedge ratios for complex structures. The cost is computational: stochastic volatility models typically require Monte Carlo simulation or Fourier transform techniques, increasing pricing times from microseconds to milliseconds. For liquid vanilla options, the added complexity is rarely justified; for exotic structures like variance swaps, barrier options, or cliquet options, it is essential.

Another limitation of Black-Scholes is the assumption of continuous trading and the ability to hedge without transaction costs. In reality, discrete-time delta hedging incurs rebalancing costs proportional to the square of the volatility and the frequency of rebalancing. For a market maker hedging a large options portfolio, these costs can be substantial, especially for short-dated options with high gamma. The optimal rebalancing policy is not to delta-hedge continuously but to establish tolerance bands around the target delta and rebalance only when the realized delta drifts outside the band. The width of the band is calibrated to balance the cost of rehedging (proportional to the bid-ask spread and position size) against the cost of being unhedged (proportional to gamma and volatility).

The distinction between implied and realized volatility is fundamental to options trading. Implied volatility is the market's consensus forecast of future realized volatility, extracted from option prices using the Black-Scholes formula. Realized volatility is computed ex post from the time series of log returns over the life of the option. If implied volatility consistently exceeds realized volatility, option buyers lose money on average, and option sellers earn a "volatility risk premium." Empirically, this premium is positive for equity index options—implied volatility exceeds realized volatility on average—reflecting investor demand for portfolio insurance and the fact that investors are willing to pay a premium to hedge crash risk.

Traders exploit this wedge through volatility trading strategies: selling options (collecting high implied volatility) and delta-hedging to eliminate directional exposure, thereby isolating exposure to the difference between implied and realized volatility. The profitability of this strategy depends critically on the accuracy of the delta hedge. If the trader hedges using Black-Scholes delta but the true data-generating process exhibits jumps or stochastic volatility, the hedge will be imperfect, and the trader will face residual risk. This is why sophisticated market makers use model-free hedging techniques, such as hedging an option with a portfolio of other options, rather than relying solely on delta-hedging with the underlying.

The Black-Scholes model also assumes that the underlying asset follows a continuous price process with no jumps. In reality, earnings announcements, macroeconomic releases, and geopolitical shocks produce discontinuous price moves that violate this assumption. The presence of jump risk has two implications. First, implied volatility in the days leading up to a scheduled announcement spikes, reflecting the market's expectation of a discrete move. Second, the Black-Scholes delta is an imperfect hedge in the presence of jumps, because the hedge ratio is computed assuming continuous diffusion. Traders address this by adjusting position sizes around known events, using options spreads that limit exposure to discrete moves, or employing jump-diffusion models that explicitly incorporate the possibility of discontinuous price changes.

Numerical stability is another practical concern. The Black-Scholes formula involves computing the cumulative normal distribution of quantities that can be extremely large or small, especially for deep in-the-money or out-of-the-money options. Naive implementations can suffer from floating-point underflow or overflow, producing nonsensical results. Robust implementations use asymptotic expansions or rational approximations that remain accurate across the full range of possible input values. For the binomial model, numerical issues arise when the number of time steps is very large or when the volatility is very high, causing $u$ and $d$ to diverge. In such cases, the lattice may require extended precision arithmetic or alternative parameterizations to maintain accuracy.

Dividend adjustments introduce additional complexity. For stocks paying quarterly dividends, the ex-dividend dates fall at irregular intervals, and the dividend amounts may be uncertain. The market convention is to model future dividends as a combination of a known discrete component (based on announced or historical dividends) and a continuous yield component (reflecting the long-run average payout ratio). The discrete dividends are subtracted from the stock price in the binomial lattice or from the strike in the Black-Scholes formula, while the continuous yield is incorporated into the drift term. Errors in estimating future dividends can produce significant mispricings, especially for long-dated options or stocks with high dividend yields.

The Black-Scholes model's assumption of a log-normal price distribution implies that prices can never go negative, which is appropriate for equities but inappropriate for futures, interest rates, or commodities that can trade at or below zero. For such underlyings, alternative models—normal models, shifted log-normal models, or SABR (Stochastic Alpha Beta Rho) models—are used. The normal model replaces geometric Brownian motion with arithmetic Brownian motion, so that $dS_t = \mu dt + \sigma dW_t$. This produces a Bachelier pricing formula in which option prices are linear in volatility rather than multiplicative, and which allows for negative prices. The shifted log-normal model applies the Black-Scholes formula to $S_t + \lambda$ rather than $S_t$, where $\lambda$ is a shift parameter calibrated to the market. These models are widely used in interest rate derivatives markets, where negative rates have been a reality since the European Central Bank and Bank of Japan adopted negative policy rates in the mid-2010s.

Calibration of the volatility surface is an art as much as a science. Given market prices for a set of options with different strikes and expirations, the goal is to find a smooth, arbitrage-free volatility surface that reprices these options exactly (or as closely as possible). Arbitrage-free constraints include no calendar spread arbitrage (longer-dated options must be more expensive than shorter-dated options with the same strike) and no butterfly arbitrage (the second derivative of the option price with respect to strike must be non-negative, ensuring that the implied probability density is non-negative). Practitioners use spline interpolation, kernel smoothing, or parametric models to construct surfaces that satisfy these constraints while remaining smooth and stable under small changes in input prices.

The interplay between model choice, calibration, and hedging is the essence of quantitative trading. A perfect model is impossible—all models are approximations—but a robust pricing and risk management framework must be internally consistent, computationally efficient, and transparent to traders. The binomial model provides pedagogical clarity and flexibility for path-dependent features. The Black-Scholes model provides closed-form solutions and a common language for volatility. More sophisticated models—stochastic volatility, local volatility, jump-diffusion—provide greater realism at the cost of complexity. Mastery of this hierarchy, and the judgment to select the right tool for each problem, is what separates a competent analyst from a seasoned derivatives trader.
