# Beginner Technical Lesson: Jazz Harmony

## 1. Core Mechanics & Basic Principles (How it works under the hood at a fundamental level)

Jazz harmony is a rich and expressive language that builds upon the foundational principles of Western tonal music. At its core, jazz harmony is characterized by the use of extended chords, modal interchange, and the exploration of harmonic tension and resolution. These elements are rooted in the interplay between the diatonic scale and its various modes, as well as the introduction of altered tones and non-diatonic chords.

### Diatonic Scales and Modes
The diatonic scale is the foundation of Western music, consisting of seven notes that form a natural sequence. In jazz, the modes of the diatonic scale are particularly important. These modes are:

- **Ionian (Major Scale)**
- **Dorian**
- **Phrygian**
- **Lydian**
- **Mixolydian**
- **Aeolian (Natural Minor Scale)**
- **Locrian**

Each mode provides a unique color and flavor, and jazz musicians often use them to create interesting harmonic progressions. For example, the Dorian mode is commonly used over minor chords, while the Lydian mode is often employed over dominant chords to introduce a raised fourth scale degree, creating a sense of tension.

### Extended Chords
Extended chords are chords that include notes beyond the basic triad (root, third, and fifth). In jazz, these chords are typically built using the seventh, ninth, eleventh, and thirteenth scale degrees. Extended chords can be major, minor, or dominant, and they can be altered with sharps or flats on various scale degrees to create even more complex and colorful harmonies.

### Harmonic Tension and Resolution
Harmonic tension and resolution is a fundamental concept in jazz harmony. Tension arises from dissonant intervals and altered chords, while resolution occurs when these dissonances are resolved to consonant intervals and stable chords. This tension-resolution cycle is a key element of jazz's rhythmic and harmonic drive.

## 2. Step-by-Step Component Breakdown

### Step 1: Understanding the Major Scale and Its Modes
To begin, it's essential to understand the major scale and its modes. Let's take the C major scale as an example:

```
C D E F G A B C
```

From this scale, we can derive the following modes:

- **C Ionian (C Major):** C D E F G A B C
- **D Dorian:** D E F G A B C D
- **E Phrygian:** E F G A B C D E
- **F Lydian:** F G A B C D E F
- **G Mixolydian:** G A B C D E F G
- **A Aeolian (A Minor):** A B C D E F G A
- **B Locrian:** B C D E F G A B

### Step 2: Building Extended Chords
Extended chords are built by stacking thirds on top of a root note. Let's build a C7 chord and then extend it to a C7/9 chord:

- **C7 (C Major 7th):** C E G Bb
- **C7/9 (C Major 7th/9th):** C E G Bb D

### Step 3: Using Altered Chords
Altered chords are chords that include altered tones, such as sharps or flats on various scale degrees. For example, a C7#9 chord is a C7 chord with a raised ninth scale degree:

- **C7#9 (C Major 7th/#9th):** C E G Bb D#

### Step 4: Creating Harmonic Progressions
A simple harmonic progression in jazz might look like this:

```
C7 | F7 | G7 | C7
```

This progression uses dominant seventh chords, which are common in jazz. To add more color and tension, we can use extended chords and altered chords:

```
C7/9 | F7#9 | G7/9 | Cmaj7
```

In this progression, the C7/9 chord introduces a ninth scale degree, the F7#9 chord adds a raised ninth scale degree, and the G7/9 chord also includes a ninth scale degree. The Cmaj7 chord at the end resolves the progression to a more consonant and stable chord.

## 4. What Can Go Wrong (Simple common mistakes and how to spot them)

### Common Mistakes

1. **Incorrect Chord Construction:**
   - Ensure that you are using the correct notes for each chord. For example, a C7/9 chord should include C, E, G, Bb, and D.

2. **Improper Progression Flow:**
   - Make sure that the chords flow logically and create a sense of tension and resolution. For example, a C7/9 chord should typically resolve to a more consonant chord like Cmaj7.

3. **Lack of Bass Line:**
   - A strong bass line is essential in jazz harmony. Ensure that your bass line complements the chords and provides a solid foundation.

4. **Inconsistent Tempo:**
   - Jazz typically has a steady tempo, so ensure that your tempo is consistent throughout the progression.

5. **Overuse of Effects:**
   - While effects can enhance the sound, overuse can make the music sound muddy. Use effects sparingly and appropriately.

### How to Spot Mistakes

- **Listen Carefully:** Play the progression and listen for any dissonances or unresolved tensions.
- **Check the Chords:** Verify that each chord is constructed correctly and that the notes are in the correct order.
- **Review the Bass Line:** Ensure that the bass line complements the chords and provides a solid foundation.
- **Check the Tempo:** Make sure that the tempo is consistent throughout the progression.
- **Evaluate the Effects:** Listen to the progression with and without effects to ensure that they enhance the sound without overwhelming it.
