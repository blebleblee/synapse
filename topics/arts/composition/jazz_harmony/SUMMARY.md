# Executive Summary: Core

## 1. Core Invariants & Mental Model

In the realm of jazz harmony, the architectural mental model is akin to a sophisticated cityscape, where each building represents a harmonic structure, and the streets are the pathways through which these structures interact. This cityscape is not static but dynamic, with constant movement and transformation, reflecting the improvisational nature of jazz. The foundational elements of this architecture include scales, chords, and progressions, which are the building blocks of harmonic structures. However, the true essence of jazz harmony lies in the way these elements are combined and transformed, creating a rich tapestry of sound.

At the heart of this dynamic system is the concept of **tonal center**, which asserts that a piece of music revolves around a central pitch or key. This principle is formalized through the concept of **tonal gravity**, where pitches are assigned a gravitational pull towards the tonic, governed by the harmonic series and the overtone spectrum. The harmonic series, a fundamental invariant in music theory, represents the natural overtones produced by a vibrating string. Defined as:

\[ f_n = f_0 \cdot n \]

where \( f_0 \) is the fundamental frequency and \( f_n \) is the \( n \)-th harmonic, this series provides a natural framework for understanding consonance and dissonance. In equal temperament, the frequency ratio between consecutive semitones is given by:

\[ f_n = f_0 \cdot 2^{n/12} \]

This ratio is a kernel invariant in Western music, providing a consistent tuning system that facilitates harmonic consonance and dissonance. In jazz, the exploration of altered chords and extensions can be seen as a manipulation of these kernel invariants, creating new harmonic landscapes while maintaining a sense of tonal coherence.

## 2. Key Architecture & Mechanisms

The architecture of jazz harmony is built upon a foundation of scales, chords, and progressions, each serving a distinct yet interconnected role. Scales and modes provide the primary material from which jazz harmonies are constructed. The most common scales in jazz are the Major, Dorian, Mixolydian, Aeolian, Phrygian, and Locrian modes, each derived from the Major scale. These modes offer a framework for improvisation and composition, providing a palette of tonal colors. For instance, the Dorian mode (1, 2, ♭3, 4, 5, 6, ♭7) is often used over minor chords, while the Mixolydian mode (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, ♭7) is frequently employed over dominant chords. Understanding the unique characteristics of each mode is crucial for creating compelling jazz harmonies.

Chords are the fundamental units of harmonic structure in jazz. The most common chords in jazz are the Major 7th, Minor 7th, and Dominant 7th chords. These chords serve as the building blocks of chord progressions, which are sequences of chords played one after another. In jazz, common chord progressions include the ii-V-I progression (Dm7-G7-Cmaj7) and the I-vi-ii-V progression (Cmaj7-Am7-Dm7-G7). Chord voicings, the specific arrangement of notes within a chord, are also crucial in jazz harmony. Voicings can be open, closed, or spread, and they play a significant role in the texture and color of the music.

The interaction between scales, chords, and progressions is governed by a set of protocols and formulas that define the rules of jazz harmony. One such protocol is the principle of **voice leading**, which involves the smooth and efficient movement of voices from one chord to the next. Voice leading is essential for creating a sense of continuity and coherence in jazz harmony. Another protocol is the principle of **modal interchange**, which allows the use of chords from one mode in the context of another. This technique adds complexity and interest to jazz harmonies, allowing musicians to explore new harmonic territories while maintaining a sense of tonal center.

## 3. Practical Applications & Production Relevance

The principles of jazz harmony have wide-ranging applications in both improvisation and composition. In improvisation, jazz musicians use scales and modes to create melodic lines that fit over chord progressions. The ability to navigate different modes and chord voicings is crucial for successful improvisation. For example, a saxophonist might use the Dorian mode over a Dm7 chord, the Mixolydian mode over a G7 chord, and the Major mode over a Cmaj7 chord in a ii-V-I progression. This modal approach allows for a rich and varied improvisational vocabulary.

In composition, jazz harmony provides a framework for creating original music. Composers can use chord progressions and voicings to create harmonic structures that support their melodies and rhythms. The use of altered chords and extensions can add complexity and interest to the music, while the principle of voice leading can help create a sense of continuity and coherence. For example, a composer might use a ii-V-I progression with altered chords (Dm7b5-G7#9-Cmaj7) to create a more sophisticated harmonic landscape.

The principles of jazz harmony also have practical applications in the production of jazz recordings. In recording sessions, producers and engineers can use their understanding of jazz harmony to make informed decisions about chord voicings, arrangements, and performances. For example, a producer might choose specific chord voicings to create a particular texture or color in a recording, or they might use their knowledge of voice leading to create a sense of continuity and coherence in the music.
