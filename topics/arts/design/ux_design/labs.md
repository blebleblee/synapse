# Operational Verification Labs: UX Design

## Objective & DAW / Studio Setup

This lab suite translates user experience design principles into auditory, compositional, and spatial verification exercises. UX design in the audio domain encompasses interaction feedback synthesis, spatial audio wayfinding, adaptive soundtrack composition, and accessibility compliance through spectral clarity and dynamic range management. You will construct interactive audio prototypes, verify cognitive load through harmonic complexity metrics, and validate accessibility standards using loudness normalization and frequency masking analysis.

Configure your production environment with a primary Digital Audio Workstation supporting MIDI routing, plugin automation, and multichannel output. Ableton Live, Logic Pro, or Reaper all provide the necessary toolchain. Install VCV Rack for modular synthesis verification, MuseScore or Dorico for notation-driven UX flow composition, Voxengo SPAN for real-time spectrum analysis, and a LUFS-compliant loudness meter such as Youlean or Waves WLM. Ensure your audio interface operates at 48 kHz sample rate with 24-bit depth and configure your DAW buffer size to 256 samples for real-time monitoring with acceptable latency below 12 milliseconds round-trip.

Verify your MIDI monitor displays note-on velocity, continuous controller messages, and pitch bend data in real time. Calibrate your studio monitors or headphones to 85 dB SPL C-weighted at the listening position using pink noise and an SPL meter. This reference level ensures consistent loudness perception across all verification exercises. Disable all system audio enhancements, equalizers, and spatial processing to maintain an uncolored monitoring chain. Create a dedicated project template with eight audio tracks, four MIDI tracks, a master bus with SPAN and a loudness meter, and a return track configured for convolution reverb. Save this template as "UX_Verification_Lab_Template" for rapid session initialization.

## Exercise 1: Practical Composition & Voice-Leading Realization

User experience design in interactive audio relies on harmonic stability to signal system states and voice-leading smoothness to reduce cognitive friction during state transitions. This exercise constructs a navigational audio prototype where each UI state receives a distinct harmonic color, and transitions between states follow strict voice-leading rules to maintain user orientation. You will compose a four-state system using rootless jazz voicings, verify smooth voice motion through MIDI velocity consistency, and validate perceptual continuity using spectral coherence analysis.

Open MuseScore or Dorico and create a new score for piano with four systems, each representing one UI state: Idle, Loading, Active, and Error. Assign Idle state to a Cmaj9 voicing in second inversion with root omitted, voiced from bottom to top as G-B-D-E across octaves 3 and 4. Loading state receives Dm11 voiced E-G-A-C to emphasize suspended anticipation. Active state anchors on Gmaj7 with stable voicing F#-A-B-D. Error state employs G7♭9 with chromatic tension voiced F-A♭-B-D to signal disruption without jarring dissonance. Notate each chord as whole notes at quarter note equals 90 BPM.

Now enforce parsimonious voice leading between states. Transition from Idle Cmaj9 to Loading Dm11 by moving G down by whole step to F-sharp, holding B and D as common tones, and stepping E down to D. Analyze the voice motion: three voices move by step or hold, one voice moves by whole tone, total displacement is three semitones. Export the MIDI file and import into your DAW. Load a clean electric piano plugin with moderate sustain and assign it to MIDI track one. Play back the sequence and observe in SPAN whether the fundamental spectral centroid remains stable between 200 and 400 Hz, indicating consistent tonal weight. If the centroid shifts abruptly by more than 100 Hz, revise the voicing to maintain spectral continuity.

Program the Loading-to-Active transition: Dm11 E-G-A-C moves to Gmaj7 F#-A-B-D. E descends by semitone to D, G ascends by whole tone to A, A holds as common tone, C descends by whole tone to B. Total displacement is four semitones with two common tones, maintaining smooth motion. The Active-to-Error transition introduces controlled chromaticism: Gmaj7 F#-A-B-D moves to G7♭9 F-A♭-B-D. F-sharp descends by semitone to F, A descends by semitone to A-flat, B and D hold as common tones. This introduces a single chromatic alteration while preserving two anchor pitches, signaling error state without perceptual discontinuity.

Record the full four-state cycle into your DAW using a MIDI keyboard with consistent velocity around 80 for all notes. Quantize to 16th-note grid with 90% strength to preserve slight humanization. Duplicate the MIDI region four times and stagger playback by one beat to create overlapping state transitions, simulating rapid UI navigation. Monitor the master bus loudness meter and verify integrated LUFS remains within −23 to −16 LUFS range, appropriate for interactive audio without dynamic compression fatigue. If peaks exceed −10 dBFS, reduce the electric piano output gain by 3 dB and re-verify.

Open SPAN in your master bus and engage the sonogram display. Observe the harmonic content during each state transition. Idle and Loading states should show strong fundamental energy between 150 and 300 Hz with sparse overtone content. Active state should introduce brighter harmonic energy between 1 and 3 kHz due to the major seventh interval. Error state should display increased energy in the 400 to 800 Hz midrange from the flatted ninth, creating perceptual tension without harshness. If Error state energy exceeds Active state by more than 6 dB in the critical 2 to 4 kHz presence band, reduce the voicing register by one octave to avoid cognitive alarm response.

Export the completed four-state composition as a 48 kHz 24-bit stereo WAV file named "UX_State_Transitions_Voiceleading.wav" and archive with the MuseScore source file. Document the total voice-leading displacement for each transition and verify that no single voice moves by more than a major third except during deliberate error signaling. This constraint ensures smooth auditory wayfinding without disorienting the user during rapid state changes in production applications.

## Exercise 2: Timbre Synthesis, Waveform Shaping & Modulation Routing

User interface feedback tones require distinct timbral identities to communicate affordance and system response without verbal instruction. This exercise synthesizes four interaction feedback tones—button press, slider movement, modal open, and destructive action—using subtractive synthesis in VCV Rack. You will construct each timbre from first principles using oscillator harmonic content, filter topology selection, envelope shaping, and modulation routing, then verify perceptual distinction through spectral separation and transient clarity.

Launch VCV Rack and initialize a blank patch. Begin with button press feedback: instantiate a VCO-1 oscillator set to sawtooth wave at 880 Hz to provide a bright, attention-commanding fundamental. Route the oscillator output to a VCF transistor ladder filter configured to 24 dB per octave low-pass topology with cutoff frequency at 2.4 kHz and resonance at 30%. This filter setting preserves the fundamental and first three harmonics while attenuating higher-order partials that contribute to harshness. Instantiate an ADSR envelope generator with attack 1 ms, decay 40 ms, sustain 0%, and release 10 ms. Patch the envelope output to the VCF cutoff frequency modulation input with attenuation at 60%, creating a rapid filter sweep from 2.4 kHz down to 800 Hz synchronized with amplitude decay. This sweep provides perceptual confirmation of button actuation.

Route the filter output to a VCA with the same ADSR envelope controlling amplitude. Set the VCA output to your audio interface output 1. Trigger the envelope using a manual gate button and record the output into your DAW. Analyze the waveform and verify attack time measures below 5 ms from zero crossing to peak amplitude, ensuring instantaneous perceptual response. Measure total duration from gate-on to −60 dB decay and confirm it falls within 50 to 70 ms, preventing overlapping feedback tones during rapid button presses.

Construct slider movement feedback using continuous timbral modulation rather than discrete triggering. Instantiate a second VCO-1 set to triangle wave at 440 Hz for a softer, less aggressive timbre. Route to a 12 dB state-variable filter in band-pass mode with center frequency at 1 kHz and Q factor at 2.5, creating a narrow resonant peak suitable for continuous scrubbing feedback. Instead of an ADSR envelope, patch a slew limiter to smooth incoming CV from a manual slider or MIDI continuous controller. Set slew rate to 20 ms per volt, creating gradual pitch and filter sweeps that track slider position without zipper noise. Route the slew limiter output to both VCO frequency modulation input with 1 volt per octave scaling and VCF cutoff with 50% attenuation. Move the slider from minimum to maximum and record the output. Verify in SPAN that the center frequency sweeps smoothly from 400 Hz to 2 kHz without abrupt spectral discontinuities, confirming anti-aliased user input tracking.

Design modal open feedback to signal non-destructive expansion of interface real estate. Instantiate a third VCO set to sine wave at 220 Hz for a warm, inviting fundamental. Route through a low-pass gate module combining VCF and VCA in a single Buchla-inspired topology. LPG modules produce smooth, organic timbral evolution without sharp attack transients. Set the LPG response curve to linear and drive it with an AD envelope: attack 80 ms, decay 300 ms. The slow attack creates a gentle bloom rather than a percussive hit, signaling invitation rather than interruption. Add a second VCO at 330 Hz tuned to a perfect fifth above the fundamental, mixed at −6 dB relative level, creating a stable consonant dyad that conveys harmonic resolution. Record the modal open tone and measure attack time exceeding 50 ms and total duration exceeding 350 ms, distinguishing it from the rapid button press feedback through temporal expansion.

Construct destructive action feedback requiring explicit user confirmation. This timbre must signal caution without inducing panic. Instantiate a fourth VCO set to pulse wave with 25% pulse width at 110 Hz, producing a hollow, unsettling timbre. Route to a high-pass filter at 200 Hz with 12 dB per octave slope to remove excessive low-frequency weight, then through a wavefolder or distortion module with moderate drive at 40% to introduce odd-order harmonics. Apply an ASR envelope with attack 10 ms, sustain at 50% level, and release 150 ms triggered by a sustained gate. This envelope sustains the tone as long as the user holds the destructive action button, providing continuous auditory warning. Add a slow LFO at 4 Hz modulating pulse width between 20% and 40% to create subtle timbral variation that sustains attention without irritation. Record the destructive action tone with a two-second gate duration and verify in SPAN that energy concentrates between 400 Hz and 1.2 kHz with increased odd harmonic content visible as regularly spaced spectral peaks, distinguishing it from the smoother button press and modal open tones.

Export all four synthesized feedback tones as individual 48 kHz 24-bit mono WAV files named according to their function. Load them into your DAW on separate tracks and align them sequentially with 500 ms spacing. Insert SPAN on the master bus and solo each tone in turn. Document the spectral centroid, peak frequency, and 3 dB bandwidth for each tone. Verify that no two tones share the same dominant frequency band within 200 Hz, ensuring perceptual separation in polyphonic playback scenarios. If tones overlap spectrally, adjust oscillator pitch or filter cutoff to achieve distinct frequency identities. This spectral orthogonality ensures users can distinguish concurrent feedback events during complex interactions.

## Exercise 3: Dynamic Mixing, Spectrum Analysis & Audio Stress Testing

Production UX audio must maintain intelligibility and emotional consistency across playback systems ranging from laptop speakers to studio monitors, in environments from silent studios to noisy transportation. This exercise subjects your composed feedback tones and state transition music to systematic stress testing using spectral masking analysis, loudness normalization, and codec degradation simulation. You will verify accessibility compliance through frequency response analysis, validate dynamic range consistency, and confirm graceful degradation under bandwidth-limited playback conditions.

Import all feedback tones from Exercise 2 and the state transition composition from Exercise 1 into a new DAW session. Arrange them sequentially on a timeline with realistic timing: button press at 0:00, slider movement from 0:01 to 0:03, modal open at 0:04, destructive action at 0:06 to 0:08, followed by the four-state transition cycle from 0:10 to 0:20. This ten-second sequence represents a condensed user interaction flow. Insert a loudness meter on the master bus and perform an integrated loudness measurement. If integrated LUFS falls outside the −23 to −16 LUFS target range for interactive audio, apply makeup gain or attenuation on the master bus to normalize. Verify that true peak level does not exceed −1 dBTP to prevent clipping during downstream codec conversion.

Instantiate a spectrum analyzer on the master bus and engage one-third octave band display mode. Play back the full sequence and observe frequency distribution. Verify that energy remains balanced across octave bands from 200 Hz to 4 kHz, with no single band exceeding adjacent bands by more than 6 dB except during deliberate error signaling. If excessive energy accumulates in the 2 to 4 kHz presence band, indicating potential listening fatigue, insert a parametric EQ before the loudness meter and apply a gentle −2 dB reduction using a 1 octave wide bell filter centered at 3 kHz with Q factor 1.5. Re-measure integrated loudness and adjust makeup gain to restore target level.

Simulate small speaker playback by inserting a high-pass filter at 150 Hz with 12 dB per octave slope on the master bus before all other processing. This filter emulates the low-frequency rolloff of laptop speakers and smartphone drivers. Play back the sequence and verify that all tones remain perceptually distinct and that the state transition composition retains harmonic function. If the Loading state Dm11 voicing loses weight and becomes indistinct from Idle state Cmaj9, transpose the composition up by a major second to center the harmonic content in the 300 to 600 Hz range where small speakers exhibit flat response. Re-export and re-verify spectral balance.

Test dynamic range consistency by duplicating the master bus output to a parallel processing chain. Insert a compressor with ratio 4:1, threshold −18 dBFS, attack 10 ms, release 100 ms, and makeup gain to match bypassed level. This aggressive compression simulates mobile device automatic gain control and broadcast limiting. A/B between bypassed and compressed signals. The compressed version should maintain intelligibility and relative loudness relationships between feedback tones. If button press and destructive action tones become perceptually identical in level under compression, increase the uncompressed button press level by 3 dB and reduce destructive action level by 2 dB to restore dynamic separation after compression.

Perform codec degradation testing by exporting the final mix as 128 kbps AAC and 96 kbps Opus files using FFmpeg or your DAW's export function. Reimport both lossy files into the DAW and null-test against the original 48 kHz WAV by inverting polarity on one track and summing. The residual represents codec artifacts. Play back the residual and verify it contains primarily high-frequency quantization noise above 10 kHz with RMS level at least −40 dB below the original signal. If residual contains audible tonal artifacts or distortion in the 2 to 6 kHz range, increase the export bitrate to 192 kbps AAC or revise synthesis parameters to reduce harmonic complexity in critical bands.

Validate accessibility compliance by measuring frequency response of all individual feedback tones using pink noise as reference. Generate a 10-second pink noise burst at −20 dBFS and route through the same processing chain applied to your feedback tones. Measure one-third octave band levels from 100 Hz to 10 kHz using SPAN. Calculate deviation from ideal pink noise response, which should measure flat within ±3 dB per octave. Repeat measurement with each feedback tone soloed. If any tone exhibits spectral tilt exceeding ±6 dB relative to pink noise between 500 Hz and 4 kHz, apply corrective EQ to flatten response within the critical speech intelligibility band.

Document all measurements in a verification matrix: for each audio asset, record integrated LUFS, true peak level, spectral centroid, 3 dB bandwidth, dynamic range from peak to RMS, and codec residual RMS. Verify that all feedback tones measure within −23 to −16 LUFS integrated, peak below −1 dBTP, spectral centroid between 400 Hz and 2 kHz, dynamic range exceeding 12 dB, and codec residual below −35 dB RMS. Export the completed verification matrix as a CSV file and archive with all source audio, synthesis patches, and notation files.

## Studio Diagnostic Cheat Sheet & Verification Checklist

Maintain a rapid diagnostic reference for real-time troubleshooting during composition, synthesis, and mixing verification. When feedback tones lack perceptual distinction, measure spectral centroid separation: distinct tones require minimum 200 Hz centroid spacing in the 400 Hz to 2 kHz range. If state transition voice leading sounds abrupt, analyze individual voice displacement: smooth motion requires maximum major third interval for 80% of voices between adjacent chords. When loudness varies across playback systems, verify integrated LUFS within −23 to −16 range and true peak below −1 dBTP using a calibrated loudness meter.

For synthesis timbre design, verify oscillator tuning accuracy using a tuner plugin: deviations exceeding 5 cents introduce perceptible beating and unstable pitch perception. Confirm filter cutoff frequency settings by measuring 3 dB down point in SPAN: specified cutoff should match measured within 10%. Validate envelope timings using oscilloscope zero-crossing measurements: attack time below 5 ms ensures imperceptible onset latency, while release time exceeding 50 ms prevents clicks and pops from premature truncation.

Spectral masking requires one-third octave analysis: adjacent critical bands should remain within 6 dB relative level to prevent masking. If low-frequency content masks midrange clarity, apply high-pass filtering at 100 to 150 Hz with 12 dB per octave slope. Codec degradation introduces harmonic distortion measurable through null-testing: acceptable residual remains below −35 dB RMS. If residual exceeds −30 dB, increase export bitrate or reduce pre-emphasis in the 6 to 10 kHz band where perceptual coding allocates fewer bits.

Dynamic range consistency requires compressor gain reduction monitoring: if gain reduction exceeds 6 dB on transient peaks, reduce threshold or increase ratio to prevent pumping artifacts. Small speaker simulation demands high-pass filtering at 150 Hz minimum: if harmonic function degrades, transpose composition to center energy in the 300 to 800 Hz range. MIDI velocity consistency ensures perceptual loudness uniformity: verify all note-on velocities remain within 10 velocity units for sustained tones, allowing 20-unit variation only for deliberate accentuation.

Real-time CPU load monitoring prevents dropouts during synthesis and processing: if load exceeds 70%, freeze tracks to audio or increase buffer size to 512 samples. Latency accumulates through plugin chains: verify round-trip latency remains below 15 ms for acceptable real-time monitoring during MIDI input. Phase coherence between stereo channels requires correlation metering: readings below 0.7 indicate phase issues requiring mono fold-down testing.

Archive all verification data in structured logs: timestamp, asset name, integrated LUFS, true peak dBTP, spectral centroid Hz, dynamic range dB, codec residual dB, and pass/fail status. Maintain golden reference exports at 48 kHz 24-bit WAV for all approved audio assets. Re-verify at 90-day intervals or after major toolchain updates to catch drift in synthesis parameters or loudness targets.
