# Studio Practice Labs: Interior Design Principles and Spatial Layouts

## Objective & Studio Setup

Interior design is a discipline of spatial orchestration grounded in human behavior, material constraint, and compositional rigor. These labs train your hand, eye, and judgment to read spaces, design with intention, and critique your own work against measurable principles. You will learn to see how proportion, material, circulation, and human scale combine to create coherent environments that serve both function and perception.

The practice is rooted in three parallel competencies: (1) the ability to analyze existing spatial compositions and understand why they work or fail; (2) the technical facility to communicate spatial ideas through drawing, model, and digital representation; (3) the critical capacity to evaluate your own proposals against human factors, tectonic logic, and compositional unity.

### Studio Setup & Required Materials

**Physical Studio:**
- A5 or larger sketchbook (140–300 gsm paper, acid-free)
- Graphite pencils: HB, 2B, 4B, 6B (sharpened to a point, not blunt)
- Eraser (kneaded and hard plastic)
- 30 cm straightedge and 60°/45° triangle set
- Charcoal pencils (Staedtler Mars or equivalent) for value study
- 1:100 and 1:50 architect's scale ruler
- Printed reference photos of five interior spaces (mix of domestic, commercial, institutional)
- Tape and drafting paper for plan overlays
- Mirror for checking composition symmetry and reversal errors

**Digital Setup (Software):**
- **Vector/2D Design**: Figma or Adobe Illustrator for floor plans, elevations, detail drawings
- **3D Spatial Visualization**: Blender (free, industry-standard for interior visualization) or Sketchup
- **Raster Reference**: GIMP or Photoshop for annotating reference images
- **Presentation**: DaVinci Resolve for mood-board sequencing and final presentation rendering

**Reference Materials:**
- Color Swatches: Pantone Fashion + Home Color Guide, or free RAL Classic 840 color system
- Material Libraries: Actual fabric/wood/tile samples if possible; digital alternatives from manufacturer spec sheets
- Dimension Library: 2 × 4 index cards printed with standard interior dimensions (door width: 900–1000 mm, counter height: 850–900 mm, ceiling height: 2700–3000 mm, seating depth: 600–800 mm)
- Critique Rubric (provided at end of this guide)

## Exercise 1: Observation & Fundamentals Drill – Reading Spatial Composition

### Part A: Spatial Field Analysis (30 minutes; traditional media + digital)

Interior spaces communicate through layers: the envelope (walls, ceiling, floor), the rhythm of divisions and openings, the material surface qualities, the light and shadow, and the human scale embedded in every dimension. Before designing, you must train yourself to *read* these layers systematically in existing spaces.

**Step 1: Select a Reference Interior**

Choose one photograph from your reference set—ideally a residential living room, kitchen, or commercial entry. Print it at no smaller than A4 (210 × 297 mm). If using a digital reference image, import it into Figma at 100% display size for annotation.

**Step 2: Identify and Overlay the Spatial Skeleton**

On a sheet of tracing paper placed over your printed image, draw the **primary boundaries** using a sharp HB pencil and straightedge:

- **Enclosing planes**: Draw the floor line, wall planes, and ceiling plane with light construction lines (not heavy strokes). Use dashed lines for edges that recede or are implied (not fully visible).
- **Figure-ground reversal**: Identify the "positive" volume occupied by the room and the "negative" volumes of alcoves, nooks, or recessed areas. Outline both in different line weights—positive space in continuous line, negative in dashed line.
- **Apertures and thresholds**: Mark door openings, windows, and transitions between zones with tick marks at each edge.

Do not render detail or surfaces yet—you are extracting the structural armature. The resulting drawing should resemble a simple architectural plan and section overlaid in one view.

**Step 3: Measure Proportional Relationships**

Using your straightedge and pencil, establish the **fundamental ratio** of the room:

- Measure (in pencil marks on your tracing paper) the distance from the camera viewpoint to the far wall, the width of the visible wall plane, and the height of the ceiling line in the photograph.
- Express these as a ratio (e.g., depth : width : height = 3 : 2 : 1). Write this ratio in the margin of your drawing.
- Compare this ratio to canonical spatial proportions:
  - **Golden Rectangle** (1 : 1.618): Often perceived as balanced and visually restful.
  - **Cube** (1 : 1 : 1): Rational, static, rarely used as an overall room proportion but common in alcoves.
  - **Double Square** (1 : 2 : variable height): Creates dynamic flow, common in corridors and galleries.
  - **Perfect Fourth** (3 : 4): Used in classical architecture and many successful domestic interiors.

Write your observation: Does the space match a known proportion? If not, what ratio emerges?

**Step 4: Identify Layered Material Transitions**

Revert to your annotation. Using colored pencils (or digital annotations in Figma), color-code material boundaries:

- **Red**: Flooring materials and transitions (tile to wood, change in direction, threshold).
- **Blue**: Wall surfaces and their divisions (paneling, texture changes, color shifts).
- **Yellow**: Ceiling elements (coffers, dropped soffits, exposed structure).
- **Green**: Textile or soft finishes (curtains, rugs, upholstery).

For each color zone, note its approximate material (e.g., "light oak strip floor," "matte white drywall," "fabric upholstered panel"). Do not guess—use context clues from the photograph (surface sheen, joint patterns, color gradients from light modeling).

**Deliverable:** A single sheet showing the traced skeleton plan with proportional ratios marked, material zones color-coded, and annotations describing the inferred material palette and construction logic.

### Part B: Human Scale Anchoring Study (20 minutes; drawing and measurement)

Interior spaces become legible only through the **human figure**. A room 3 × 3 meters feels spacious or claustrophobic depending on how its proportions relate to standing height, arm reach, and gaze direction. This study trains your eye to sense the implicit human scale in every dimension.

**Step 1: Identify and Mark Human Figures**

Return to your reference photograph. Locate any visible people, furniture, or objects of known dimension (a standard chair is approximately 400–450 mm high; a door is 2000 mm).

Using an overlay of tracing paper or digital annotation, mark:
- The **eye line** of any visible figure (approximately 1550 mm from the floor for an adult standing).
- The **arm reach** envelope from key positions (typically 2000 mm forward and 900 mm to the side from a standing position).
- The **seated eye line** (approximately 1100 mm).

If no people are visible, use furniture as a scale reference:
- A standard sofa seat height is 400 mm; armrest height is 650 mm.
- A dining chair seat height is 450 mm.
- A kitchen counter is 850–900 mm high.

**Step 2: Establish the Visual Hierarchy**

Draw sight lines from a presumed viewer position (typically standing in the entry or sitting on the main seating) toward the dominant focal point in the space:

- Draw a **sight line** from eye level toward the most visually prominent element (a fireplace, window, art, architectural feature).
- Measure (in pencil mark units on your overlay) the angle of this sight line relative to horizontal. Mark it as a degree measurement or notation (e.g., "45° upward gaze," "10° downward to work surface").
- Repeat for secondary focal points (secondary seating, entry to next zone, etc.).

This exercise trains you to understand **visual momentum**—the way a space directs attention through proportion, alignment, and contrast.

**Step 3: Measure Ergonomic Clearances**

Using your architect's scale ruler and the known dimensions of furniture or figures, back-calculate room dimensions and clearances:

- If a sofa is 2000 mm wide and occupies one-third of a wall, the wall is approximately 6000 mm wide.
- If a person standing in the room has their head at a certain proportion of the ceiling, and the person is 1700 mm tall, calculate the ceiling height.
- Identify **circulation zones**—the pathways between furniture where people move. These should be minimum 750 mm wide for single-file passage, 1200 mm for comfortable two-person passage.

**Deliverable:** A marked-up version of your reference photograph showing eye lines, sight lines, focal-point vectors, and calculated dimensions. Write a brief annotation: "The room is approximately 5 m × 4 m × 3 m high. The sofa is positioned 1.2 m from the entry, requiring visitors to shift their gaze downward 20° from entry sight line to the seating zone."

### Part C: Material and Tectonics Recognition (25 minutes; annotation and research)

Materials carry meaning. A space lined in exposed concrete and steel reads as industrial, rational, and austere. The same space clad in warm oak and plaster reads as domestic, craft-oriented, and intimate. Learning to *see* material is learning to see the values and construction logic embedded in a design.

**Step 1: Catalog Surface Materials**

Create a material legend for your reference space. Using your color-coded annotations from Part A, list each distinct material:

```text
FLOORING:
  - Light oak strip flooring (horizontal grain, matte finish)
  - Ceramic tile at entry (gray, 400 × 400 mm modules, slight sheen)

WALLS:
  - Matte white painted drywall (upper 2200 mm)
  - Wainscot paneling: light gray, horizontal boards, 900 mm height

CEILING:
  - Suspended acoustic panel system, white, recessed linear LED
  
SOFT FINISHES:
  - Curtains: linen-blend, natural beige, floor-to-ceiling
  - Upholstery: wool-mix fabric, warm taupe
```

For each material, estimate its visual properties:
- **Reflectance** (matte = <10% specular reflection; satin = 20–50%; gloss = >50%)
- **Color temperature** (warm, neutral, cool)
- **Texture** (smooth, woven, articulated)

**Step 2: Infer Tectonic Intention**

Tectonics is the art of *expressing* construction through material and form. A material reveals (or hides) how it is attached, joined, and supported. Ask:

- **Horizontal divisions**: Are walls divided by a chair rail, wainscot, or cornice? At what height? (Standard chair rail is 900–1050 mm to protect plaster in traditional interiors; modern divisions often follow functional zones like upper wall storage, lower wall base.)
- **Vertical rhythm**: Are columns, pilasters, or wall panels spaced at regular intervals? Measure and note the spacing.
- **Joints and seams**: Are material transitions emphasized (expressed) or concealed? For example:
  - An expressed joint: a shadow gap or reveal between drywall and trim, clearly showing two materials meeting.
  - A concealed joint: caulk and paint hiding the seam, materials appearing continuous.

Write a short analysis: "The wainscot paneling is expressed with a continuous shadow gap at the 900 mm line, emphasizing the horizontal division and protecting the wall below from contact damage. This suggests a domestic interior where practicality is balanced with aesthetic intention."

**Step 3: Research Material Specifications**

For one primary material in your reference space, research real-world specifications. For example, if the flooring is oak:

- Look up the standard thickness (typically 18–20 mm for strip flooring).
- Note the finished dimensions (e.g., 100 mm width, 1000 mm length).
- Identify the surface finish (satin, matte, or oil-based).
- Document the installation method (glue-down, tongue-and-groove floating, nailed).
- Record the cost range (€40–80 per square meter for good-quality engineered oak).

Use material suppliers' spec sheets (available online from manufacturers like Junckers, Bauwerk, or Nydree). Write a half-page material note in your sketchbook.

**Deliverable:** A material legend with visual swatches (actual samples if available, or printed color chips), technical specifications for one primary material, and a brief tectonic analysis of how materials express construction intent.

## Exercise 2: Technique Study – Designing a Spatial Composition Step by Step

Building on your ability to *read* spaces, you now design one. This exercise teaches the sequential process of spatial design: program definition, conceptual zoning, proportional refinement, material integration, and documentation. The goal is a floor plan and two elevations (or one section and one elevation) that embody compositional unity and human-centered thinking.

### Part A: Program Definition and Conceptual Zoning (40 minutes; sketch and digital)

**Step 1: Define Your Program**

You are redesigning a **small residential living and dining space** with the following constraints:

- Total floor area: 25–30 square meters
- Ceiling height: 2800 mm (fixed structural condition)
- One entry door (standard 900 mm width)
- Two windows on one wall (each 1200 mm wide, 1500 mm high, sill at 900 mm)
- No structural columns in the space
- No mechanical systems visible (heating, cooling, electrical outlets must be integrated into walls)

**Program Brief:**
- Primary function: Comfortable seating and conversation for 4–6 people
- Secondary function: Dining for 4 people
- Storage: Shelving for books and small objects (not full pantry)
- Lighting: Both natural (windows) and artificial ambient + task
- Mood: Calm, craft-oriented, with material warmth

Write this program on the first page of your design sketchbook. Leave 100 mm margins for annotations.

**Step 2: Establish Zoning Relationships**

Sketch rapid plan variations (minimum three) at 1:100 scale showing different **zone arrangements**. Each zone should be a distinct spatial pocket:

- **Zone A: Seating conversation area** (approximately 12 square meters)
- **Zone B: Dining area** (approximately 8 square meters)
- **Zone C: Storage/buffer** (approximately 2–3 square meters)

For each sketch, draw:
- The entry point, door swing, and immediate arrival zone.
- Seating arrangements (use simple rectangular shapes for furniture).
- Dining table location and chair positions.
- Circulation paths (draw with arrows showing traffic flow).

Annotate each sketch with:
- **Flow logic**: "Entry flows naturally to seating; dining is secondary and requires deliberate choice to visit."
- **Sight lines**: "Seated figures can see entry and windows simultaneously, creating openness."
- **Storage proximity**: "Shelving near entry for coats/books; keeps clutter from view."

Compare the three options and select one based on:
1. **Functional clarity**: Does each zone have a single primary purpose?
2. **Visual flow**: Can an entering visitor read the spatial hierarchy?
3. **Daylighting**: Are the inhabited zones positioned to benefit from window light?

**Deliverable:** Three zone sketches at 1:100 scale with annotated logic, plus a brief written justification for the selected zoning scheme.

### Part B: Proportional Refinement and Grid Establishment (45 minutes; digital plan and section)

**Step 1: Establish a Proportional Grid**

Interior spaces are strengthened when their proportions follow a coherent system. You will design using a **modular grid** based on the Golden Section (1 : 1.618).

Your room is approximately 5.0 m × 5.5 m (a floor area of 27.5 m², roughly square to slightly rectangular). Use this grid:

- **Module width**: 500 mm (a human-scale unit; 2.5 modules = 1250 mm, typical seating depth).
- **Grid interval**: 500 mm lines across the entire floor.

In Figma or Illustrator, create a floor plan template:
1. Draw the outer walls at 5000 mm × 5500 mm.
2. Draw the 500 mm modular grid in light gray (opacity 20%) as a background layer.
3. Place windows and doors as fixed constraints.

The grid is a **design aid, not a straitjacket**. Use it to snap major elements (furniture, shelving edges) to grid intersections, but allow human-scaled ergonomic overrides.

**Step 2: Position Furniture Using Ergonomic Clearances**

Using your architect's scale ruler in Figma (or digital scale in Blender), place furniture pieces with accurate dimensions:

**Seating Zone:**
- Sofa: 2200 mm wide × 900 mm deep (standard 3-seater)
- Coffee table: 1200 mm × 600 mm
- Side chair: 850 mm wide × 850 mm deep
- Clearance from sofa to coffee table: 450 mm minimum (for leg room and traffic)
- Clearance from seating to wall: 300 mm minimum (for armrests and comfort)

**Dining Zone:**
- Dining table: 1200 mm × 800 mm (seats 4 comfortably, 6 if 1 on each end)
- Chair depth: 500 mm per place setting
- Clearance behind chair for pulling back: 300 mm minimum
- Clearance between chairs for circulation: 750 mm minimum

**Storage:**
- Floating shelf unit: 2000 mm × 300 mm depth (book depth is 200–250 mm; 300 mm allows for display objects)
- Mounted at 900 mm height (elbow/eye height when seated; comfortable reach when standing)

Place all furniture on your floor plan, snapping major edges to the 500 mm grid. Leave a minimum **circulation zone** of 750 mm width between seating and dining areas, and 900 mm from entry to seating.

**Step 3: Create a Section View Showing Vertical Proportions**

A floor plan shows only horizontal arrangement. To understand spatial volume and human scale, you must also draw a **section**—a vertical slice through the most important part of the space (typically through the seating area and windows).

In Figma, create a section at 1:50 scale showing:

- **Floor line** at 0 mm (baseline).
- **Wall surfaces**: floor to 2800 mm (ceiling). Indicate wall thickness (typically 100–150 mm for interior partitions).
- **Windows**: sill at 900 mm, head at 2400 mm (1500 mm height). Draw the frame depth (typically 75–100 mm).
- **Ceiling**: a simple line at 2800 mm. If suspended, show the drop (typically 100–150 mm for recessed lighting).
- **Furniture**: Draw sofa from side (approximately 400 mm seat height, 650 mm to armrest top, back height 750 mm). Draw a standing figure at 1700 mm to show eye-level relationship.
- **Storage**: Show the floating shelf at 900 mm with mounting bracket visible.

For the standing figure, use the **Loomis figure proportion system**:
- Total height: 7.5 head-heights (approximately 1700 mm for an average adult)
- Eye line: 1–1.5 head-heights from the top (1550 mm from ground)
- Armpit to elbow to fingertip: used to verify arm reach and seating back height relationships

Overlay a **sight line** from the seated figure's eye level (1100 mm) through the window to show what is visible from the main seating.

**Deliverable:** A digital floor plan at 1:100 scale with modular grid visible, furniture positioned with ergonomic clearances annotated, and a section at 1:50 scale showing vertical relationships and human scale.

### Part C: Material Selection and Tectonic Expression (40 minutes; material research and annotation)

**Step 1: Develop a Material Palette**

You will specify four material zones, each with a primary material and one secondary accent. Base your selection on:
- **Warmth and comfort** (soft textures, warm hues for a residential living space)
- **Durability** (materials that withstand regular use and cleaning)
- **Tectonic expression** (joints and edges that reveal construction, not hide it)

Flooring:
- **Primary**: European white oak strip flooring (100 mm × 1000 mm × 18 mm), natural matte finish, laid in random width pattern for visual rhythm.
- **Secondary accent**: Ceramic border tile (300 mm × 300 mm, gray-brown, matte) defining the dining zone threshold.
- **Specification note**: Oak finish is a natural oil (Osmo Hardwax Oil or equivalent), two-coat, allowing wood grain to remain visible and tactile. Installed on a floating engineered subfloor for acoustic comfort.

Walls:
- **Primary**: Smooth, matte-finish painted gypsum board (white, Farrow & Ball "All White" or similar; RGB 250, 250, 248).
- **Secondary accent at seating back**: Textured natural linen fabric panel (600 mm high, behind sofa, providing acoustic absorption and visual warmth). Mounted on a 50 mm ventilation cavity to allow sound dissipation.
- **Specification note**: Linen is a medium-weight plain-weave (350 gsm), natural flax color (warm taupe). It is stapled to a wooden frame and expressed with a 10 mm reveal at top edge, emphasizing the tectonic separation.

Ceiling:
- **Primary**: Smooth gypsum board, painted matte white (same as walls), creating visual continuity.
- **Secondary accent**: Exposed structural element (a wood beam, 150 mm × 100 mm oak, running perpendicular to wall). Left unpainted to show tectonic expression and wood grain.
- **Recessed linear lighting**: 2700K warm-white LED strips (3000 mm total) positioned 200 mm inset from the beam edge, creating a halo effect that emphasizes the ceiling void.

Soft Finishes:
- **Upholstery**: Medium-weight wool-blend fabric (80% wool, 20% synthetic for durability), medium-gray (RGB 120, 120, 115).
- **Curtains**: Natural linen (200 gsm), natural beige (warm off-white), floor-to-ceiling, 100 mm hem.
- **Rug**: Wool flatweave, natural undyed cream with charcoal stripes (200 mm stripe width), 2500 mm × 3000 mm, defining the seating zone.

**Step 2: Justify Material Choices Against Program**

For each material, write a 2–3 sentence justification linking it to the program, human factors, and tectonic intention:

*Oak flooring:* The natural matte finish accepts footfall and use without showing scratches or dust. The random-width pattern creates visual movement without formal repetition, suiting a craft-oriented aesthetic. Oil finish allows the wood to age gracefully and be re-oiled locally rather than sanded and refinished, reducing embodied carbon.

*Linen wall panel:* Positioned at the back of seating, the linen absorbs acoustic energy from conversations, reducing echo in a 25 m² room. Its horizontal grain and warm taupe color provide tactile comfort for backs of heads and necks during extended sitting. Expressed edge reveals the structural frame, teaching the user how the space is constructed.

*Exposed wood beam:* The beam spans the room and is revealed to show the structural logic—the room does not feel arbitrarily rectangular but rather supported by a clear structural order. The 2700K LED positioned to highlight the beam creates focal interest above seated eye level without glare.

**Step 3: Create a Material Board and Color Study**

In a 2000 × 2500 pixel Figma artboard or physical collage (A3 paper), arrange:
- **Physical samples or printed swatches** of each material (5 × 5 cm minimum per sample).
- **Color chip**: Each material's primary color isolated in a 50 × 50 mm square.
- **Labeling**: Material name, finish, supplier, approximate cost per unit.

Under the material board, write **color relationships**:
- **Dominant**: White walls and ceiling (60% of visual field when standing).
- **Secondary**: Oak flooring (25%, warm neutral).
- **Accent**: Linen panel (10%, warm taupe).
- **Accent**: Upholstery and rug (5%, medium gray and cream).

This hierarchy ensures visual restfulness (neutral dominants) with warmth from natural materials (wood and linen) and subtle tonal variation (gray upholstery, cream rug) for interest.

**Deliverable:** A material palette specification sheet (1–2 pages, digital or photographed collage) with physical or printed samples, written justifications for each material, and a color relationship analysis.

## Exercise 3: Finished Piece & Self-Critique – Spatial Visualization and Presentation

A design exists only when it can be **imagined and communicated**. A floor plan alone is abstract. A perspective view, a mood board, or a physical model makes the spatial experience tangible. In this final exercise, you create a presentation-ready visualization and rigorously critique it against compositional and human-centered criteria.

### Part A: 3D Spatial Visualization in Blender (90 minutes; digital)

You will model your designed space in Blender at 1:1 scale (actual millimeters), apply materials, light it, and render a perspective view that communicates the spatial experience.

**Step 1: Scene Setup and Geometry Creation**

Launch Blender 3.6 or later (free, open-source). Create a new General project (File > New > General).

**Units and world setup:**
- Set Blender units to millimeters: Scroll to Scene Properties (cone-and-sphere icon in right sidebar) → Units → Length, set to Metric and Scale to 1 mm.
- Set the world background to a neutral gray for consistent rendering: World Properties (sphere icon) → Background → Color = RGB (128, 128, 128) (hex #808080).

**Create the room geometry:**

1. **Floor**: Delete the default cube (X, confirm). Add a plane (Shift + A > Mesh > Plane). Scale it to room dimensions:
   - Press S (scale), then X (constrain to X-axis), type 2.5 (the default 2 m plane becomes 5 m, i.e. 5000 mm wide), Enter.
   - Press S, then Y, type 2750 (half of 5500 mm depth), Enter.
   - In the properties panel (N key to show), set Location Z to 0 mm (floor surface at world origin).

2. **Walls**: 
   - Add a cube for the left wall: Shift + A > Mesh > Cube.

### Part B: Mood Board and Presentation Sequence (45 minutes; digital collage and sequencing)

A mood board communicates the **emotional and atmospheric** intent of a design. It is not a floor plan or technical drawing, but a collection of images, materials, colors, and typography that embodies the design's character.

**Step 2: Compose the Mood Board in Figma or Photoshop**

**Layout principles:**

- Create a focal hierarchy: One image (the primary interior reference) is 40% of the board; others support at smaller scales.
- Include your material swatches (from Exercise 2) in one corner (100 × 100 mm each).
- Add typography: Use a sans-serif typeface (Helvetica, Roboto, or Inter) in 36 pt for a headline and 18 pt for 2–3 descriptive words.

**Example layout:**
```text
[Large primary interior image—60% left]  [Material swatches—top right, 3 × 3 grid]

```

**Color treatment:**

## Exercise 4: Self-Critique – Rigorous Evaluation Against Compositional and Human-Centered Criteria

A design is only as strong as your ability to **see its flaws and defend its strengths**. This final phase trains your critical eye to evaluate your own work using measurable criteria.

### Part A: Composition Checklist (40 minutes; annotated drawings and written analysis)

You will score your design against five compositional principles. Use a 1–5 scale (1 = fails to address; 5 = exemplary).

**Criterion 1: Spatial Hierarchy and Visual Clarity (Human Factors)**

**Question:** Can an entering visitor immediately understand the spatial organization and primary purpose?

**Evaluation method:**
- Print or display your floor plan and perspective render side by side.
- Imagine entering the space. Mark the **sight lines** from the entry (draw red arrows on the plan showing what is visible immediately upon entry).
- Do sight lines lead naturally to the seating area? Or do they encounter obstacles (walls, furniture) that obscure purpose?
- Assess:
  - **Score 5:** Entry sight line flows directly to main seating; zoning is immediately legible; secondary spaces (dining, storage) are discoverable without confusion.
  - **Score 3:** Entry sight line requires a slight turn or reorientation; primary zone is clear, secondary zones are slightly ambiguous.
  - **Score 1:** Entry sight line encounters barriers; spatial purpose is unclear; zoning feels arbitrary or congested.

**Your evaluation:** _____/5

**Write 3 sentences:** "The entry sight line is unobstructed to the seating zone, clearly establishing it as primary. The dining table is visible in peripheral vision but subordinate, requiring a deliberate turn to engage. Storage shelving is integrated into the seating wall, so it reads as part of the composition, not an afterthought."

---

**Criterion 2: Proportional Coherence (Composition)**

**Question:** Do room dimensions, furniture scale, and material zones follow a coherent proportional system?

**Evaluation method:**
- Measure (using your scale ruler on the floor plan):
  - Room width-to-depth ratio: 5000:5500 ≈ 1:1.1 (nearly square, slightly elongated).
  - Seating zone width: 2500 mm = half the room width.
  - Dining zone width: 2000 mm = 40% of room width.
  - Linen panel height: 600 mm = 21% of wall height (2800 mm).
  
- Check against the Golden Section (1:1.618):
  - Is seating zone related to dining zone by approximately 1:0.618? (2500 × 0.618 ≈ 1545 mm; actual dining ≈ 2000 mm—close but not exact.)
  - Is linen panel height related to wall height by this ratio? (2800 × 0.618 ≈ 1730 mm; your panel is 600 mm—not aligned.)

- Assess:
  - **Score 5:** Multiple proportions follow the Golden Section or another coherent ratio (1:2, 3:4, 2:3). Relationships feel harmonious without redundancy.
  - **Score 3:** Some proportions align (room width-to-depth); others are approximate or arbitrary. Overall effect is balanced but not demonstrably intentional.
  - **Score 1:** Proportions appear random; no discernible system governs zone sizes or material divisions.

**Your evaluation:** _____/5

**Write 3 sentences:** "The room width (5000 mm) to depth (5500 mm) is nearly square (1:1.1), which provides stability. Seating occupies half the width; dining occupies 40%, establishing a clear functional hierarchy. Linen panel height (600 mm) does not align with the Golden Section, but its position at 1100 mm (seated eye level + 100 mm above) is ergonomically justified, prioritizing human use over mathematical purity."

---

**Criterion 3: Material Tectonic Expression (Material & Craft)**

**Question:** Do materials and their joints reveal (not hide) how the space is constructed and maintained?

**Evaluation method:**
- Review your material specifications and elevation drawings. For each major material, ask:
  - **Is the joint expressed?** (a visible gap, shadow line, or reveal indicating where two materials meet)
  - **Is the attachment logic visible?** (e.g., the linen panel's mounting bracket is a 10 mm reveal; the baseboard sits atop the flooring, clearly separating vertical and horizontal elements)
  - **Is material aging anticipated?** (e.g., oil-finished oak will darken and show wear; linen will fade and develop a patina; this is accepted and valued, not hidden by polyurethane or paint)

- Assess:
  - **Score 5:** All major material transitions are expressed with reveals, shadow gaps, or visible joinery. Construction logic is transparent; materials can be replaced or repaired individually without affecting others.
  - **Score 3:** Some transitions are expressed; others are concealed (caulked, painted over). Joinery is partially visible.
  - **Score 1:** Most transitions are hidden; materials appear to meet without visible logic; construction seems arbitrary.

**Your evaluation:** _____/5

**Write 3 sentences:** "The linen panel behind the sofa is mounted with a 10 mm shadow gap at the top, clearly indicating it is a separate installed element, not part of the wall. The baseboard sits atop the oak flooring, visually separating the wall base from the floor plane. The window frame shows a 75 mm reveal, expressing the depth of the wall construction and the window's recessed position."

---

**Criterion 4: Human Scale and Ergonomic Fit (Human Factors)**

**Question:** Do spatial proportions and furniture placement accommodate the user's body, movement, and comfort?

**Evaluation method:**
- Use the **Loomis figure proportions** (7.5 head-heights = 1700 mm for standing, 1100 mm for seated eye level).
- Measure clearances in your plan and section:
  - **Circulation zones:** Minimum 750 mm for single-file; 1200 mm for two-person side-by-side. Do you meet these minimums?
  - **Seating comfort:** Sofa seat height 400 mm; armrest height 650 mm. Are these comfortable for the median adult (25th to 75th percentile heights)?
  - **Eye-level sightlines:** From seated position (1100 mm eye level), can the user see windows, entry, and secondary seating without strain? Or must they crane their neck?
  - **Reach distances:** Can a seated person reach a side table without full upper-body rotation? (Comfortable arm reach ≈ 600 mm forward, 450 mm to the side from center of torso.)

- Assess:
  - **Score 5:** All clearances exceed minimums; seating dimensions are standard and comfortable; sightlines require minimal head movement; reach distances are intuitive and within normal arm range.
  - **Score 3:** Most clearances meet minimums; one or two sightlines require slight head rotation; reach is slightly stretched but feasible.
  - **Score 1:** Clearances are tight; multiple sightlines require significant head movement; reach is awkward or requires standing.

**Your evaluation:** _____/5

**Write 3 sentences:** "Circulation from entry to seating is 750 mm minimum (single-file), meeting code minimums. The sofa seat height (400 mm) and depth (900 mm) are standard, accommodating adults 25th to 75th percentile. From the seated position, eye level (1100 mm) has unobstructed views of both windows and the dining zone, requiring only 15° head rotation—minimal strain."

---

**Criterion 5: Material Authenticity and Craft Intent (Material & Craft)**

**Question:** Do your material choices reflect authentic craft values, or do they simulate luxury without substance?

**Evaluation method:**
- For each material, ask:
  - **Is this material chosen for performance or appearance alone?** (e.g., oak flooring is chosen because wood is durable, beautiful, and repairs locally—not because "wood looks warm")
  - **Is the finish honest?** (e.g., oil finish shows the wood grain and accepts patina vs. polyurethane, which hides grain and conceals aging)
  - **Is the material appropriate to its function?** (e.g., linen absorbs acoustic energy, reducing echo—it is not merely decorative)
  - **Can the material be maintained by the user?** (e.g., oil-finished oak can be re-oiled with simple tools; polyurethane-sealed oak requires professional refinishing)

- Assess:
  - **Score 5:** All materials are chosen for performance + beauty; finishes are authentic and reveal use; materials are repairable and maintainable by the user.
  - **Score 3:** Most materials are chosen for both function and aesthetics; some finishes hide or obscure age; some materials require professional care.
  - **Score 1:** Materials are chosen primarily for appearance; finishes conceal; materials require specialized maintenance; design prioritizes novelty over longevity.

**Your evaluation:** _____/5

**Write 3 sentences:** "Oak flooring is finished with natural oil (Osmo Hardwax Oil), which accepts wear, darkens over time, and can be re-oiled by the owner—this supports longevity and local care. Linen panel provides acoustic absorption (functional) while expressing craft through visible weave (aesthetic). All materials are sourced from sustainable suppliers and are repairable, supporting a craft-centered ethos."

### Common Design Errors and Fixes

**Error 1: "The space feels disconnected—seating and dining have no visual relationship."**

**Root cause:** Zoning is too divided; furniture is pushed to walls, leaving a dead center void.

**Fix:** 
- Reposition seating and dining to share a visual axis (both entities align along a sight line from entry).
- Use a continuous flooring material or a shared rug to unify zones.
- Add a low shelf or half-height divider (600–800 mm) between zones instead of full wall, maintaining visual connection.
- Ensure at least 1200 mm of clear circulation width between zones so they read as adjacent, not separate.

---

**Error 2: "The room feels cramped even though it's 25 m²."**

**Root causes:** 
- Furniture is oversized for the space (a 2500 mm sofa in a 5 m room consumes 50% of one wall).
- Clearances are minimal (300 mm from walls instead of 450–600 mm).
- Ceiling height feels low relative to floor area (2800 mm for a 5 × 5.5 m room has a volume ratio of 1:5.6, skewing horizontal emphasis).

**Fixes:**
- Downsize furniture: Choose a 2000 mm sofa instead of 2200 mm, or a 150 mm-shallower chair (800 mm instead of 850 mm).
- Increase wall clearances to 500 mm minimum, creating breathing space around furniture.
- Visually raise the ceiling: Use vertical wall elements (tall shelving, narrow vertical paneling, or a taller linen panel at 1500 mm instead of 600 mm). Paint ceiling a lighter color (white) than walls (pale gray) to create perceived height.
- Use horizontal materials (long, low shelves; a horizontal stripe in the linen panel) to emphasize width and openness.

---

**Error 3: "The material palette feels disconnected—each zone looks like a different room."**

**Root causes:**
- Too many distinct materials (wood flooring, ceramic tile, painted drywall, linen panel, metal shelving frame, fabric upholstery, etc.).
- Color temperatures clash (warm oak + cool gray paint + warm linen + cool stainless steel).
- No visual repeated element to unify the palette.

**Fixes:**
- Reduce material count to 4–5 primary materials.
- Ensure all warm materials (oak, linen, upholstery) sit in a consistent color temperature (2700K warm white, 95–110 RGB on the warm axis).
- Repeat one material or color in multiple locations: e.g., the warm taupe of the linen panel echoes in upholstery; the white of walls echoes in ceiling; the oak of flooring echoes in a trim or accent beam.
- Use a continuous material transition (e.g., baseboard that runs the full perimeter) to visually tie zones together.

---

**Error 4: "The space is beautiful but feels impractical—there's nowhere to put things."**

**Root causes:**
- Insufficient storage (open shelving for 25 m² living/dining typically requires 8–12 running meters of shelf; your design may have only 2 m).
- No side tables or surfaces for lamps, books, drinks (circulation prioritized over functionality).
- Lighting is atmospheric only; no task lighting for dining or reading.

**Fixes:**
- Add a low console table (600 mm × 400 mm) behind seating for lamps and decorative objects.
- Extend shelving from 2 m to 4 m (a full wall-mounted system instead of a single floating shelf).
- Add a small side table (500 mm × 500 mm) next to the dining table for place settings or serving.
- Include pendant lighting over the dining table (1500 mm above table, 300 mm diameter fixture) for task lighting.
- Reposition entry storage (a narrow console, 1200 mm × 300 mm, for keys and coats) if currently absent.

---

**Error 5: "The floor plan is beautiful, but the rendered perspective doesn't match—materials look different."**

**Root causes:**
- Render lighting is unrealistic (shadows too harsh, ambient fill too low, or artificial light is color-shifted).
- Material properties (roughness, reflectance, color) don't match physical specifications.
- Camera position or focal length distorts spatial perception (ultra-wide-angle lens exaggerates depth; telephoto compresses it).

**Fixes:**
- Verify material Roughness values: matte paint ≥0.9; oil-finished wood 0.3–0.5; linen fabric 0.6–0.8; glazing (windows) 0.0–0.1.
- Set lighting color temperatures: daylight 5500K (cool), artificial indoor 2700K (warm). In Blender, use the Blackbody temperature node or manual RGB values.
- Use a 50 mm focal length (standard, natural perspective) instead of 35 mm (ultra-wide, distorting) or 85 mm (telephoto, compressing).
- Render with 256+ samples for clean, shadow-free lighting.
