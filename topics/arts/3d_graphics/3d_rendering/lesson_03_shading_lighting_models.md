# Lesson 3: Shading Models, Lighting Equations, Material Systems

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The visual appearance of any object in a rendered scene emerges from a fundamental physical process: light strikes a surface, interacts with the material's microscopic structure, and either reflects, absorbs, or transmits that energy. The shading model is your mathematical description of this interaction. For centuries, painters understood this intuitively—how a silk fabric catches light differently than rough canvas, how metal gleams while marble diffuses. Computer graphics formalizes this intuition into equations we can evaluate in real time.

The journey from early computer graphics to modern rendering reveals a progression of increasing physical accuracy. The Phong reflection model, introduced by Bui Tuong Phong in 1975, was a pragmatic approximation that captured the essential visual characteristics of how surfaces behave under light: diffuse reflection spreading light uniformly across a surface, and specular reflection concentrating light into bright highlights. Phong was never meant to be physically correct—it was engineered to *look good* and run fast on 1970s hardware. Yet it established a conceptual framework that remains foundational: every surface has inherent color properties, responds differently to direct light depending on surface orientation, and exhibits highlights.

Today's physically-based rendering (PBR) systems take a different philosophical stance. Rather than tweaking arbitrary parameters to achieve a desired look, PBR grounds shading in measured material properties that correspond to real-world measurements. A material's roughness, metallic content, and base reflectivity are derived from actual spectrophotometric data or established physical principles. This approach yields two profound advantages: materials behave consistently across different lighting environments, and you can build extensive material libraries that work reliably in any scene you construct.

At the deepest level, all shading models solve the same fundamental problem: given a point on a surface, a light source, an observer, and the material properties, compute the reflected radiance—the intensity and color of light traveling from that point toward the camera. The mathematics differs, but the purpose remains identical. The Phong model approximates this through geometric calculations and empirically chosen exponents. PBR models derive their equations from the bidirectional reflectance distribution function (BRDF), which mathematically describes how light reflects from a surface given the incoming and outgoing directions.

Normal mapping introduces a critical layer of complexity and efficiency. A surface's geometric normal—the direction perpendicular to the polygonal face—defines how light interacts with that surface. Yet true geometric detail is expensive: a finely wrinkled leather jacket would require millions of polygons. Normal mapping achieves the visual illusion of geometric detail by storing surface normals in a texture. As the shader evaluates the lighting equations, it reads the perturbed normal from the texture rather than using the geometric normal. This technique has been revolutionary because it decouples visual complexity from geometric polygon count, allowing artists to create intricate surface detail at minimal performance cost.

Texture coordinates form the bridge between abstract 3D geometry and the image data that defines appearance. Every vertex in your mesh carries UV coordinates—a 2D parameterization that maps points on the 3D surface to locations in a 2D texture. The U and V axes correspond to the horizontal and vertical axes of your texture image. This mapping is neither automatic nor trivial. A UV map must unfold the 3D surface into 2D space while minimizing distortion, similar to how a cartographer flattens Earth's spherical surface onto a flat map. Seams in the UV layout are inevitable—the points where the 2D texture wraps around the 3D geometry—and careful seam placement is an art unto itself.

The shader execution model describes *where* and *when* these calculations occur. In a traditional graphics pipeline, the vertex shader runs once per vertex of your mesh, performing transformations and passing data to the fragment shader. The fragment shader then runs for every pixel that the triangle covers on screen. This is where the shading calculations—the lighting equations, texture lookups, and normal perturbations—typically execute. Modern GPUs run thousands of fragment shaders in parallel, which is why shaders must be highly optimized: a single expensive operation repeated across millions of pixels can devastate performance.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

### The Phong Reflection Model: Geometric Foundations

The Phong model decomposes reflected light into three independent components: ambient, diffuse, and specular. Let's examine each with rigor because understanding their geometric meaning shapes how you use them effectively.

**Ambient light** represents indirect illumination—light that has bounced around the environment so many times that its origin is effectively forgotten. In Phong, it's a constant color multiplied by the surface's ambient material coefficient. This is a crude approximation because real indirect light varies by position and orientation, but it's computationally free and prevents unlit areas from appearing completely black. In practice, the ambient term should be relatively dim; setting it too high flattens the image by reducing shadow depth.

**Diffuse reflection** occurs when light strikes a rough surface and scatters uniformly in all directions. A piece of matte paper or unglazed ceramic exhibits primarily diffuse behavior. The intensity of diffuse reflection depends on the angle between the incoming light direction and the surface normal. When light hits perpendicular to the surface (angle of zero degrees), energy transfer is maximum. As the angle increases, the light spreads across a larger area per unit intensity, and reflection diminishes. This relationship is governed by the cosine of the angle—a relationship so fundamental that it appears in virtually every lighting model. Mathematically, diffuse intensity equals the maximum diffuse color multiplied by max(0, dot(normal, light_direction)). The max operation prevents negative values when light comes from behind the surface.

**Specular reflection** models the bright highlights that appear on shiny surfaces. Unlike diffuse light, specular reflection has a preferred direction: the direction of perfect mirror reflection. When you look toward a light source reflected in polished metal, the reflection appears bright precisely in the mirror direction. The specular term in Phong computes this by measuring the angle between the view direction and the reflection direction. If they align, you see a bright highlight; if they diverge, the intensity rapidly falls off. This falloff is controlled by a *shininess* exponent, an arbitrary parameter that ranges from values like 4 (very rough, broad highlight) to 128 or higher (very shiny, sharp specular peak). The exponent is not physically motivated—it's purely an aesthetic parameter chosen to achieve desired visual results.

The Phong equation in its complete form reads:

```text
L_out = k_a * I_ambient + k_d * I_light * max(0, N·L) + k_s * I_light * max(0, R·V)^shininess
```

where N is the surface normal, L is the normalized light direction, V is the normalized view direction, R is the reflection direction (computed as reflect(L, N)), k_a/k_d/k_s are material coefficients between 0 and 1, I_ambient and I_light are light color values, and shininess is the exponent controlling specular sharpness.

### Physically-Based Rendering and the BRDF

Physically-based rendering inverts the philosophical approach: instead of choosing arbitrary parameters to match a desired look, measure real materials and encode those measurements into rendering equations. The mathematical foundation is the bidirectional reflectance distribution function, which describes the fraction of incoming light energy that reflects in any given direction.

A BRDF is fundamentally a function of four angles: the incoming light direction (theta_i, phi_i) and the outgoing view direction (theta_o, phi_o), both expressed relative to the surface normal. Integrating the BRDF over all possible incoming directions, weighted by the cosine of the angle and the light intensity, yields the total reflected radiance at that point. This is the rendering equation—a deceptively simple formula that encapsulates all of computer graphics: the reflected light in a given direction equals the integral of incoming light from all directions multiplied by the BRDF for that direction pair.

The Disney/Pixar BRDF and similar physically-based models represent real-world materials using just a handful of intuitive parameters. The most critical is **roughness**, which ranges from 0 (mirror-like) to 1 (completely matte). Physically, roughness parameterizes the microscopic surface irregularities. A perfectly smooth surface has parallel microfacets that all reflect light in the same direction, producing a sharp specular peak. A rough surface has randomly oriented microfacets; light reflects in many directions simultaneously, producing broad, diffuse-looking highlights or no highlight at all.

Roughness is implemented through the **Fresnel term** and the **distribution function** within the BRDF. The Fresnel term describes how reflectivity changes with viewing angle—a phenomenon you observe when looking at water or glass nearly edge-on versus straight down. At grazing angles, even non-metallic surfaces become highly reflective; this is why distant ocean horizons appear like mirrors. Metals exhibit different Fresnel behavior than dielectrics. For dielectrics, the Fresnel value at normal incidence is low (typically 0.04 for most plastics and ceramics); metals like aluminum have high Fresnel values across all angles.

The distribution function, often parameterized by roughness, controls how the specular highlight spreads. A microfacet-based BRDF models the surface as composed of tiny mirror facets at random angles. Rough surfaces have facets oriented in many directions, so light reflects broadly. Smooth surfaces have facets nearly parallel, producing concentrated reflection. Common distribution functions include Beckmann and GGX (Trowbridge-Reitz). GGX has become an industry standard because it produces visually pleasing results and behaves well at grazing angles.

The PBR rendering equation in its most common practical form uses a split-sum approximation for real-time performance. Rather than integrating across the entire hemisphere every frame—computationally prohibitive—the integral is split into two precomputed components. The first is a diffuse integration stored in an irradiance map or irradiance volume; the second is a specular integration stored in a split-sum texture. During real-time rendering, you look up these precomputed values using the surface normal and roughness, avoiding expensive integration at runtime. This technique is called image-based lighting (IBL), and it is the workhorse of modern real-time PBR.

### Normal Mapping and Perturbation

A normal map is simply a texture where each pixel's color encodes a 3D normal vector. The convention is to store the X, Y, and Z components of the normal in the R, G, and B channels respectively. Since normal components range from -1 to 1, and texture channels range from 0 to 1, the transformation is: (R, G, B) = (X + 1, Y + 1, Z + 1) / 2. This is reversed in the shader to recover the actual normal.

The art of normal mapping lies in understanding reference frames. A normal map can be authored in **tangent space** or **world space**. Tangent-space normal maps are the industry standard because they are tileable and reusable across many different surfaces and orientations. A tangent-space normal map is defined relative to the local surface tangent and bitangent vectors, not the world coordinate system. To use a tangent-space normal map, the shader must construct a tangent-to-world transformation matrix—the TBN matrix—using the vertex normal, tangent, and bitangent (the binormal). The TBN matrix transforms the normal from tangent space into world space, where it can be used in the lighting calculations.

Computing the TBN matrix is a common source of confusion. The normal and tangent vectors are typically provided by the 3D modeling software and stored as vertex attributes. The bitangent (or binormal) is computed as the cross product of the normal and tangent: `bitangent = cross(normal, tangent)`. However, tangent space can be oriented in two ways (mirrored), distinguished by the tangent's W component (also called handedness). When constructing the TBN matrix, you must account for this: `bitangent = cross(normal, tangent) * tangent.w`. This single scalar reverses the bitangent direction if tangent.w is -1, ensuring correct orientation.

In the fragment shader, you read the normal map texture, convert from [0, 1] range to [-1, 1] range, and transform using the TBN matrix:

```text
vec3 normal_tangent = texture(normalMap, uv).rgb * 2.0 - 1.0;
vec3 normal_world = normalize(TBN * normal_tangent);
```

Normal map intensity—how strongly the perturbation affects the final normal—is controlled by a strength parameter. Multiplying the XY components of the tangent-space normal by a strength value before transformation scales the effect. Strength of 0 leaves the geometric normal unchanged; strength of 1 uses the map as authored; values above 1 exaggerate the effect.

Normal maps can also use the derivative of height, which is more intuitive for artists to author. A height map stores scalar height values; the normal is derived by taking finite differences of adjacent height samples. This is often precomputed offline: the height map is converted to a normal map during asset import so the shader need not perform expensive derivative calculations at runtime.

### Texture Coordinate Systems and UV Unwrapping

Every surface point in your mesh must have associated UV coordinates that map to locations in your texture images. The UV parameterization is established during modeling, and its quality directly affects both visual fidelity and shader performance.

The fundamental constraint is that UV coordinates must be continuous across the visible surface and discontinuous only at seams, which are typically placed along edges where the seam is least visible—inside an object's crevices, along hair partings, or along the spine of a book. A poorly seamed UV map will exhibit visible discontinuities in textures at seam locations. Seams must be explicitly welded in the mesh: vertices on opposite sides of a seam are separate vertices in the geometry, not shared vertices. This allows the fragment shader to sample different parts of the texture at geometrically adjacent pixels.

Texel density—the ratio of texture pixels (texels) to world-space area—should be consistent across the mesh. If one part of the mesh has much higher texel density than another, it will appear either blurry or unnaturally sharp depending on the direction of the mismatch. Most modern engines provide tools to visualize texel density and identify problem areas.

Distortion is another critical metric. When you unfold a 3D surface into 2D space, some stretching or compression is inevitable unless the surface is already naturally developable (like a cylinder or cone). Conformal mappings preserve angles but not areas; equal-area mappings preserve proportions but may distort angles. For real-time graphics, some distortion is acceptable—it's usually imperceptible if kept below about 5-10% across most of the mesh—but extreme distortion will make textures appear warped or incorrectly scaled.

For objects with multiple UV sets, the convention is to pack UV channels: the diffuse/color texture uses UV channel 0; the normal map might use UV channel 0 or 1; lightmaps typically use a separate, pre-computed UV channel with higher density in shadow areas; and specialty maps like detail normal maps might use a third channel. This allows different texture types to share or diverge in their parameterization, optimizing either memory or quality depending on requirements.

### The Shader Execution Model

The graphics pipeline executes in stages, but the two stages most relevant to shading are the vertex shader and the fragment shader. Understanding their constraints and capabilities is essential for efficient shader design.

The **vertex shader** runs once per vertex of your mesh. It receives vertex attributes (position, normal, tangent, UVs, colors) and outputs the transformed position and any data needed by the fragment shader. This is where you perform vertex transformations: multiplying the vertex position by the model matrix, then the view matrix, then the projection matrix to convert from object space to screen space. The vertex shader also typically transforms normals and tangents to world space or view space, and passes them as varying variables to the fragment shader. Because the vertex shader runs only once per vertex regardless of how many pixels that triangle covers, expensive calculations here scale well. Conversely, calculations repeated per fragment scale poorly.

The **fragment shader** (also called the pixel shader) runs once for every pixel that a triangle covers on screen. With modern screen resolutions and multi-sampling antialiasing, this can mean tens of millions of invocations per frame. Any calculation performed in the fragment shader is amplified by this factor, making efficiency paramount. Texture lookups, dependent calculations (calculations that depend on texture results), and expensive math operations should be minimized.

Data flows from the vertex shader to the fragment shader through varying variables. The GPU automatically interpolates these values across the triangle face. If the vertex shader outputs a normal at each vertex, the GPU linearly interpolates those normals across the triangle surface, and the fragment shader receives the interpolated value. This interpolation is perspective-correct, accounting for the triangle's depth in the view frustum.

Conditional logic in shaders deserves special consideration. Modern GPUs handle branching, but branches that diverge across a warp of threads (typically 32 pixels on NVIDIA hardware, 64 on AMD) can cause performance degradation. If some pixels in the warp take one branch and others take a different branch, the GPU must serialize the execution, running both branches and discarding results from the irrelevant branch. Branches that are uniform across a warp—all pixels take the same path—execute with minimal overhead. In practice, complex conditional logic is sometimes slower than computing both branches and blending results, depending on the operations involved.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

### Optimization Strategies for Real-Time Shading

The primary constraint in real-time rendering is time: you have a frame budget, typically 16.67 milliseconds at 60 frames per second, within which you must execute vertex shaders for millions of vertices, fragment shaders for millions of pixels, and compute any other necessary calculations. Shading optimization requires both algorithmic choices and practical measurement.

**Texture lookups** are the most expensive operations in fragment shaders because they require memory access. A single texture lookup involves accessing GPU memory, which can have a latency of 100+ clock cycles if the texel is not cached. However, modern GPUs have sophisticated texture caches that exploit spatial and temporal locality, making sequences of nearby lookups relatively fast. The implication is that you should prefer multiple lookups of nearby texture coordinates over fewer lookups of distant coordinates. Also, dependent texture lookups—lookups whose coordinates depend on results from prior lookups—prevent the GPU from prefetching the required data, stalling the pipeline. Minimize dependent lookups where possible.

**Floating-point precision** trades quality for speed. Using half-precision floats (16-bit) instead of full precision (32-bit) halves memory bandwidth and computation time for many operations, and many modern GPUs execute half-precision operations faster than full precision. For intermediate calculations and values that don't directly affect visual output, half precision is often sufficient. Colors are typically stored in 8-bit or 16-bit formats anyway; additional precision in intermediate calculations provides diminishing returns. However, transformations and normal vectors benefit from full precision because small errors accumulate and produce visual artifacts.

**Normal map compression** reduces memory bandwidth. Standard normal maps use three channels (X, Y, Z), but the third component can be reconstructed from the first two because normals are unit vectors: Z = sqrt(1 - X² - Y²). Storing only X and Y reduces texture size by 50% and bandwidth proportionally. This technique is widely supported and produces no visual loss because the reconstruction is mathematically exact (barring quantization error inherent to 8-bit storage anyway).

**Parallax mapping and parallax occlusion mapping** enhance the apparent depth of normal maps. Simple normal mapping creates an illusion of surface detail, but examining the silhouette reveals the fraud—the geometric outline remains unchanged. Parallax mapping offsets the UV coordinates based on the view direction and height map, creating the visual impression that the surface is displaced. The offset is small but effective at conveying relief. Parallax occlusion mapping (POM) performs multiple samples of the height map along the view ray, iteratively refining the UV offset to find the intersection between the view ray and the displaced surface. POM is more expensive than parallax mapping but produces superior visual results, particularly at grazing angles. The performance cost is typically acceptable in fragment shaders where texture bandwidth is already the dominant expense.

**Deferred rendering** is a fundamentally different shading architecture that can offer significant optimization in high-light-count scenarios. Rather than computing lighting per surface immediately (forward rendering), deferred rendering first renders all surface properties (normal, diffuse color, roughness, etc.) into off-screen buffers—the G-buffer. Then, in a separate pass, lights are rendered as geometry (typically full-screen quads or spheres) and accumulate their contribution. This decouples the cost of shading from the number of lights: forward rendering's cost is O(surfaces × lights); deferred rendering's cost is O(surfaces) + O(lights × screen_area). For scenes with dozens of lights, deferred rendering is substantially faster. The tradeoff is increased memory bandwidth due to G-buffer writes and reads, and complexity in supporting transparent objects and anti-aliasing.

### Common Failure Modes and Diagnostics

**Normal map artifacts** are frequent sources of visual errors. Seam discontinuities appear as visible lines or misalignment of texture detail along UV seams. This usually indicates that normal map padding is insufficient; the edges of the normal map should be padded with a one-pixel border that's a continuation of the adjacent texture content, preventing sampling edge pixels that correspond to a discontinuous region. Another source is incorrect tangent space orientation—if the TBN matrix is computed incorrectly or the normal map was authored in the wrong handedness, normals will point in wrong directions, producing lighting that appears inverted or skewed.

**Specular aliasing** occurs when rough surfaces with small specular highlights are undersampled, producing flickering or noise in specular regions. This is a fundamental limitation of single-sample-per-pixel rendering. Anti-aliasing strategies like temporal anti-aliasing (using information from prior frames to improve current frame quality) or adaptive super-sampling can mitigate this.

**Energy conservation violations** manifest as surfaces that appear too bright or that gain energy under certain lighting conditions. This typically stems from incorrect BRDF normalization—the integral of the BRDF over the hemisphere must not exceed one for any incoming direction. Common causes include specular lobes that are too wide or too intense, or diffuse terms that fail to account for the Fresnel effect's energy distribution between reflection and refraction. The diagnostic approach is to render a sphere under uniform white lighting and verify that no point exceeds the albedo brightness; if highlights blow out, the specular term is likely unnormalized. Another source is double-counting: adding a separate specular reflection on top of a physically-based material that already accounts for specular via Fresnel, effectively counting specular energy twice.

**Shadow acne and peter-panning** are depth-precision artifacts in shadow mapping. Shadow acne appears as self-shadowing noise on surfaces facing the light, caused by insufficient depth bias—the surface's depth value in the shadow map is nearly equal to the fragment's depth during shadow testing, and floating-point precision causes random pass/fail results. The fix is a constant or slope-scaled bias that offsets the shadow comparison. Too much bias, however, causes *peter-panning*: shadows detach from their casters and appear to float. The correct bias value depends on shadow map resolution, surface slope relative to the light, and depth precision. A robust implementation uses adaptive bias based on the dot product of the surface normal and light direction, applying minimal bias to surfaces perpendicular to the light and more bias to grazing angles. Another mitigation is percentage-closer filtering (PCF), which samples the shadow map multiple times with slight offsets and averages the results, softening hard shadow edges and reducing the visibility of precision artifacts.
