# Advanced Foundation: Guitar
## 1. Theoretical Invariants & Formal Frameworks

### Formal Proofs in Guitar Theory
Formal proofs in guitar theory can be constructed around the invariants of the instrument's acoustics and the mathematical properties of music. For instance, the proof of the equal temperament scale can be formalized using logarithmic functions and the twelfth root of two, which ensures that the frequency ratio between any two adjacent notes is constant.

### Kernel or Physical Invariants
The physical invariants of a guitar include the string tension, bridge position, and the material properties of the body and neck. These invariants are crucial for maintaining the instrument's acoustic properties and tuning stability. For example, the bridge position is a critical invariant that affects the balance between the fundamental frequency and the overtones produced by the strings.

## 2. Low-Level Invariant Taxonomy

### Deep Registers
Deep registers on a guitar refer to the lower-pitched strings (E, A, D). The invariants in these registers include the string gauge, tension, and the physical properties of the wood used in the body and neck. These invariants are essential for producing a rich, resonant sound in the lower frequencies.

### Memory Models
In the context of guitar performance, memory models can be analogized to the guitarist's muscle memory and cognitive memory. Muscle memory is the physical memory of the fingers and hands, while cognitive memory is the mental memory of chord shapes, scales, and techniques. Both types of memory are critical for executing complex guitar parts with precision and speed.

### Hardware Constraints
Hardware constraints on a guitar include the number of strings, the scale length, and the material properties of the strings. These constraints affect the instrument's range, tuning, and overall sound. For example, the scale length determines the distance between the nut and the bridge, which affects the string tension and the pitch of the notes.

### Axiomatic Definitions
Axiomatic definitions in guitar theory include the fundamental principles of music theory, such as the twelve-tone equal temperament scale and the harmonic series. These definitions provide a foundation for understanding the mathematical properties of music and the acoustics of the guitar.

## 3. Micro-Architecture & Concurrency Dynamics

### Mermaid and ASCII State Machines
State machines can be used to model the different modes and techniques used in guitar performance. For example, a state machine can be used to model the different fingerings and techniques used in playing a scale or a chord. The Mermaid and ASCII state machines can be used to visualize the transitions between different states and the conditions that trigger these transitions.

### Cache and Race Condition Diagrams
Cache and race condition diagrams can be used to model the different ways in which a guitarist can approach a complex piece of music. For example, a cache diagram can be used to model the different ways in which a guitarist can memorize and execute a complex chord progression. A race condition diagram can be used to model the different ways in which a guitarist can coordinate the left and right hands to execute a complex technique, such as a sweep picking pattern.

## 4. Subtle Failure Modes & Boundary Conditions

### Subtle Failure Modes
Subtle failure modes in guitar performance include issues such as intonation problems, buzzing strings, and poor tone. These issues can arise from a variety of factors, such as improper string tension, incorrect bridge position, or poor finger technique. Identifying and addressing these subtle failure modes is critical for producing a high-quality sound.

### Boundary Conditions
Boundary conditions in guitar performance include the limits of the instrument's range, the limits of the guitarist's physical abilities, and the limits of the music theory. For example, the boundary conditions for the instrument's range include the lowest and highest notes that can be played on the guitar. The boundary conditions for the guitarist's physical abilities include the limits of finger dexterity and endurance. The boundary conditions for music theory include the limits of the twelve-tone equal temperament scale and the harmonic series.
