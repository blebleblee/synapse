# Advanced Foundation: Drums

## 1. Theoretical Invariants & Formal Frameworks

### Formal Proofs in Drumming
Formal proofs in drumming can be constructed by defining a set of axioms and deriving theorems that govern the behavior of drumming techniques and patterns. For example, an axiom could be that a basic stroke on a drumhead produces a sound wave of a certain frequency and amplitude. From this, we can derive theorems about the relationship between the force of the stroke and the resulting sound wave.

### Kernel or Physical Invariants
Physical invariants in drumming refer to the unchanging principles that govern the interaction between the drummer, the drum, and the environment. One such invariant is the conservation of energy in the system. The energy applied by the drummer through their limbs is converted into sound energy, which is then dissipated into the environment. This principle can be formalized using the laws of physics, particularly the conservation of energy and the principles of wave propagation.

## 2. Low-Level Invariant Taxonomy

### Deep Registers
In the context of drums, deep registers refer to the lower frequency sounds produced by larger drums or deeper parts of the drum set. These sounds are characterized by longer wavelengths and lower energy density. The invariant here is that the frequency of the sound wave is inversely proportional to the wavelength, and the amplitude is related to the energy input.

### Memory Models
In drumming, memory models can be thought of as the mental models that drummers use to store and recall patterns and rhythms. These models are crucial for improvisation and complex pattern execution. The invariant is that the memory model must accurately represent the patterns and rhythms to ensure consistent and accurate performance.

### Hardware Constraints
Hardware constraints in drumming refer to the physical limitations of the drum set and the drummer's body. For example, the size and weight of the drum set can affect the drummer's ability to perform certain techniques, and the physical limitations of the drummer's limbs can affect the speed and precision of their playing. The invariant is that the hardware constraints must be respected to avoid injury and ensure optimal performance.

### Axiomatic Definitions
Axiomatic definitions in drumming are the fundamental truths that are accepted without proof. For example, an axiom could be that a closed hi-hat produces a sound with a shorter decay than an open hi-hat. These axioms form the basis for more complex theories and techniques in drumming.

## 3. Micro-Architecture & Concurrency Dynamics

### Mermaid and ASCII State Machines
State machines can be used to model the different states and transitions in drumming techniques. For example, a state machine could model the different states of a hi-hat (open, closed, half-open) and the transitions between these states. The invariant is that the state machine must accurately represent the possible states and transitions in the technique.

### Cache and Race Condition Diagrams
In drumming, cache and race condition diagrams can be used to model the interaction between different parts of the drum set and the drummer's limbs. For example, a cache diagram could model the different ways that a drummer can store and recall patterns, and a race condition diagram could model the potential conflicts that arise when multiple limbs are used to play different parts of the drum set simultaneously. The invariant is that the diagrams must accurately represent the interaction between the different parts of the system.

## 4. Subtle Failure Modes & Boundary Conditions

### Subtle Failure Modes
Subtle failure modes in drumming refer to the small errors that can occur during performance that are not immediately obvious but can affect the overall quality of the performance. For example, a subtle failure mode could be a slight inconsistency in the timing of a stroke that is not noticeable on its own but can accumulate over time and affect the overall rhythm. The invariant is that subtle failure modes must be identified and corrected to ensure optimal performance.

### Boundary Conditions
Boundary conditions in drumming refer to the extreme cases that can occur during performance that push the limits of the drummer's technique and the hardware. For example, a boundary condition could be playing a very fast pattern on a very small drum or playing a very loud pattern on a very large drum. The invariant is that boundary conditions must be understood and respected to avoid injury and ensure optimal performance.
