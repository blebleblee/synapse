# Lesson 2: Chord Progressions

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Chord progressions form the bedrock of Western music, offering harmonic structure and emotional depth to compositions. At their core, chord progressions are sequences of chords that traverse a piece of music, generating a sense of tension and resolution. This movement is governed by the interplay of tonal harmony and the listener's expectations, grounded in the principles of equal temperament and the overtone series.

In equal temperament, the frequency ratio between any two adjacent notes is the twelfth root of two, ensuring that the scale is evenly divided into twelve semitones. This system allows for the modulation of keys without significant tuning issues, facilitating the use of various chord progressions across different musical styles. The overtone series, the natural harmonic series produced by a vibrating string or air column, plays a crucial role in our perception of consonance and dissonance. Chords that align closely with the overtone series are perceived as consonant, while those that do not are dissonant, creating a sense of tension that can be resolved by moving to a more consonant chord.

The most fundamental chord progression in Western music is the I-IV-V-I progression, which is based on the tonic, subdominant, and dominant chords of a key. This progression is deeply ingrained in our musical consciousness, providing a sense of resolution and closure. The I chord, being the tonic, serves as the home base of the key, while the IV and V chords introduce tension that is resolved by returning to the I chord. This progression can be found in countless pop songs, classical pieces, and jazz standards, making it a versatile and essential tool for composers and performers.

Understanding chord progressions requires a grasp of functional harmony, which categorizes chords based on their role in a progression. The tonic (I) is the most stable chord, serving as the resting point of the progression. The dominant (V) is the most unstable, creating a strong pull towards the tonic. The subdominant (IV) and mediant (III) chords provide a sense of contrast and movement, while the submediant (VI) and leading tone (VII) chords offer a more subtle tension and resolution. By understanding these functional roles, composers can create complex and emotionally rich chord progressions that resonate with listeners.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The architecture of chord progressions is built upon the interplay of harmonic functions and the underlying mathematical principles of equal temperament. At a fundamental level, a chord progression can be represented as a sequence of chord symbols, each indicating the root note and quality of the chord (major, minor, diminished, augmented, etc.). For example, the I-IV-V-I progression in the key of C major can be represented as Cmaj7-Fmaj7-Gmaj7-Cmaj7.

To dissect the mechanics of chord progressions, we can examine the voice leading between chords, which refers to the movement of individual notes from one chord to the next. Effective voice leading ensures smooth and efficient transitions between chords, minimizing dissonance and maintaining a sense of continuity. One common technique in voice leading is the use of common tones, notes that are shared between two chords and remain stationary as the other notes move. For example, in the Cmaj7-Fmaj7 progression, the note C can serve as a common tone, providing a stable point of reference as the other notes move.

Another important aspect of chord progressions is the use of chord inversions, which refer to the different ways a chord can be arranged by rearranging the order of its notes. In root position, the root note of the chord is in the bass, while in first inversion, the third of the chord is in the bass, and in second inversion, the fifth of the chord is in the bass. Chord inversions can be used to create smooth voice leading and to emphasize different notes within the chord, providing a richer harmonic texture.

To illustrate the mechanics of chord progressions, we can examine the I-IV-V-I progression in the key of C major using chord inversions. In root position, the progression would be Cmaj7-Fmaj7-Gmaj7-Cmaj7. By using first inversion for the Fmaj7 chord and second inversion for the Gmaj7 chord, the progression becomes Cmaj7-Fmaj7/3-Gmaj7/5-Cmaj7. This inversion creates a smoother voice leading, with the note F moving to G and the note G moving to C, providing a more fluid and harmonious transition.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

In the realm of chord progressions, high-performance optimization involves creating efficient and effective progressions that resonate with listeners and serve the musical goals of the composition. One key aspect of optimization is the use of harmonic tension and resolution, which can be achieved through the strategic use of dominant and diminished chords. Dominant chords, particularly the V7 chord, create a strong pull towards the tonic, providing a sense of resolution and closure. Diminished chords, on the other hand, create a sense of unresolved tension, which can be used to build anticipation and create dramatic effects.

Another important aspect of optimization is the use of modal interchange, which involves borrowing chords from parallel modes to create harmonic interest and variety. For example, in the key of C major, the chord progression Cmaj7-Gmaj7-Amaj7 can be varied by borrowing the D7 chord from the parallel minor mode, creating the progression Cmaj7-Gmaj7-D7-Amaj7. This borrowing of the D7 chord adds a sense of harmonic tension and interest, providing a richer and more dynamic progression.

Failure modes in chord progressions can arise from a lack of harmonic coherence or an overreliance on clichéd progressions. A lack of harmonic coherence can result in dissonant and jarring progressions that fail to resonate with listeners. To mitigate this, composers should pay close attention to voice leading and chord inversions, ensuring smooth and efficient transitions between chords. An overreliance on clichéd progressions can result in a lack of originality and creativity, failing to engage listeners on an emotional level. To avoid this, composers should experiment with different chord progressions and harmonic techniques, drawing inspiration from a wide range of musical styles and traditions.

Chord progressions are a fundamental aspect of Western music, providing harmonic structure and emotional depth to compositions. By understanding the theoretical grounding, core mechanics, and optimization techniques of chord progressions, composers and performers can create rich and engaging musical works that resonate with listeners. Through the strategic use of harmonic tension, resolution, and modal interchange, composers can create chord progressions that are both efficient and effective, providing a foundation for compelling musical narratives.
