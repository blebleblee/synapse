# Lesson 1: Metering Reference Calibration

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Every mastering decision downstream of this chapter rests on measurement. When the measurement is wrong, or when you misunderstand what it represents internally, every subsequent decision inherits that error silently. This is the same discipline you practice as an engineer when you refuse to reason about system behavior from a dashboard without first understanding the sampling interval, aggregation method, and clock source behind the numbers. Loudness and peak metering in audio demand exactly that skepticism. The numbers are deceptively simple floating-point values that hide several layers of signal processing, each with its own assumptions and failure modes.

The foundational problem metering solves is that human perception of loudness does not correlate with raw signal amplitude. A sine wave at $-10$ dBFS and a dense, cymbal-heavy mix at $-10$ dBFS RMS can produce wildly different perceived loudness. The ear's sensitivity is frequency-dependent and temporally integrating. Early engineers used VU meters, simple ballistic devices with a 300 ms integration time constant, calibrated so that 0 VU corresponded to a reference voltage, typically $+4$ dBu in professional American broadcast chains. VU meters are crude analog low-pass filters on signal energy. They persist today as a stylistic choice rather than a precision instrument. Their slow, soft response flatters transient-heavy material and makes mix buses look musically alive rather than technically accurate.

Peak Program Meters (PPM) were built to protect transmission and recording chains from clipping. They used fast attack times, on the order of a few milliseconds, to catch transient peaks that VU meters would physically miss due to inertia. The tension between these two meter philosophies—one tracking perceived loudness, the other tracking instantaneous overload risk—is the direct ancestor of the modern split between LUFS (Loudness Units relative to Full Scale) and True Peak measurement. These are two orthogonal axes of information, not two measurements of the same thing from different angles. One tells you how loud something will sound to a human over time. The other tells you how close the raw waveform comes to the hard mathematical ceiling of your digital system. Mastering requires satisfying constraints on both axes simultaneously. Conflating them is the single most common error made by engineers who are mathematically literate but new to audio.

The modern standard, ITU-R BS.1770, with EBU R128 as its most widely adopted broadcast-delivery profile, was developed because broadcasters discovered that simple peak normalization let advertisers and program producers compete to sound louder than their neighbors through dynamic range compression, a phenomenon colloquially known as the loudness war. If you normalize only peak amplitude, you can make something sound subjectively far louder without the peak meter ever registering a difference. Perceived loudness is a function of average energy weighted by frequency, not instantaneous amplitude. BS.1770 solves this by defining an algorithm that approximates the human ear's frequency sensitivity curve and integrates energy over time, producing a number in LUFS that correlates far more reliably with subjective loudness than any peak-based or flat-RMS measurement.

There are three temporal windows in the standard. Conflating them is the second most common error in the field. Momentary loudness integrates over a sliding 400 ms window with no gating, intended to give you a fast-responding but still perceptually meaningful readout, analogous to watching instantaneous CPU load. Short-term loudness integrates over a 3-second sliding window, giving you a sense of a phrase or section's loudness trajectory, analogous to a rolling average over a short observation period that smooths out transient noise. Integrated loudness measures the entire program from start to stop, but critically, it is not a simple mean. It applies a two-stage gating algorithm. First, an absolute gate discards any 400 ms block quieter than $-70$ LUFS, removing silence and noise floor from skewing the average. Then a relative gate discards any block quieter than 10 LU below the ungated mean, removing quiet passages like a soft piano intro from unduly lowering the perceived loudness of a track that is mostly loud. This double-gating structure exists because a single arithmetic mean would be dragged down by silence between songs or quiet intros, producing a number that technically describes the file but not what a listener will report as perceived loudness.

True Peak is the other invariant you must hold constant in your mental model. It is frequently misunderstood even by engineers who know DSP thoroughly. Sample peak measurement simply reports the largest absolute sample value in the digital signal, the maximum of $|x[n]|$ across the buffer. But a digital signal is a set of discrete samples representing an underlying continuous waveform. When you reconstruct that continuous waveform—which is exactly what a DAC, or any downstream lossy codec's decoder, does—the interpolated curve between samples can overshoot the sample values themselves, particularly in high-frequency content close to Nyquist where adjacent samples are both near the ceiling but the true waveform between them peaks higher. This is a classic reconstruction filter ringing phenomenon, mathematically identical to Gibbs phenomenon overshoot. True Peak metering estimates this real continuous-time peak by oversampling the signal, typically 4× per the BS.1770 specification, and then reporting the maximum absolute value of the oversampled, reconstructed waveform. Sample peak metering can show $-0.1$ dBFS while True Peak metering on the same file reports $+0.8$ dBTP. That 0.9 dB discrepancy is not measurement noise. It is real energy that will clip in your DAC, in a listener's lossy-codec decoder, or in a broadcast chain's analog output stage, even though your file's raw sample values never technically exceeded digital full scale.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The BS.1770 loudness measurement pipeline is best understood as a signal processing chain with four discrete, ordered stages. Picture it exactly as you would picture a request pipeline in a backend service, where each stage transforms the signal and hands it to the next with no backward dependency.

The first stage applies K-weighting, a composite frequency-weighting filter designed to approximate the ear's sensitivity curve. K-weighting is realized as two cascaded second-order IIR filters (biquads). The first is a high-frequency shelf filter centered around 1.5 kHz, providing approximately $+4$ dB of gain above 2 kHz. This models the ear's increased sensitivity to high-frequency content, particularly in the presence region where consonants and attack transients live. The second is a high-pass filter (RLB filter, Revised Low-frequency B-weighting) with a cutoff around 38 Hz, rolling off subsonic content that contributes energy but not perceived loudness. These two filters are applied in series to each channel independently. The combined response approximates the inverted equal-loudness contour, meaning that after K-weighting, signals of equal perceived loudness should produce roughly equal energy regardless of frequency content.

The second stage computes the mean square (power) of the filtered signal over the measurement window. For momentary loudness, the window is 400 ms. For short-term loudness, the window is 3 seconds. For integrated loudness, the window is divided into overlapping 400 ms blocks, which are then processed through the gating algorithm. The mean square is computed as:

$$\overline{x^2} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} x[n]^2$$

where $N$ is the number of samples in the window and $x[n]$ is the K-weighted sample at index $n$. This is the unweighted power. For multi-channel audio, each channel's mean square is computed independently and then combined using channel-specific weighting coefficients.

The third stage applies channel weighting. BS.1770-4 specifies the following weights: left and right channels are weighted at 1.0 each, center channel at 1.0, left and right surround channels at 1.41 (approximately $+1.5$ dB), and left and right back surround channels at 1.41. LFE (low-frequency effects) channels are excluded from the loudness calculation entirely. The weighted sum of mean squares is:

$$\overline{x^2}_{\text{total}} = \sum_{c} G_c \cdot \overline{x^2}_c$$

where $G_c$ is the weighting coefficient for channel $c$ and $\overline{x^2}_c$ is the mean square of that channel. The surround channels are weighted higher because they contribute less to frontal perceived loudness in typical playback environments, and the standard compensates by giving them more weight in the calculation.

The fourth stage converts the weighted mean square to a loudness value in LUFS:

$$L = -0.691 + 10 \log_{10}(\overline{x^2}_{\text{total}})$$

The $-0.691$ dB offset is a calibration constant that aligns the LUFS scale with the original Leq(RLB) scale used in early drafts of the standard. This ensures that 0 LUFS corresponds to full scale on a perfectly K-weighted sine wave. In practice, you rarely encounter signals that reach 0 LUFS without clipping, because full-scale sine waves are mathematical constructs, not music.

For integrated loudness specifically, the gating algorithm operates on the 400 ms block loudness values computed in the second stage. The absolute gate at $-70$ LUFS removes blocks that are effectively silence or noise floor. After applying the absolute gate, the algorithm computes the mean loudness of the remaining blocks. This ungated mean is then used to set the relative gate threshold at $-10$ LU below the ungated mean. Any block quieter than this relative threshold is discarded. The final integrated loudness is the mean of all blocks that passed both gates. This double-gating approach ensures that the integrated loudness reflects the dominant loudness level of the program, not the arithmetic mean of all energy including silent passages.

True Peak measurement operates independently of the loudness pipeline. The signal is first upsampled by a factor of four using a polyphase FIR filter bank that implements bandlimited interpolation. The BS.1770-4 specification provides exact filter coefficients for the 4× upsampler to ensure interoperability across implementations. After upsampling, the algorithm computes the sample peak of the upsampled signal. This upsampled peak approximates the continuous-time peak of the reconstructed analog waveform. The True Peak level in dBTP is:

$$\text{TP} = 20 \log_{10}(\max(|x_{\text{upsampled}}[n]|))$$

where $x_{\text{upsampled}}[n]$ is the 4× oversampled signal. True Peak metering is always applied per-channel and the maximum across all channels is reported as the program True Peak. Broadcasting standards typically require True Peak to stay below $-1$ dBTP to provide headroom for downstream processing and lossy codec artifacts.

The relationship between LUFS and dBTP is uncorrelated in the general case. A heavily compressed track can have a high LUFS value (say, $-8$ LUFS) and a True Peak close to 0 dBTP. A dynamic orchestral recording might have a low integrated LUFS (say, $-23$ LUFS) but occasional transient peaks that approach 0 dBTP. The two measurements constrain different failure modes. LUFS constrains perceived loudness to prevent the loudness war and ensure consistent playback levels across programs. True Peak constrains intersample peaks to prevent clipping in reconstruction and codec artifacts. You must satisfy both constraints independently. Normalizing to a LUFS target does not guarantee True Peak compliance, and limiting to a True Peak ceiling does not guarantee LUFS compliance.

The practical implementation of this pipeline requires careful attention to numerical precision and filter state management. The K-weighting biquads are second-order IIR filters, which means they have internal state and can accumulate numerical error if implemented naively in single-precision floating point. Professional implementations use double-precision arithmetic for the filter state or periodically flush denormals to zero to prevent underflow. The 4× upsampling FIR filter is typically implemented using a polyphase decomposition to avoid computing three out of every four output samples that would be immediately discarded. The gating algorithm requires buffering the entire program in memory or streaming through it twice, once to compute the ungated mean and once to apply the relative gate. For real-time metering, only momentary and short-term loudness are available, as integrated loudness requires knowledge of the entire program.

These two pipelines run in parallel and produce independent measurements. The loudness pipeline produces LUFS values for momentary, short-term, and integrated windows. The True Peak pipeline produces a single dBTP value representing the maximum intersample peak across the program. Both are necessary for broadcast and streaming delivery compliance.

TheBS.1770 algorithm is deterministic and platform-independent when implemented correctly. Two conformant implementations processing the same audio file must produce identical LUFS and True Peak values within a small tolerance dictated by floating-point rounding. If your measurements differ significantly from reference implementations, your filter coefficients are wrong, your gating logic is incorrect, or your upsampling filter is not bandlimited. The ITU-R BS.1770-4 technical specification includes test vectors for validation. Use them.

The gating algorithm's behavior on edge cases is worth internalizing. A program consisting entirely of silence will report $-\infty$ LUFS because no blocks pass the absolute gate. A program with a single loud sample surrounded by silence will have a very low integrated LUFS because the relative gate will discard most of the program. A program with two distinct loudness levels, such as a quiet verse and a loud chorus, will report an integrated loudness closer to the louder section because the relative gate discards the quieter section if it is more than 10 LU below the ungated mean. This is intentional. The standard is designed to measure the dominant loudness level, not the average of all energy. If this behavior surprises you, revisit your assumptions about what integrated loudness is supposed to represent. It is not an RMS measurement. It is a perceptual model of sustained program loudness.
