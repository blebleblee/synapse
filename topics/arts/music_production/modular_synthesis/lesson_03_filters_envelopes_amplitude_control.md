# Lesson 3: Filters, Envelopes, Amplitude Control

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The lineage of voltage-controlled filters in modular synthesis originates not from digital signal processing textbooks, but from the empirical circuit designs of analog engineers working in the 1960s and 1970s. Robert Moog's transistor ladder filter, Don Buchla's low-pass gate combining filtering with amplitude control, and the state-variable designs pioneered by ARP and Oberheim emerged from direct acoustic observation: the natural world rarely produces static, unchanging timbres. A struck drum exhibits a sharp transient followed by rapid decay. A bowed violin string swells gradually into sustained resonance. Wind instruments articulate attacks with breath pressure curves that shape both amplitude and harmonic brightness simultaneously. The modular synthesizer, in its quest to generate and sculpt voltage-controlled sound, must therefore provide two essential transformation domains—spectral shaping through filtering and temporal envelope control—unified under a final amplitude scaling stage that translates control voltages into audible dynamic range.

At its foundation, a voltage-controlled filter operates as a frequency-dependent attenuator. Where an oscillator generates a harmonically rich waveform—a sawtooth wave carries energy at the fundamental frequency plus every integer harmonic above it, diminishing in amplitude at a rate of 6 dB per octave—the filter selectively removes or emphasizes portions of this spectrum according to its topology. The lowpass filter passes frequencies below a cutoff point while attenuating those above. The highpass filter inverts this behavior, removing bass energy and preserving treble. The bandpass filter isolates a narrow spectral window, passing only frequencies within a defined bandwidth centered around the cutoff. Each topology imposes a slope steepness measured in decibels per octave: a 12 dB/octave filter halves the amplitude of frequency components one octave above the cutoff, while a 24 dB/octave design quarters it. This slope steepness directly correlates with the filter's order—a second-order filter yields 12 dB/octave, a fourth-order filter produces 24 dB/octave—and the number of reactive components (capacitors and inductors or their transistor-based emulations) in the signal path.

Resonance introduces a deliberate instability at the cutoff frequency itself. By feeding a portion of the filter's output back into its input with correct phase alignment, energy accumulates at the cutoff point, creating a pronounced peak in the frequency response. Engineers quantify this peak as the quality factor $Q$, defined as the ratio of center frequency to bandwidth. A filter with $Q = 0.707$ exhibits maximally flat passband response (the Butterworth characteristic). As $Q$ increases beyond unity, the peak sharpens into a resonant horn, emphasizing frequencies precisely at the cutoff with intensities that can exceed the input signal by 20 dB or more. At the threshold of self-oscillation—typically $Q$ values approaching 50 or higher depending on circuit topology—the filter becomes an oscillator in its own right, generating a sine wave at the cutoff frequency even with no input signal present. This behavior reflects a pole-zero constellation in the complex frequency plane migrating onto the imaginary axis: the system transitions from stable filtering to sustained oscillation, a phase transition software engineers might recognize as analogous to a control system crossing from damped to undamped response.

The voltage control of cutoff frequency follows the 1 volt per octave standard established across modular synthesis. A control voltage increase of 1 volt shifts the cutoff frequency upward by one octave, doubling its Hertz value. This exponential voltage-to-frequency relationship mirrors the logarithmic nature of human pitch perception: we hear equal musical intervals (octaves, fifths, fourths) as equal perceptual distances despite their exponential spacing in linear frequency. Internally, analog filter circuits achieve this exponential conversion through matched transistor pairs operating in their exponential collector current regime, or through precision exponential converters built from op-amps and thermally coupled transistor arrays. Digital implementations employ lookup tables or polynomial approximations to the exponential function, trading ROM space or computational cycles for tracking accuracy across the full 10-octave audio range.

## 2. Envelopes & Temporal Scaffolding

Envelopes provide the temporal scaffolding upon which static voltages evolve into dynamic gestures. The canonical ADSR envelope generator divides a triggered event into four distinct phases: Attack, Decay, Sustain, and Release. Upon receiving a gate signal—a positive voltage indicating a key press or sequencer trigger—the envelope output begins rising toward a peak voltage over the Attack interval. Once the peak is reached, the voltage descends toward the Sustain level over the Decay phase. Unlike the first three stages measured in time constants, Sustain represents a static voltage level maintained as long as the gate remains high. When the gate drops to zero, the Release phase initiates, returning the envelope output to ground over its specified duration. This four-stage contour maps directly onto the mechanical physics of acoustic instruments: the Attack corresponds to the hammer striking the piano string or the bow engaging the violin, the Decay captures the rapid initial damping as transient energy dissipates, the Sustain models the steady-state vibration while energy input continues, and the Release mirrors the final ring-down after the player releases the key or lifts the bow.

The shape of these transitions—linear ramps versus exponential curves—profoundly affects musical realism and perceptual impact. Human auditory perception operates on a logarithmic amplitude scale: we perceive equal ratios of intensity as equal differences in loudness. A linear envelope ramping from 0 to 10 volts over one second increases voltage at a constant 10 volts per second, but the perceptual loudness change appears to accelerate as the voltage climbs. An exponential envelope, by contrast, approaches its target asymptotically according to a time constant $\tau$, reaching approximately 63% of the final value after one $\tau$, 86% after two $\tau$, and 95% after three $\tau$. This exponential curvature matches the charging behavior of an RC (resistor-capacitor) circuit, the fundamental building block of analog envelope generators, and delivers perceptually smooth transitions that mirror natural acoustic decay.

## 3. Voltage-Controlled Amplification & Dynamic Scaling

Voltage-controlled amplifiers complete the signal chain, translating control voltages into gain coefficients that scale audio signals from silence to full amplitude. A VCA operating under linear voltage control applies gain proportional to the control voltage: 5 volts yields 50% amplitude scaling, 10 volts produces full amplitude. This arithmetic relationship simplifies patching and modulation routing but creates perceptual nonlinearity when controlling loudness. The human ear perceives amplitude on a logarithmic scale, where each 10 dB increase represents a doubling of perceived loudness but requires a tenfold increase in acoustic power. Consequently, synthesizer designers frequently implement exponential VCA response curves, where the gain $G$ follows $G = 10^{(V/10)}$ for control voltage $V$, ensuring that equal voltage increments produce equal loudness increments across the dynamic range. This distinction between linear and exponential VCA response becomes critical when patching envelopes to control amplitude: an exponential envelope feeding a linear VCA compounds curvature, while a linear envelope driving an exponential VCA may produce perceptually more natural dynamics.

The interplay between filters, envelopes, and amplitude control extends beyond sequential signal processing into cross-domain modulation that defines the character of a synthesized voice. Patching the same ADSR envelope to both VCA gain and filter cutoff frequency creates synchronized brightness and loudness contours: as the note swells in volume during the Attack phase, its harmonic spectrum simultaneously opens, mimicking the natural behavior of acoustic instruments under increasing excitation. Inverting this relationship—applying an envelope with fast Attack to the VCA but slow Attack to the filter—produces percussive attacks with gradually blooming timbral complexity, a technique exploited in plucked string emulations and synthetic bass patches. The feedback loop inherent in high-resonance filter settings introduces dynamic instability: as the filter self-oscillates, its output feeds back through the VCA, creating amplitude modulation that transforms static resonance peaks into evolving, quasi-chaotic textures. These emergent behaviors, arising from the nonlinear interactions between nominally linear building blocks, distinguish modular synthesis from fixed-architecture instruments and reward exploratory patching with sonically unpredictable results.

Understanding the analog circuit implementations underlying these modules reveals the constraints and coloration that define vintage and contemporary hardware character. The Moog transistor ladder filter achieves its iconic sound through four cascaded transistor stages, each contributing 6 dB/octave of rolloff for a cumulative 24 dB/octave slope. The exponential relationship between transistor base-emitter voltage and collector current provides inherent voltage-to-frequency tracking, but temperature dependence and component matching tolerances introduce subtle pitch drift and harmonic distortion at high resonance settings. This distortion, far from a defect, contributes to the filter's musical character: as resonance increases and the filter approaches self-oscillation, harmonic and intermodulation products emerge, adding grit and animation to otherwise sterile waveforms. The Buchla low-pass gate, by contrast, employs vactrols—photoresistive optocouplers where an LED illuminates a cadmium sulfide photoresistor—to simultaneously control filtering and amplitude. The intrinsic lag in the vactrol's response, typically 5 to 50 milliseconds depending on LED drive current, imposes natural slew rate limiting on control voltage changes, softening transients and imparting an organic, breath-like quality to envelope-controlled passages. State-variable filters, implemented with operational amplifiers and resistor-capacitor integrators, offer simultaneous lowpass, bandpass, and highpass outputs from a single input, enabling parallel spectral processing and voltage-controlled morphing between filter types by crossfading between output taps.

Digital filter implementations, whether executed in dedicated DSP hardware or general-purpose CPUs, confront different tradeoffs. Finite impulse response (FIR) filters achieve linear phase response and guaranteed stability at the cost of computational expense: realizing a steep 24 dB/octave rolloff may require hundreds of multiply-accumulate operations per sample, straining real-time processing budgets on resource-constrained platforms. Infinite impulse response (IIR) filters, implemented as recursive difference equations that mirror analog prototypes, demand far fewer operations—a fourth-order Butterworth lowpass typically requires only five multiplications and four additions per sample—but introduce phase distortion and potential numerical instability when high resonance drives filter poles near the unit circle in the $z$-plane. Oversampling and multi-rate processing mitigate aliasing artifacts that arise when time-varying cutoff frequencies or high resonance cause spectral content to fold back into the audio band, but at the expense of proportionally increased computational load. Modern plugin architectures employ SIMD (single instruction, multiple data) vector processing to parallelize filter calculations across multiple voices, achieving polyphony counts that would have been unthinkable in early digital synthesizers while preserving the low-latency response essential for expressive performance.

The mathematical formalism underlying filter design begins with the analog prototype transfer function $H(s)$ in the Laplace domain, where $s = \sigma + j\omega$ represents complex frequency. A second-order lowpass filter takes the canonical form:

$$H(s) = \frac{\omega_c^2}{s^2 + \frac{\omega_c}{Q}s + \omega_c^2}$$

where $\omega_c = 2\pi f_c$ denotes the cutoff angular frequency and $Q$ specifies resonance. The denominator polynomial defines the filter's poles—the values of $s$ that make $H(s)$ infinite—which determine both rolloff steepness and resonance peak height. For $Q < 0.5$, the poles remain real and distinct, yielding overdamped response with no overshoot. At $Q = 0.707$, the poles coincide on the real axis, producing critically damped Butterworth response. Beyond $Q = 0.707$, the poles become a complex conjugate pair, migrating toward the imaginary axis as $Q$ increases. When the poles reach the $j\omega$ axis itself, the filter achieves self-oscillation, sustaining a sinusoidal output indefinitely.

Translating this analog prototype into the digital domain requires the bilinear transform, which maps the entire left half of the $s$-plane (stable analog systems) into the interior of the unit circle in the $z$-plane (stable digital systems). The substitution:

$$s \to \frac{2}{T}\frac{1 - z^{-1}}{1 + z^{-1}}$$

where $T$ denotes the sampling period, yields the discrete-time transfer function $H(z)$. Frequency warping distorts the analog frequency axis near the Nyquist limit, requiring pre-warping the cutoff frequency before transformation to ensure the digital filter's cutoff matches the analog specification. The resulting difference equation, obtained by cross-multiplying and inverse $z$-transforming $H(z)$, becomes the recursive formula implemented in code:

$$y[n] = b_0 x[n] + b_1 x[n-1] + b_2 x[n-2] - a_1 y[n-1] - a_2 y[n-2]$$

where $x[n]$ represents the input sample stream, $y[n]$ the output, and the coefficients $\{b_i, a_i\}$ depend on the pre-warped cutoff frequency and $Q$ value. Modulating these coefficients at audio rate—for instance, applying an LFO to cutoff frequency—requires recalculating trigonometric and hyperbolic functions every sample, a computational bottleneck that drives optimizations like coefficient interpolation, lookup table quantization, and SIMD vectorization in production synthesizer code.

Envelope generator circuit topologies span a continuum from simple RC charge-discharge networks to precision voltage-controlled integrators. The elementary capacitor-based envelope employs a transistor switch to alternately charge the capacitor through an Attack resistor and discharge through a Decay resistor, with the gate signal controlling the switch state. The exponential voltage curves arising naturally from $V(t) = V_{\text{final}}(1 - e^{-t/RC})$ provide musically pleasing transitions, but the fixed time constants offer limited range. Digitally controlled analog envelopes replace fixed resistors with voltage-controlled current sources, allowing microprocessor-generated control voltages to dictate Attack, Decay, and Release times across spans from microseconds to tens of seconds. Fully digital envelopes, implemented as state machines incrementing or decrementing counters according to user-specified rates, achieve arbitrary curvature through piecewise-linear approximation or spline interpolation, at the cost of quantization artifacts when low sample rates or limited bit depths constrain resolution.

The sustain phase introduces a conceptual asymmetry: while Attack, Decay, and Release measure time durations, Sustain specifies a voltage level. This distinction reflects the gate-driven nature of keyboard performance, where note duration remains under the player's continuous control during the Sustain phase. Some envelope generators extend the ADSR model with additional stages: the ADSR + Delay prepends a fixed waiting period before Attack begins, useful for staggered modulation in layered patches. The AHDSR inserts a Hold stage between Attack and Decay, maintaining the peak voltage for a specified duration regardless of gate length, mimicking the initial plateau in struck percussion transients. The multi-stage envelope, exemplified by Buchla's 281 Quad Function Generator and contemporary Eurorack modules like Maths, provides up to eight independently adjustable segments, each with voltage level and time duration, enabling complex contours that transcend the acoustic instrument metaphors underlying the original ADSR formulation.

Patching strategies that exploit the interaction between filters, envelopes, and VCAs define musical genres and synthesis styles. In subtractive synthesis, a sawtooth or pulse oscillator feeds a lowpass filter with high resonance, while an ADSR envelope modulates both the filter cutoff and VCA gain. Short Attack and Decay times with zero Sustain yield plucked or percussive timbres; longer Attack and sustained amplitude produce pad textures. The filter cutoff envelope often employs faster time constants than the amplitude envelope, creating brightness transients that precede loudness peaks, a perceptual cue the ear interprets as sharpness or aggression. In West Coast synthesis, pioneered by Buchla, the low-pass gate merges filtering and amplitude control into a single vactrol-driven module, with envelope depth simultaneously affecting brightness and loudness in a unified gesture that mirrors breath or bow pressure. The absence of separate VCA and filter stages reduces patching complexity but constrains independent timbral and dynamic shaping, a philosophical tradeoff that distinguishes East Coast (Moog-derived, modular flexibility) from West Coast (Buchla-derived, integrated gestural control) synthesis traditions.

Voltage-controlled filter tracking, where the cutoff frequency follows the pitch of the played note via a 1V/octave control voltage derived from the keyboard or sequencer, maintains consistent timbral balance across the musical range. Without tracking, a lowpass filter set to 1 kHz cutoff darkens bass notes while leaving treble notes unaffected, a frequency-dependent coloration that may or may not serve musical intent. Full tracking—cutoff frequency proportional to note pitch—preserves the ratio of cutoff to fundamental across octaves, ensuring a C2 bass note and a C5 melody note exhibit identical harmonic truncation relative to their respective fundamentals. Partial tracking, achieved by attenuating the pitch CV before it reaches the filter's frequency modulation input, offers intermediate behaviors: 50% tracking raises the cutoff by half an octave for every octave increase in pitch, brightening higher notes while maintaining low-frequency warmth. This technique proves essential in bass synthesis, where full tracking would render low notes too bright and high notes excessively dull.

The resonance parameter, beyond its spectral emphasis function, interacts nonlinearly with envelope modulation to produce dynamic timbral complexity. A filter with moderate resonance ($Q \approx 5$) and cutoff swept by a fast envelope generates a vowel-like formant peak that slides through the harmonic series, a technique exploited in analog bass lines and lead synthesis. Increasing resonance to near-self-oscillation ($Q \approx 30$) transforms the filter into a pitched resonator: the resonant peak becomes so narrow that it emphasizes only harmonics closely aligned with the cutoff frequency, effectively quantizing the output spectrum to a harmonic series based on the cutoff pitch rather than the oscillator pitch. This behavior, called filter FM or resonance tracking, enables melodic sequences played purely by modulating filter cutoff, with the oscillator providing only a noisy excitation source. At the limit of self-oscillation, the filter outputs a sine wave at the cutoff frequency independent of input signal, functioning as an auxiliary oscillator that can be mixed with the primary oscillator for detuned beating effects or used standalone for pure-tone drones.

Amplitude control via VCAs extends beyond simple envelope-gated loudness to encompass tremolo, ring modulation, and dynamics processing. Tremolo results from patching a low-frequency oscillator to the VCA's control input, imposing periodic gain variation at sub-audio rates (0.1 to 20 Hz). When the modulation frequency exceeds approximately 20 Hz, the ear ceases to perceive individual amplitude pulses, instead hearing sidebands at frequencies equal to the carrier frequency plus and minus the modulation frequency—the hallmark of amplitude modulation (AM). Ring modulation, achieved by feeding the VCA control input with an audio-rate oscillator and ensuring the VCA operates in a linear multiplication mode, produces sidebands at the sum and difference of the carrier and modulation frequencies, creating inharmonic clangorous tones characteristic of metallic percussion and science-fiction sound effects. Dynamics processing, implemented by analyzing the input signal's amplitude envelope via a peak or RMS detector and using the detected level to inversely control VCA gain, yields compression, limiting, or gating effects. A compressor attenuates loud signals more than soft ones, reducing dynamic range and increasing perceived loudness. A noise gate mutes the output when the input falls below a threshold, eliminating background noise during silent passages.

The cascade and parallel connection topologies of multiple filters further expand timbral possibilities. Cascading two lowpass filters in series doubles the rolloff steepness: two 12 dB/octave filters produce a cumulative 24 dB/octave slope, yielding sharper spectral truncation. Resonance interactions between stages can reinforce or cancel depending on cutoff frequency alignment and phase relationships, a complexity that motivates the use of matched multi-pole filter designs rather than independent single-pole stages. Parallel filtering splits the signal into multiple paths, each passing through a filter with distinct cutoff and resonance settings, then recombines the outputs via a mixer. This vocoder-like approach creates formant-like spectral peaks at each filter's resonant frequency, enabling vowel synthesis and complex timbral morphing controlled by voltage-scaling the individual filter outputs. The state-variable filter's simultaneous lowpass, bandpass, and highpass outputs invite parallel mixing with voltage-controlled crossfading, morphing continuously from lowpass through bandpass to highpass by sweeping a single control voltage.

The historical evolution of envelope and filter modules reflects the ongoing tension between analog authenticity and digital flexibility. Early modular synthesizers like the Moog System 55 and ARP 2500 employed discrete transistor circuits, offering lush analog warmth at the cost of temperature drift, component aging, and expensive hand-matched parts. The advent of voltage-controlled integrated circuits like the SSM2040 and CEM3320 in the late 1970s commoditized filter design, enabling mass-market instruments like the Sequential Prophet-5 and Oberheim OB-series to deliver multiple voices of subtractive synthesis at accessible price points. The 1990s saw the rise of fully digital synthesis, with plugins and workstation synthesizers emulating analog filter characteristics through oversampled IIR algorithms and waveshaping nonlinearities that mimic transistor saturation. The 21st-century Eurorack renaissance revived discrete analog designs, with boutique manufacturers hand-building ladder filters, vactrols, and other vintage topologies for niche markets willing to pay premium prices for the ineffable qualities—phase coherence, distortion profiles, control voltage droop—that digital emulation struggles to capture. Meanwhile, hybrid designs like the DSP-driven Intellijel Polaris combine digital control precision with analog signal paths, offering patch storage and MIDI integration alongside the sonic idiosyncrasies of capacitor-charged envelopes and transistor-based VCAs.

Practical patching exercises illuminate the interplay between theoretical principles and hands-on synthesis technique. Patching a sawtooth oscillator into a 24 dB/octave lowpass filter with resonance set to self-oscillation threshold, then modulating the cutoff with an ADSR envelope having 50 ms Attack, 200 ms Decay, 50% Sustain, and 500 ms Release, produces a classic analog bass tone: the rapid Attack creates a sharp brightness transient, the Decay settles into a sustained mid-range resonance, and the Release gently closes the filter as the note decays. Increasing the envelope amount (the scaling factor applied to the ADSR output before it modulates the filter) extends the cutoff sweep range, opening the filter further during Attack and creating more dramatic timbral animation. Adjusting resonance upward pushes the filter toward self-oscillation, adding a pitched component at the cutoff frequency that harmonically reinforces the oscillator fundamental when the cutoff tracks keyboard pitch. Reducing Attack time to 1 ms or less yields percussive plucks; extending it to 1 second or more creates ambient swells that bloom from silence into full spectral complexity.

Layering multiple envelopes controlling different parameters within a single voice enables polyphonic modulation schemes that transcend the limitations of single-envelope architectures. A secondary envelope, inverted and scaled, can modulate oscillator pulse width while the primary envelope controls filter cutoff, creating timbral and harmonic variations that evolve independently. A tertiary envelope driving resonance amount automates filter emphasis dynamically, causing the resonant peak to swell and recede asynchronously with brightness and amplitude changes. These multi-envelope patches, while conceptually simple, demand disciplined gain staging and offset management: envelope outputs typically span 0 to 8 volts, but a VCF's frequency modulation input may require only 0 to 5 volts for full range modulation, necessitating attenuators or precision adders to scale and combine control voltages without clipping or underutilization.

The role of filters and envelopes in sound design extends beyond imitative synthesis into speculative sonic territories that exploit the modular synthesizer's capacity for emergent behavior. Self-patched feedback loops, where a filter's output returns to its own input after passing through a VCA and delay line, generate evolving textures that blur the line between filtering and generative composition. Chaotic modulation, achieved by cross-coupling multiple oscillators and filters with resonance set high, produces fractal-like spectra that change unpredictably yet remain bounded within defined frequency ranges. These experimental techniques, rooted in cybernetic and systems-theory approaches popularized by composers like Morton Subotnick and Suzanne Ciani in the 1960s and 1970s, continue to inspire contemporary electronic musicians exploring the intersection of algorithmic control and analog unpredictability.

In conclusion, the triumvirate of filters, envelopes, and amplitude control constitutes the dynamic engine of modular synthesis, transforming static oscillator waveforms into evolving, expressive sonic gestures. Filters carve spectral contours through frequency-dependent attenuation and resonant emphasis, envelopes impose temporal arcs that mirror acoustic instrument physics, and VCAs translate control voltages into the loudness variations that define musical phrasing. Mastery of these modules demands both theoretical understanding of their signal-processing foundations and empirical intuition developed through exploratory patching. The analog circuit idiosyncrasies—temperature drift, component tolerances, nonlinear distortion—that early synthesizer designers struggled to minimize have become, in the contemporary landscape, celebrated sonic signatures that distinguish vintage hardware from digital emulation. Whether working in a hardware modular system, a software plugin environment, or a hybrid rig combining both, the synthesist who internalizes the principles outlined in this lesson gains the fluency to shape sound with intention, coaxing from voltage-controlled circuits the timbral transformations and dynamic gestures that elevate synthesis from technical exercise to musical expression.
