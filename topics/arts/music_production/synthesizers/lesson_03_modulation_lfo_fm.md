# Lesson 3: Modulation Architecture, LFO, FM Synthesis

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The fundamental distinction between static and dynamic sound lies in modulation. A sine wave oscillator producing 440 Hz will generate a pure, lifeless tone—mathematically perfect but musically sterile. The moment we introduce time-variant control over any parameter of that oscillator, we cross the threshold into organic, evolving timbre. Modulation is the systematic application of one signal to control the behavior of another, and it represents the single most powerful architectural pattern in synthesis design.

At its core, modulation operates on a control voltage paradigm inherited from the analog modular era. In the Eurorack standard, one volt per octave governs pitch, while auxiliary control voltages manipulate filter cutoff, pulse width, amplitude, and dozens of other parameters. This voltage-as-data abstraction translates seamlessly into the digital domain: where analog circuits use varying DC offsets and AC waveforms to modulate transistor bias points and integrator charging rates, digital synthesizers use floating-point sample streams to control algorithm parameters at audio or sub-audio rates. The invariant principle remains identical—a source signal imposes time-variant change upon a destination parameter.

Low-frequency oscillators occupy the foundational layer of modulation architecture. Unlike audio-rate oscillators that generate audible pitched tones above approximately 20 Hz, LFOs operate in the sub-audio range from 0.01 Hz to roughly 20 Hz, producing cyclical control signals too slow to hear as pitch but fast enough to create rhythmic, pulsing, or sweeping parameter changes. The genius of the LFO lies in its dual nature: it is simultaneously a clock generator for rhythmic effects and a continuous control source for smooth, organic variation. A sine wave LFO modulating filter cutoff produces a gentle wah effect; the same LFO routed to amplitude creates tremolo; applied to pitch, it generates vibrato. The waveform shape determines the character of modulation—sine waves yield smooth, symmetrical motion; triangle waves provide linear ramps; square waves produce hard-switched alternation; and sawtooth waves create rising or falling sweeps. Sample-and-hold waveforms introduce stochastic stepped modulation, essential for algorithmic composition and generative sequences.

The mathematical relationship between modulation rate and musical perception reveals critical design constraints. At 0.1 Hz, an LFO completes one cycle every ten seconds—glacially slow, suitable for evolving pad textures or cinematic soundscapes. At 1 Hz, we perceive a clear pulse synchronized to one beat per second at 60 BPM. At 4 Hz, the modulation rate begins to approach rhythmic subdivision, creating rapid tremolo or trill-like effects. As LFO frequency crosses approximately 10 Hz, we enter the audio-rate boundary where modulation becomes fast enough to generate audible sidebands—this is the threshold where LFOs transition from control signals into audio-rate modulation sources capable of producing timbral transformation through frequency modulation synthesis.

Frequency modulation represents a fundamentally different synthesis paradigm from the subtractive architecture we explored in previous lessons. Where subtractive synthesis begins with harmonically rich oscillators and removes spectral content through filtering, FM synthesis generates complex harmonic and inharmonic spectra through the mathematical interaction of multiple oscillators. The core principle, discovered by John Chowning at Stanford in 1967 and commercialized by Yamaha in the DX7 synthesizer, involves using one oscillator—the modulator—to control the instantaneous frequency of another oscillator—the carrier. This seemingly simple relationship produces astonishingly complex timbral results that would require dozens of oscillators and filters to approximate through subtractive means.

The mathematics of frequency modulation begin with a carrier oscillator producing a sine wave at frequency $f_c$. When a modulator oscillator at frequency $f_m$ is applied to control the carrier's instantaneous frequency, the modulation depth (called the modulation index, denoted $I$) determines the intensity of the effect. The resulting spectrum contains the carrier frequency plus an infinite series of sidebands located at $f_c \pm n \cdot f_m$, where $n$ ranges over all integers. The amplitude of each sideband is governed by Bessel functions of the first kind, $J_n(I)$, which determine how energy distributes across the harmonic and inharmonic partials. As modulation index increases, energy spreads into higher-order sidebands, producing brighter, more complex timbres with metallic or bell-like qualities.

The ratio between carrier and modulator frequencies determines whether the resulting spectrum will be harmonic or inharmonic. When $f_c : f_m$ forms a simple integer ratio like 1:1, 2:1, or 3:2, the sidebands align with the harmonic series, producing musical, pitched tones. A 1:1 ratio yields a spectrum resembling a sawtooth wave with odd and even harmonics. A 2:1 ratio emphasizes even harmonics, creating a hollow, clarinet-like timbre. Irrational or complex ratios such as 1:1.414 or 7:5 generate inharmonic spectra—sidebands fall between the harmonic series, producing bell-like, gong-like, or metallic timbres that evoke struck metal or tuned percussion.

The concept of operators extends this two-oscillator model into multi-stage FM algorithms. Each operator is a complete sine wave oscillator with independent frequency, envelope generator, and output level control. Operators can be configured as carriers (producing audible output) or modulators (controlling other operators' frequencies). The DX7 architecture provided six operators that could be interconnected in 32 different algorithm topologies, ranging from simple two-operator stacks to complex parallel and feedback configurations. A serial algorithm chains operators linearly: operator 6 modulates operator 5, which modulates operator 4, and so forth, producing deep, evolving timbres with rich transient behavior. Parallel algorithms split operators into independent carrier-modulator pairs, creating layered sounds with distinct timbral regions. Feedback routing allows an operator to modulate its own frequency, introducing chaotic nonlinearities that produce noise-like textures or raspy, distorted tones.

The envelope generator applied to each operator's output level represents the critical temporal dimension of FM synthesis. Unlike subtractive synthesis where a single filter envelope shapes timbral evolution, FM synthesis achieves spectral animation through differential envelope timing across operators. A classic electric piano patch employs two operators in a simple 1:1 ratio, but the modulator envelope decays rapidly while the carrier envelope sustains. During the attack phase, high modulation index produces a bright, percussive transient rich in upper harmonics. As the modulator envelope decays, the modulation index decreases, collapsing the spectrum toward the fundamental and producing the characteristic mellowing of an electric piano note. This time-variant modulation index creates dynamic timbral evolution without requiring filters, resonance, or subtractive processing—the spectral content is generated algorithmically through controlled oscillator interaction.

## 2. LFO Architecture and Modulation Routing

The internal architecture of a low-frequency oscillator mirrors that of an audio-rate oscillator but operates under different performance constraints and design priorities. At the core sits a phase accumulator that increments at the LFO frequency, wrapping from $2\pi$ back to zero at the end of each cycle. This phase value feeds into waveform shaping functions that convert the linear phase ramp into the desired output shape. A sine LFO applies $\sin(\phi)$ to produce smooth, symmetrical modulation. A triangle LFO uses a piecewise linear function that rises from $-1$ to $+1$ during the first half-cycle and falls back to $-1$ during the second half. Square waves threshold the phase at $\pi$, outputting $+1$ for $\phi < \pi$ and $-1$ otherwise. Sawtooth waves map phase directly to output, creating a rising ramp from $-1$ to $+1$ before hard resetting.

Sample-and-hold LFOs introduce randomness into the modulation signal by generating a new random value at each cycle boundary and holding that value constant until the next trigger. The update rate determines whether the effect sounds smooth (slow rates produce gradual drift) or steppy (fast rates create rhythmic, quantized pitch or filter changes). Many synthesizers provide a slew or glide parameter that smooths the transitions between random values, interpolating linearly or exponentially to reduce the harshness of instantaneous jumps. This produces a random modulation signal that retains continuity, useful for simulating analog voltage noise or creating evolving, non-repetitive textures.

Phase relationships between multiple LFOs create polyrhythmic and polymetric modulation patterns. Two LFOs running at 1 Hz and 1.5 Hz will cycle in and out of phase alignment, producing complex beating patterns that repeat every six seconds (the least common multiple of their periods). Free-running LFOs maintain independent phase, starting whenever a note triggers and drifting relative to the global tempo. Tempo-synced LFOs lock to the host sequencer or internal clock, quantizing their frequency to musical divisions like 1/4 note, 1/8 note, or 1/16 note. This synchronization ensures that modulation effects align with rhythmic structure, critical for creating coherent, groove-locked patches in electronic music production.

Modulation depth and polarity controls shape how an LFO affects its destination parameter. Depth scales the LFO amplitude before applying it to the target, ranging from subtle vibrato (depth = 0.05 semitones) to extreme pitch swoops (depth = 12 semitones). Polarity determines whether the LFO both raises and lowers the parameter value (bipolar modulation, ranging from $-1$ to $+1$) or only increases it (unipolar modulation, ranging from $0$ to $+1$). Bipolar LFOs work well for pitch and pan modulation where symmetrical deviation feels natural. Unipolar LFOs suit filter cutoff and amplitude modulation where negative values would produce silence or closed filters.

The modulation matrix represents the routing architecture that connects sources to destinations. In a hardware modular synthesizer, this takes the form of patch cables connecting output jacks to input jacks. In software synthesizers, it manifests as a routing grid where rows represent sources (LFO1, LFO2, envelope followers, MIDI controllers) and columns represent destinations (oscillator pitch, filter cutoff, pulse width, pan position). Each intersection contains a depth control that scales the modulation amount. Advanced matrices support multiple sources modulating a single destination with independent depth controls, enabling complex additive modulation schemes. Some architectures allow sources to modulate modulation depths—an envelope controlling the intensity of LFO vibrato, for example, causing vibrato to increase over the duration of a note.

## 3. Frequency Modulation Synthesis: Mathematical Foundations

The time-domain representation of a frequency-modulated signal begins with a carrier sine wave:

$$x_c(t) = A_c \sin(2\pi f_c t)$$

When a modulator signal $x_m(t) = A_m \sin(2\pi f_m t)$ modulates the instantaneous frequency of the carrier, the phase of the carrier becomes time-variant:

$$x_{\text{FM}}(t) = A_c \sin\left(2\pi f_c t + I \sin(2\pi f_m t)\right)$$

The modulation index $I = \Delta f / f_m$ captures the ratio between the peak frequency deviation $\Delta f$ and the modulating frequency. This single parameter controls the spectral richness of the output. At $I = 0$, no modulation occurs and we hear only the carrier frequency. At $I = 1$, significant sideband energy appears at $f_c \pm f_m$. At $I = 5$, the spectrum broadens dramatically, with energy distributed across ten or more sideband pairs.

The frequency-domain analysis reveals the sideband structure through the Fourier expansion of the modulated signal. Applying the Jacobi-Anger expansion to the FM equation yields:

$$x_{\text{FM}}(t) = A_c \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} J_n(I) \sin\left(2\pi (f_c + n f_m) t\right)$$

Each term in this infinite sum represents a sideband at frequency $f_c + n f_m$ with amplitude $A_c J_n(I)$, where $J_n$ denotes the $n$-th order Bessel function of the first kind. Bessel functions oscillate and decay as $|n|$ increases, meaning that higher-order sidebands carry progressively less energy. For small modulation indices ($I < 1$), only the first few sidebands contain significant energy. For large indices ($I > 5$), dozens of sidebands contribute to the spectrum, producing dense, complex timbres.

The symmetry properties of Bessel functions reveal critical features of FM spectra. For integer $n$, $J_{-n}(I) = (-1)^n J_n(I)$, meaning that sidebands at $f_c + n f_m$ and $f_c - n f_m$ have equal magnitude but may differ in phase. When negative sidebands fall below 0 Hz, they fold back into the positive frequency domain as mirror images, a phenomenon called aliasing in digital implementations or spectral folding in analog contexts. This folding introduces inharmonic partials even when $f_c : f_m$ ratios would otherwise produce harmonic spectra, particularly at high modulation indices.

The harmonic-to-inharmonic transition depends entirely on the $f_c : f_m$ ratio. Consider a carrier at 440 Hz and a modulator at 440 Hz (ratio 1:1). The sidebands appear at $440 + n \cdot 440 = 440(1 + n)$, producing frequencies $0, 440, 880, 1320, 1760, \ldots$—a perfect harmonic series. Now consider a carrier at 440 Hz and a modulator at 311.1 Hz (ratio 1.414:1, or $\sqrt{2}:1$). The sidebands appear at $440 + n \cdot 311.1$, yielding $128.9, 440, 751.1, 1062.2, 1373.3, \ldots$—no longer integer multiples of a fundamental, producing the inharmonic, bell-like timbre characteristic of FM bells and metallic percussion.

Modulator-to-carrier ratios expressed as simple fractions $p:q$ generate spectra where every $q$-th partial aligns with the harmonic series of the fundamental $f_c / q$. A 3:2 ratio produces a spectrum with strong partials at $f_c, 2f_c, 4f_c, 5f_c, 7f_c, \ldots$, emphasizing odd harmonics with a hollow, woody character. A 5:4 ratio yields a complex mix of even and odd harmonics with a reedy, oboe-like quality. By selecting ratios carefully, FM synthesis can approximate the harmonic structures of acoustic instruments, from brass and woodwinds to struck idiophones.

## 4. FM Operator Algorithms and Feedback Topologies

The transition from two-operator FM to multi-operator algorithms unlocks exponential timbral complexity. Each additional operator multiplies the number of possible sideband interactions, creating spectra that would be intractable to analyze mathematically but remain musically coherent when designed with care. The DX7's 32 algorithms represent a curated subset of the thousands of possible six-operator topologies, chosen to balance timbral diversity with user comprehension.

Algorithm 1, the simplest topology, arranges six operators in a single serial chain: OP6 → OP5 → OP4 → OP3 → OP2 → OP1, where OP1 serves as the carrier and outputs to the audio path. Each upstream operator modulates the frequency of the next operator in the chain, producing a cascade of nested frequency modulations. The resulting spectrum exhibits extreme complexity, with hundreds of sidebands spread across the audible range. This topology excels at evolving pads, metallic textures, and synthetic timbres that defy traditional harmonic analysis. The challenge lies in controlling the spectral density—without careful envelope and level tuning, serial algorithms produce harsh, noisy tones that lack musical focus.

Algorithm 5 presents a parallel configuration: six independent carriers, each receiving no modulation. This topology functions as a simple additive synthesizer, summing six sine waves at independent frequencies and amplitude envelopes. While harmonically limited compared to modulated algorithms, parallel carriers provide precise control over spectral content, useful for organ-like tones, electric pianos with distinct tine and tone bar components, or additive resynthesis of analyzed sounds.

Algorithm 32 employs a hybrid structure: two parallel carrier-modulator pairs (OP1 modulated by OP2, OP3 modulated by OP4) plus two independent carriers (OP5, OP6). This configuration balances complexity and clarity, allowing two independent timbres with distinct harmonic characters to blend while retaining additional pure tones for brightness or sub-bass reinforcement. It represents one of the most versatile architectures for bread-and-butter sounds—electric pianos, plucked basses, bells, and mallets.

Feedback introduces nonlinearity into FM synthesis by routing an operator's output back to its own frequency input. Mathematically, this creates a recursive equation:

$$x_{\text{FB}}(t) = A \sin\left(2\pi f t + \beta \cdot x_{\text{FB}}(t - \Delta t)\right)$$

where $\beta$ controls feedback intensity and $\Delta t$ represents a one-sample delay required to prevent infinite recursion. At low feedback levels ($\beta < 0.5$), the effect resembles increased harmonic richness, adding subtle upper partials and warmth. At moderate levels ($\beta \approx 1$), the spectrum becomes dense and saturated, producing sawtooth-like waveforms with strong odd and even harmonics. At high levels ($\beta > 2$), the operator enters chaotic behavior, generating noise-like or distorted timbres useful for aggressive basses, screaming leads, and sound effects.

The DX7 feedback path operates at the operator level, allowing any operator to modulate itself regardless of its position in the algorithm. This creates fascinating interactions: an operator receiving external modulation and self-modulation simultaneously produces spectra that depend on the phase relationship between the incoming modulation signal and the delayed feedback signal. Rapidly changing modulation indices from external sources interact with the feedback loop's memory, creating transient-rich, unpredictable timbres that feel alive and responsive.

## 5. Envelope Generators in FM Synthesis

The envelope generator in FM synthesis serves a dual role: it controls the amplitude of an operator's output (as in subtractive synthesis) and, when applied to a modulating operator, it dynamically adjusts the modulation index over time. This second function enables the spectral animation that distinguishes FM synthesis from static additive approaches.

The DX7 envelope generator employs a four-stage design: attack, decay, sustain, release, but with critical differences from the traditional ADSR model. Each stage is defined by a rate parameter (controlling the time to reach the next breakpoint) and a level parameter (setting the target amplitude). This rate-level architecture provides greater flexibility than time-based envelopes, allowing decay and release slopes to vary based on the initial and target levels rather than following fixed time constants.

Consider a two-operator electric piano patch with a 1:1 carrier-to-modulator ratio. The carrier envelope employs a fast attack (rate = 99), short decay to a moderate sustain level (rate = 80, level = 60), and medium release (rate = 40). The modulator envelope uses the same attack rate but a much faster decay to zero (rate = 95, level = 0) with no sustain. During the attack phase, both operators output at maximum level, producing a modulation index around $I = 5$. The spectrum contains dense sidebands, creating a bright, percussive transient. As the modulator decays rapidly, the modulation index collapses toward zero, narrowing the spectrum to the carrier fundamental and low-order harmonics. The carrier sustains at a moderate level, providing a mellow, sustained tone. When the key releases, the carrier decays smoothly, avoiding abrupt cutoffs.

This differential envelope timing creates the perceptual impression of a lowpass filter sweep, but without using any filters. The modulation index acts as a brightness control—high index yields complex spectra, low index yields simple sine-like tones. By modulating the modulation index with an envelope, FM synthesis achieves time-variant timbral evolution that rivals subtractive synthesis for expressiveness.

Velocity sensitivity in FM synthesis operates differently than in subtractive contexts. Rather than scaling a single filter cutoff or amplitude, velocity can independently control the output levels of multiple operators, the envelope rates, or both. A common technique assigns velocity to modulator level: soft key presses produce low modulation indices and mellow tones, while hard strikes generate high indices and bright, aggressive attacks. This creates a dynamic response curve that feels natural and responsive, mimicking the harder-hit-equals-brighter-tone relationship of acoustic instruments.

Scaling envelopes across operators allows complex, multi-stage timbral evolution. A four-operator brass patch might employ a carrier with a slow attack and long sustain, a first modulator with a fast attack and medium decay, a second modulator with a medium attack and long sustain, and a third modulator with a slow attack that gradually increases. During the initial attack, the first modulator dominates, producing a bright, punchy transient. As it decays, the second modulator takes over, sustaining a warm, reedy midrange. Finally, the third modulator fades in, adding upper harmonics for brilliance and air. The result is a brass sound that evolves through distinct timbral regions over several seconds, far more complex than a single envelope could produce.

## 6. Modulation Strategies and Routing Paradigms

The architecture of modulation routing determines the flexibility and workflow efficiency of a synthesizer. Fixed-architecture instruments like the DX7 provide predetermined algorithm topologies with no user-configurable modulation routing beyond operator levels and ratios. This limitation forces users to think in terms of selecting the right algorithm for a desired sound rather than constructing arbitrary signal flow graphs. While restrictive, fixed architectures ensure that every configuration produces musically useful results—the curated algorithm set eliminates pathological topologies that generate silence, extreme aliasing, or unstable feedback.

Modular synthesizers and modular software environments like VCV Rack or Reaktor adopt the opposite philosophy: every parameter is a potential modulation destination, and any signal can serve as a modulation source. A modulation matrix with 20 sources and 100 destinations yields 2,000 possible routings, an overwhelming design space for beginners but a playground for advanced users. The matrix-based paradigm excels at creating polyrhythmic modulation schemes, chaotic feedback networks, and emergent behaviors where multiple LFOs and envelopes interact in unpredictable ways.

Modulation depth scaling introduces a critical control: the amount by which a modulation source affects a destination. A filter cutoff might have a base value of 1000 Hz, with an LFO modulation depth of ±200 Hz, yielding a sweep range from 800 Hz to 1200 Hz. Depth parameters allow precise control over modulation intensity without altering the source waveform's amplitude. In hardware terms, depth acts as a voltage attenuator, scaling the LFO output before summing it with the destination parameter's base value.

Modulation of modulation unlocks recursive control schemes. An envelope generator controlling the depth of an LFO creates time-variant vibrato: notes start with no vibrato, gradually increasing as the envelope rises, and fading out as the envelope decays. A second LFO modulating the frequency of a first LFO produces complex polyrhythmic patterns that cycle through long, non-repeating phrases. A velocity-controlled modulation depth allows performance dynamics to shape not just amplitude but timbral evolution, brightness, and filter resonance, creating instruments that respond organically to playing technique.

Cross-modulation between oscillators extends beyond traditional FM paradigms. Ring modulation multiplies two audio signals, producing sum and difference frequencies: $f_1 \pm f_2$. Unlike FM, which generates infinite sideband series, ring modulation produces only two sidebands per carrier-modulator pair, creating hollow, metallic timbres useful for bells, gongs, and robotic vocal effects. Amplitude modulation (AM) multiplies a carrier by a unipolar modulator (ranging from 0 to 1 rather than -1 to +1), producing sidebands at $f_c \pm f_m$ with different amplitude envelopes than FM sidebands. AM sidebands decay more gracefully than FM sidebands, producing warmer, less aggressive timbres.

Hard sync forces one oscillator to reset its phase whenever another oscillator completes a cycle. This creates a spectrum with harmonics at integer multiples of the sync master frequency, regardless of the slave oscillator's frequency. When the slave frequency is detuned from the master, sync introduces sharp discontinuities that add high-frequency harmonics, producing the characteristic buzzy, aggressive timbres of classic analog leads. Modulating the slave frequency with an LFO or envelope creates dynamic sync sweeps, a signature sound of 1980s synthpop and techno.

## 7. Practical FM Patch Design

Building an FM patch from first principles requires understanding the relationship between operator ratios, envelopes, and modulation indices. Begin with a two-operator configuration—the simplest topology that produces genuine FM timbres. Set the carrier frequency to the desired fundamental pitch and the modulator frequency to a simple ratio: 1:1 for harmonic sawtooth-like tones, 2:1 for hollow clarinets, 3:2 for woody reeds, 7:5 for bell-like inharmonicity.

Adjust the modulator output level to control the modulation index. Start with a low level (around 30% of maximum) to hear the transition from a pure sine carrier to a spectrum with a few prominent sidebands. Gradually increase the level to 70% and observe the spectral broadening—more sidebands emerge, the tone becomes brighter and more complex. Push the level to 100% for extreme, metallic timbres with dense sideband clusters.

Apply a fast attack, medium decay envelope to the modulator while giving the carrier a slower attack and longer decay. Play a note and listen for the characteristic brightness-to-mellow evolution. The attack phase should produce a percussive transient rich in upper harmonics, while the sustain phase settles into a warm, fundamental-focused tone. This differential envelope timing is the signature of expressive FM instruments.

Experiment with feedback on the carrier operator. At low feedback levels, the tone gains warmth and saturation without becoming harsh. At high feedback levels, the spectrum collapses into noise or distortion, useful for aggressive basses and lead synths. Combine feedback with external modulation from a second operator to create hybrid timbres with both harmonic richness and chaotic noise components.

For multi-operator patches, start with a clear architectural intent. Parallel carriers (additive) provide spectral precision but limited complexity. Serial chains (cascaded FM) produce dense, evolving textures but require careful envelope tuning to avoid harshness. Hybrid algorithms balance both approaches, offering independent timbral layers that can be mixed to taste.

Velocity mapping extends dynamic expression. Assign velocity to modulator levels so soft notes produce mellow tones and hard strikes yield bright attacks. Assign velocity to envelope rates so forceful playing produces faster transients and longer decays. Assign velocity to feedback depth for playing-style-dependent timbral variation. These mappings create instruments that feel responsive and alive, bridging the gap between digital precision and acoustic expressiveness.

---

The interplay of LFOs, FM operators, and envelope generators defines modern synthesis architecture. Modulation transforms static waveforms into dynamic, evolving timbres that capture the expressive nuance of acoustic instruments while enabling entirely new sonic territory. Frequency modulation, despite its mathematical complexity, produces spectra of astonishing richness from minimal computational resources—a sine wave modulating a sine wave can generate timbres that would require hundreds of partials in additive synthesis or dozens of filters in subtractive approaches. Mastery of modulation architecture unlocks the full expressive potential of synthesis, enabling instruments that respond organically to performance while maintaining the precision and repeatability of digital control.
