# Studio Practice Labs: Visual Composition and Harmonic Framing

## Objective & Studio Setup

Visual composition is the deliberate orchestration of spatial elements—line, shape, color, value, and texture—to create coherence, hierarchy, and meaning in a two- or three-dimensional frame. Harmonic framing refers to the deliberate agreement of these elements within the composition: shared proportions, related angles, repeated intervals and a coherent palette. Elements that clash create visual unrest and tension; elements that agree create resolution and calm.

This lab sequence teaches you to observe compositional relationships in real work, then to execute them through repeated technical drill and finished studies. You will work across media—drawing, painting, digital imagery, and optional 3D or video—to internalize how spatial ordering, visual weight, focal point placement, and value relationships generate compositional unity.

### Studio Setup Checklist

**Traditional Media (all exercises adaptable):**
- Sketchbook or paper pads: 9×12 inches (230×305 mm) for quick studies; 11×14 or 16×20 inches (280×356 or 406×508 mm) for finished work.
- Drawing media: 2H, HB, 2B, 4B graphite pencils; vine and compressed charcoal; kneaded eraser; stumps (blending tools).
- Painting media: acrylic paint (titanium white, ivory black, burnt sienna, ultramarine blue, cadmium yellow, alizarin crimson), synthetic bristle brushes (sizes 2, 6, 10 flat; size 6 round), palette (disposable or ceramic), water cup, paper towels.
- Reference setup: printed or digital reference images (high-resolution); tablet or monitor at arm's height.
- Diagnostic tools: value greyscale card (Munsell or DIY 5-step gray card), mirror or phone camera for instant flip checks.

**Digital Workflow (Krita or equivalent open-source; GIMP and Blender optional for extended work):**
- Monitor: 1920×1080 minimum (27 inches preferred for precision); calibrated or consistently lit workspace.

## Exercise 1: Observation & Fundamentals Drill

### Part A: Value Mapping & Spatial Hierarchy (Graphite Drawing)

Before manipulating composition, train your eye to perceive value relationships and spatial depth cues. This drill builds the foundation for understanding visual weight distribution and focal emphasis.

**Objective:** Extract the essential value structure of a complex image; build a gestural hierarchy map.

**Duration:** 90 minutes (three 30-minute passes).

**Setup:**
1. Select a master reference image with clear foreground, middle ground, and background (landscape, interior, still life, or figurative work). Print or display at 8×10 inches on-screen.
2. Obtain a 9×12-inch sketchbook page and 2B, 4B graphite pencils, kneaded eraser.
3. Measure your reference on-screen or print. Use a ruler to divide your sketchbook into a matching proportional grid (e.g., if reference is 8×10, your 9×12 page will accept an 8×10 grid with 1-inch margins).
4. Lightly draw grid lines in both images with a 2H pencil (barely visible after erasing).

**Pass 1: Value Blocking (10 minutes)**
Using your 4B pencil and broad side strokes, establish five distinct value zones: pure white (paper), light gray (20% tone), mid-gray (50% tone), dark gray (80% tone), and black (100% tone). Do not render detail. Squint at your reference to suppress fine texture and see only large masses. Block these zones with hatching or side-stroke shading. Use the kneaded eraser to lift highlights and correct overshading. Your goal is a legible five-value silhouette—a visual skeleton of spatial depth.

**Pass 2: Spatial Depth Reinforcement (10 minutes)**
Introduce subtle mid-tone transitions within zones to suggest recession. Apply 2B pencil to atmospheric perspective areas (increase value saturation toward foreground; reduce toward background). Add light hatching to refine the boundary between adjacent value zones. Do not cross into photorealism; maintain the abstracted block structure. Use a value greyscale card or printed 5-step gray reference to calibrate your pencil strokes against known tone intervals.

**Pass 3: Focal Emphasis (10 minutes)**
Identify the brightest highlight and deepest shadow in your reference. Exaggerate this value range in your drawing by pushing darks darker (apply multiple 4B passes; optionally layer with black ink pen for maximum depth) and preserving the lightest white of your paper at key highlights. This creates visual "pull"—your eye naturally returns to maximum contrast zones. Add a final layer of 2B hatching to unify mid-tones and reduce harsh transitions. Erase grid lines fully and step back 3–4 feet to verify that value masses read as cohesive spatial layers.

**Self-Check:**
- Does your drawing read as three distinct spatial zones (foreground, middle, background) even when viewed as a 2×3-inch thumbnail?
- Is the brightest white concentrated in one focal area, not scattered across the page?
- Are your five value steps visibly distinct, or do any adjacent tones merge?

**Diagnostic Procedure (Value Greyscale Check):**
Hold a printed or digital 5-step Munsell greyscale card (or DIY chart: white, light gray, 50% gray, dark gray, black) next to corresponding zones in your drawing. If your mid-tones drift into light-gray territory where they should anchor at 50%, re-establish them with a fresh 2B layer. This drill trains your eye to perceive absolute value rather than relative contrast within your composition.

### Part B: Spatial Ordering—Grid & Line Analysis (Digital, Krita)

Harmonic visual relationships emerge from the spatial grid beneath a composition. This drill teaches you to perceive and construct the geometric ordering system that holds elements in relationship.

**Objective:** Map the underlying linear and proportional armature of a master composition; recreate it as a reusable structural template.

**Duration:** 60 minutes.

**Setup:**
1. Open Krita. Create a new document: 1280×960 pixels, 300 DPI (for print-quality output), RGB 8-bit. Use File > New.
2. Import your master reference as a new layer: File > Open as Layers. Select a composition with strong architectural or figurative structure (e.g., a landscape by Cézanne, a portrait by Ingres, or a still life by Morandi). Position the reference at 50% opacity on Layer 1.
3. Create a new vector layer (Vector > New Vector Layer) for drawing guides and grid lines.

**Step 1: Rule of Thirds Grid (5 minutes)**
On your vector layer, construct a Rule of Thirds grid. The Rule of Thirds divides your frame into nine equal rectangles (three rows × three columns), and places key focal elements at the intersection points or along the lines themselves. Mathematically, if your canvas width is W and height is H, the vertical lines occur at x = W/3 and x = 2W/3; horizontal lines at y = H/3 and y = 2H/3.

Using Krita's Line tool (Tools > Paint Tools > Line), draw vertical and horizontal guides:
- Select a bright guide color (cyan or magenta, 100% opacity) so it contrasts with your reference.
- Draw the first vertical line at exactly (1280 × 1)/3 = 426 pixels from the left edge. Use Krita's measurement ruler (View > Show Ruler) to verify pixel positions.
- Draw the second vertical at (1280 × 2)/3 = 853 pixels.
- Draw horizontal lines at (960 × 1)/3 = 320 pixels and (960 × 2)/3 = 640 pixels from the top.

You now have nine equal cells. Mark the four intersection points (426, 320), (853, 320), (426, 640), (853, 640) with a small circle or crosshair.

**Step 2: Primary Focal Area Identification (10 minutes)**
Examine your reference. Identify the dominant focal point (eye contact in a portrait, brightest highlight in a landscape, or the primary object in a still life). Overlay a semi-transparent circle or bounding box at this location. If the focal point sits exactly at a Rule of Thirds intersection, note this. If it deviates, measure the offset: does it sit 50 pixels left of center? 80 pixels above? Record this relationship numerically (e.g., "focal center at [+45, −35] pixels from primary intersection").

**Step 3: Proportional Harmonic Divisions (15 minutes)**
Beyond Rule of Thirds, examine secondary spatial divisions. Many classical compositions use the Golden Ratio (φ ≈ 1.618) to proportion recurring intervals. If your canvas width is W, the Golden Division occurs at approximately 0.618W (and 0.382W creates a complementary division).

For a 1280-pixel width: 0.618 × 1280 ≈ 791 pixels; 0.382 × 1280 ≈ 489 pixels. Draw these secondary vertical lines on your vector layer in a contrasting color (e.g., yellow if you used cyan for Rule of Thirds).

Examine your reference again. Do secondary figures, background edges, or compositional subdivisions align with these Golden Division lines? If yes, record the layer name and coordinates. This reveals the deep harmonic structure of the composition—the invisible skeleton that generates visual consonance.

**Step 4: Gestural Line Mapping (20 minutes)**
Using the Brush tool (a soft round brush, size 15, opacity 60%), draw thin gesture lines that connect focal points, guide the eye through the composition, and reveal the flow of visual energy. These lines are not visible in the final image; they reveal compositional *intention*. For example, in a landscape, draw a curved line that traces the horizon, then follows a path of receding trees toward a vanishing point. In a portrait, draw lines along the sitter's eye-line, shoulders, and spine to reveal postural rhythm.

Layers > Merge Down your gesture layer with your grid layer to create a unified "compositional armature" document. Save this as "Composition_Armature_[Reference_Title].kra" (Krita format).

**Self-Check:**
- Are your Rule of Thirds lines perfectly perpendicular to canvas edges (90-degree angles)?
- Do any focal points, edges, or lines in your reference align with marked grid intersections or Golden Division coordinates?
- Does your gesture line map create a coherent visual path, or do lines zigzag without direction?

**Output Artifact:**
Export your armature as a 300 DPI PNG: File > Export As > "[Reference_Title]_Armature.png". Print this 8×10 inches and store in your exercise portfolio. You now have a reusable spatial template for Exercise 2.

## Exercise 2: Technique Study, Step by Step

### Part A: Modular Value Composition (Acrylic Painting on Paper)

Having internalized spatial grids and value relationships through observation, you now execute a finished study that deliberately applies harmonic framing principles. This exercise builds visual weight and focal emphasis through controlled value relationships.

**Objective:** Paint a non-representational composition using restricted value range (three distinct tones plus white and black) that generates clear focal hierarchy and spatial depth through value alone.

**Duration:** 120 minutes (across two 60-minute sessions, with drying time between).

**Materials:**
- Acrylic paint: titanium white, ivory black, burnt sienna.
- Brushes: size 6 flat (basecoat), size 2 flat (refinement), size 6 round (details).
- Paper: 11×14-inch 140 lb cold-pressed watercolor or drawing paper.
- Palette: ceramic or disposable with a water cup.
- Paper towels, mixing palette knife.

**Concept Rationale:**
A modular value composition uses rectangular shapes of different sizes, arranged on a grid, to explore spatial relationships without the distraction of representational subject matter. This isolates the variables of value, proportion, and placement—the core mechanics of harmonic framing. Each rectangle represents a "spatial module" with its own value tone. The intervals between modules, the proportions of modules, and the value differences between adjacent modules generate visual consonance or tension.

**Preparatory Step (10 minutes, Day 1):**
On your 11×14-inch paper, lightly draw a grid using a 2H pencil: divide the paper into four columns and four rows (a 4×4 matrix). Each cell is approximately 2.75×3.5 inches. This grid is your structural armature; it prevents modules from drifting across the page and ensures measured, harmonic distribution.

**Step 1: Establish Your Value Palette (5 minutes, Day 1):**
On your mixing palette, create three acrylic color mixtures:
- Light Value: Mix 1 part burnt sienna + 3 parts titanium white. Test on a scrap of paper. This should read as a tan or warm light gray (approximately 70% brightness).
- Mid Value: Mix 2 parts burnt sienna + 2 parts titanium white + 1 part ivory black. This yields a warm mid-brown, approximately 50% brightness.
- Dark Value: Mix 3 parts burnt sienna + 1 part ivory black. This yields a deep warm brown, approximately 25% brightness.

Reserve pure titanium white and pure ivory black from the tube for highlights and deepest accents. Test each mixture against your Munsell greyscale card to verify absolute brightness levels. If your light value reads too bright (beyond 70%), add a touch of mid-value to darken. If your mid-value is too dark (below 50%), lighten with white.

**Step 2: Block Base Values (20 minutes, Day 1):**
Using your size 6 flat brush, paint rectangular modules across the 4×4 grid. You will fill approximately 10–12 of the 16 cells. Leave 4–6 cells unpainted (white paper shows through). Your placement strategy is critical:

- Place your single darkest value (pure ivory black or dark value from above) at a Rule of Thirds intersection or slightly off-center. This anchors visual focus.
- Distribute light and mid values around the composition to balance mass. Do not cluster all light values in one corner.
- Leave white paper cells strategically: typically one or two white cells near your focal point to amplify contrast, and one white "breathing room" cell away from the main cluster to prevent visual congestion.

Work in broad strokes; allow paint to dry between layers. Your goal is a legible arrangement of four value zones (white, light, mid, dark) that reads as spatially coherent even when squinted at.

**Step 3: Refine Edges & Intervals (15 minutes, Day 1):**
Once base layers are dry, use your size 2 flat brush to refine module edges. Introduce soft edges (by dragging your brush across a boundary line with minimal paint) in some locations and hard edges (clean, defined lines) in others. Soft edges suggest spatial recession or gentle transitions; hard edges create visual separations and focal emphasis.

Paint a soft edge between adjacent mid and light values (representing middle-ground depth). Paint a hard edge between your dark focal module and an adjacent light module (creating maximum contrast and visual pull). Leave other edges in their natural basecoat state.

**Step 4: Accent & Finalize (10 minutes, Day 2, after drying overnight):**
Using undiluted titanium white and your size 2 round brush, add one or two thin white lines or small rectangular accents on the boundary of your focal (dark) module. These create a highlight shimmer and emphasize focal area. Do not overload; two to three accents maximum. 

Step back 4–5 feet and assess. Your composition should now read as three to four distinct value zones with clear spatial recession and a visually dominant dark focal point. The white paper cells should create visual "breathing room" and prevent visual fatigue.

**Documentation:**
Photograph your finished painting under consistent studio lighting (north-facing daylight or neutral LED). Save the image at minimum 1920×1440 pixels (8×10 inches at 240 DPI). Label the file "Value_Study_[Date]_v1.jpg".

**Self-Check Against Critique Rubric (covered below):**
- Does your focal point (dark module) sit at or near a Rule of Thirds intersection?
- Are the three value zones visibly distinct (not muddy or intermediate)?
- Is at least one edge visibly softer than others, suggesting spatial recession?
- Do white paper cells comprise approximately 25–30% of total composition area?

### Part B: Color Harmony & Chromatic Modulation (Krita Digital Painting)

Building on Exercise 2A's value structure, this study layers color relationships across a compositional framework. Harmonic framing extends beyond value to color: analogous colors (adjacent on the color wheel) create visual consonance; complementary or triadic colors create tension. This drill teaches you to orchestrate color relationships to reinforce compositional hierarchy.

**Objective:** Develop a digital painting using analogous and complementary color pairs to amplify focal emphasis and spatial depth established through value.

**Duration:** 90 minutes.

**Setup:**
1. Open Krita. Create a new document: 1280×960 pixels, RGB 8-bit, 72 DPI (screen resolution).
2. Import your Exercise 2A photograph (Value_Study_[Date]_v1.jpg) as a reference layer at 30% opacity: File > Open as Layers.
3. Create a new Raster layer for painting. Lock the reference layer to prevent accidental edits.

**Color Harmony Theory (Application, Not Abstraction):**
On a standard 12-step color wheel (12 o'clock = red, 3 o'clock = yellow, 6 o'clock = cyan, 9 o'clock = magenta), analogous colors sit 30–60 degrees apart (e.g., red–red-orange–orange). They appear visually harmonious and stable because adjacent hues share common pigment components. Complementary colors sit 180 degrees apart (e.g., red and cyan). They vibrate against each other and create maximum visual tension. In a composition, using a dominant analogous palette (e.g., warm reds and oranges) with a small complementary accent (e.g., a cyan highlight) generates consonance with intentional focal disruption.

**Step 1: Select Your Color Palette (10 minutes):**
Create a custom palette in Krita reflecting an analogous + complementary color scheme:

- Analogous Base (choose one): 
  - Warm: deep red (HSV: 0°, 100%, 70%), orange-red (30°, 100%, 80%), pale orange (40°, 50%, 90%).
  - Cool: deep cyan (180°, 100%, 70%), blue-cyan (200°, 100%, 80%), pale blue (220°, 50%, 90%).
- Complementary Accent: If you choose the warm analogous base, your complementary accent is a deep cyan or blue-green (160–200°). Add one accent color at full saturation and 50% brightness.

In Krita, create a custom palette: Windows > Dockable Dialogs > Palette Editor. Create a new palette and add your chosen colors as hex codes or by sampling with the color picker (right-click canvas, pick color).

**Step 2: Underpaint with Analogous Base (20 minutes):**
Using a soft round brush (size 30, opacity 70%), block in large areas using your analogous palette. If you chose warm tones, paint the focal area (corresponding to your Exercise 2A dark module) in deep red. Extend warm orange-red around the focal point, and pale orange in distant/edge areas. Build form gradually; allow colors to blend slightly where they overlap. Do not aim for photorealism; seek visual resonance through hue relationships.

**Step 3: Introduce Complementary Accent (15 minutes):**
Add your complementary accent color (e.g., cyan) in precisely one location: a small area adjacent to or overlapping your focal point. If your focal area is red, add a thin cyan line or small shape at its edge. This creates visual tension and reinforces focal emphasis by color contrast (in addition to value contrast from Exercise 2A).

Reduce the accent to 20–30% of the focal area's visible surface; too much complementary color de-emphasizes your focal point. The accent should read as a visual "pop" or highlight, not as a competing focal area.

**Step 4: Harmonize with Soft Transitions (20 minutes):**
Using a soft round brush at reduced opacity (40–50%), blend boundaries between warm and cool zones. Add semi-transparent layers of intermediate hues (e.g., yellow-orange bridges between red and pale orange; blue-green bridges between cyan and pale blue) to soften color transitions and prevent jarring shifts. This reinforces visual harmony by reducing edge harshness.

Add white highlights (pure white, 100% opacity) to areas where complementary accent meets focal point, amplifying visual contrast.

**Step 5: Final Saturation & Value Check (15 minutes):**
Flatten the image (Image > Flatten Image) and immediately open the Histogram panel (Windows > Dockable Dialogs > Histogram) to verify value distribution. Your histogram should show:
- A spike at the dark end (100% saturation in blacks/deep reds from your focal area).
- A secondary peak at mid-values (orange-red and analogous colors).
- A tail at the bright end (pale oranges and whites, approximately 15–20% of pixel density).

If your histogram is centered (too much mid-tone, not enough contrast), apply a Curves adjustment to darken your dark values and brighten whites.

**Diagnostic: Color Picker & Contrast Check**
Use the color picker (Tools > Color Picker) to sample three points: focal area, analogous base area, and complementary accent. For each, note the HSV values (Hue, Saturation, Value). Your focal area should have maximum saturation (100%) and reduced value (50–60%). Your analogous areas should show 60–80% saturation. Your complementary accent should have 100% saturation and 40–50% value—visibly distinct but not brighter than focal point.

**Export & Document:**
Save as "Color_Harmony_Study_[Date].kra". Export as 300 DPI PNG: File > Export As > "Color_Harmony_Study_[Date].png". Resize to 1920×1440 pixels and save a print-ready version.

**Self-Check:**
- Are your three color zones (deep analogous focal, light analogous mid-ground, complementary accent) visibly distinct?
- Does the complementary accent occupy less than 25% of focal area's visual mass?
- Do soft edges between warm and cool zones prevent harsh color transitions?

## Exercise 3: Finished Piece & Self-Critique

### Capstone Study: Integrated Harmonic Composition (Media of Choice)

Synthesizing value, spatial ordering, and color harmony, you now execute a fully realized study that demonstrates command of harmonic framing principles across your chosen medium.

**Objective:** Create a finished representational or non-representational composition that integrates spatial ordering (grid/proportion), value hierarchy (focal point, depth), and color harmony into a cohesive whole. Document your process and reflect on decision points.

**Duration:** 240–300 minutes across three to four studio sessions.

**Media Choice (choose one):**
1. **Traditional Painting:** Acrylic or oil on canvas board, 16×20 inches minimum.
2. **Digital Painting:** Krita, Photoshop, or GIMP, minimum 2560×1920 pixels (print-ready resolution).
3. **Mixed Media / Collage:** Paper, paint, printed elements, 16×20 inches, fully composed and adhered.
4. **Photography + Editing:** Original photograph color-graded and composed in DaVinci Resolve or similar, 1920×1440 pixels minimum.
5. **3D Render:** Blender scene composed, lit, and rendered at 2560×1920 pixels with intentional focal point and harmonic color palette.

**Subject Matter:** Any representational (portrait, landscape, still life, interior) or non-representational (abstract, pattern, geometric) subject that allows you to exercise deliberate compositional choice.

**Preparatory Phase (60 minutes):**

**Step 1: Conceptual Sketch & Armature (20 minutes):**
In a sketchbook or digital sketch layer, execute three quick thumbnail compositions (each 3×4 inches or 400×500 pixels) of your chosen subject. For each, overlay your Rule of Thirds grid and Golden Division lines (use a light overlay or drawn guides). Identify where you intend to place your focal point, secondary figures/elements, and background masses. Mark focal point location by coordinates relative to grid intersections (e.g., "primary focal point at Rule of Thirds intersection [2,2], offset +20 pixels right").

Choose the strongest of three thumbnails based on:
- Clear focal hierarchy (one primary point of visual interest, secondary points supporting it).
- Balanced spatial distribution (not all elements clustered in one corner; white space distributed intentionally).
- Coherent directional flow (gestural lines guide eye through composition without confusion).

Scan or photograph the chosen thumbnail at 1200 DPI and store as "Capstone_Concept_Sketch.jpg".

**Step 2: Value Study (20 minutes):**
Using graphite on 8×10-inch paper, execute a quick five-value study mirroring your chosen thumbnail. Establish:
- Brightest white (100% brightness, reserved for focal highlight).
- Light gray (70% brightness, used in secondary/mid-ground areas).
- Mid-gray (50% brightness, dominant in composition).
- Dark gray (25–30% brightness, used for depth/background).
- Deepest black (0% brightness, used minimally for maximum focal contrast).

Photograph this study at 1200 DPI and store as "Capstone_Value_Study.jpg".

**Step 3: Color Palette Development (20 minutes):**
If working with color, develop a cohesive palette using your chosen harmonious relationship:
- Analogous (three hues, 30–60 degrees apart on color wheel, all at similar saturation/brightness for harmony).
- Complementary (two opposite hues, one dominant at full saturation, one accent at reduced saturation).
- Triadic (three hues equally spaced, 120 degrees apart; use one as dominant, two as secondary/accents).

Document your palette: on a 5×7-inch card or digital document, paint or display five color swatches, labeled by hue name and RGB/HSV values. Example:
```text
Primary (Focal): Deep Red (RGB: 170, 20, 30 | HSV: 356°, 82%, 67%)
Secondary (Mid-ground): Orange-Red (RGB: 215, 100, 50 | HSV: 20°, 77%, 84%)
Tertiary (Background): Pale Orange (RGB: 240, 180, 120 | HSV: 30°, 50%, 94%)
Accent (Complementary): Deep Cyan (RGB: 20, 140, 170 | HSV: 192°, 88%, 67%)
Neutrals: White (RGB: 255, 255, 255), Black (RGB: 0, 0, 0)
```

**Execution Phase (180–240 minutes, across three sessions):**

**Session 1: Blocking & Underpainting (60 minutes)**

**For Traditional Painting:**
1. Transfer your concept sketch to your canvas board using graphite paper or light pencil guidelines. Establish basic composition structure lightly.
2. Using diluted acrylic (or oil thinned with solvent), block in broad color masses using your three-color palette. Work from background (distant, cool or light) to foreground (near, warm or dark). Allow paint to dry between layers (acrylics: 10–20 minutes; oils: 2–4 hours).
3. Establish clear value separation between focal point and surrounding areas. Your focal point should read distinctly even at this underpainting stage.

**For Digital Painting (Krita):**
1. Create a new 2560×1920 canvas, RGB 8-bit.
2. Sketch your composition using a hard-edged brush (size 10, opacity 100%) in neutral gray (RGB: 128, 128, 128). Keep sketch lines loose and gestural; do not render detail.
3. Create a new layer for color blocking beneath your sketch. Block broad color masses using your chosen palette at 80% opacity. Use a soft round brush (size 50) for blended transitions.
4. Lock your sketch layer and proceed with color refinement on the layer beneath.

**For Photography + DaVinci Resolve:**
1. Take or select an existing photograph (minimum 1920×1440 resolution) of your chosen subject. Import into DaVinci Resolve: File > Import Media.
2. In the Color Page, establish a neutral baseline: apply a Log to Linear transform (Curves: Input = Rec.709 Log; Output = Linear). This prevents color shifts during grading.
3. Apply primary color balance (Lift, Gamma, Gain adjustment node) to shift overall color temperature toward your chosen palette. If aiming for warm (orange-red focal), reduce cyan and increase red in Gamma and Gain nodes.

**For 3D (Blender):**
1. Import or model a simple object or scene (cube, sphere, cone, or downloaded asset) into Blender.
2. Establish a 3-point studio lighting rig: Key light (45 degrees from camera, 60 degrees elevation), Fill light (opposite side, reduced intensity), Back light (behind subject, emphasizing silhouette). Adjust light intensity to create clear focal highlight and shadow separation.
3. Apply an initial material (Principled BSDF shader) using your chosen palette colors. Render at 2560×1920 pixels.

**Session 2: Refinement & Detail (60–90 minutes)**

**For Traditional Painting:**
1. Build form through layered application of medium-value and light-value colors. Gradually refine edges: soft edges in transitional areas (depth cues), hard edges at focal points (emphasis).
2. Introduce secondary hue variation within your analogous palette (e.g., add yellow-orange touches within predominantly red-orange areas) to increase visual richness without breaking harmony.
3. Reserve your deepest black and brightest white for final emphasis. Apply these sparingly around your focal point.
4. Step back frequently (3–4 feet) to assess overall value distribution and focal strength.

**For Digital Painting (Krita):**
1. Create a new layer for mid-tone refinement. Using a soft brush (size 25, opacity 60%), paint semi-transparent layers of intermediate hues to blend color transitions and suggest form dimension.
2. Add detail on a new layer: use smaller brushes (size 5–10) for edge refinement, texture, or representational detail if applicable.
3. Sample colors from your established palette repeatedly (color picker tool) to maintain harmonic consistency; avoid introducing new hues outside your planned palette.
4. Merge layers strategically (Layers > Merge Down) to consolidate color passes and prevent file bloat.

**For Photography + DaVinci Resolve:**
1. Create secondary adjustment nodes for localized color grading: HSL qualifier to isolate specific color ranges (e.g., reds in focal area) and apply saturation/hue shift.
2. Add a Power Window mask (polygon or ellipse) around focal point; apply Curves adjustment to increase local contrast (darken shadows, brighten highlights) in focal region only.
3. Apply spatial noise reduction (Fusion Page > Noise Reduction node) to reduce digital noise while preserving edge definition.

**For 3D (Blender):**
1. Refine material properties: adjust Roughness (0.2–0.5 for semi-matte surfaces), Metallic (0–1 for reflective surfaces), and Subsurface Scattering (for translucent materials like skin or fabric).
2. Modify lighting: increase Key light intensity to push focal area brighter; reduce Fill light to deepen shadow depth. Render test at 1920×1440 and assess.
3. If desired, composite multiple render layers (Beauty, AO, Emission) in DaVinci Resolve to increase grading control.

**Session 3: Finishing & Integration (60 minutes)**

**For All Media:**
1. Verify focal point clarity: does the brightest white/lightest color and deepest shadow (maximum value contrast) sit at or near your chosen focal point? If not, amplify contrast locally.
2. Check color harmony: are your analogous colors reading as visually unified? Is your complementary accent visibly distinct but not competing? If colors are muddying, apply a final glaze layer (acrylics: diluted transparent layer; digital: reduced-opacity layer) of your primary palette color to unify tone.
3. Assess edge quality: scan your composition and verify at least three visibly soft edges (suggesting depth) and two to three hard edges (suggesting focal emphasis). Add edge softness or hardness adjustments if needed.
4. White space check: is at least 20–30% of your composition "breathing room" (unfilled white/background areas)? If overloaded, reduce detail or selectively lighten background areas.

**Documentation & Reflection (30 minutes):**
1. Photograph your finished work under consistent studio lighting (north-facing window or neutral LED). Save at minimum 2560×1920 pixels and label "Capstone_Finished_[Date].jpg".
2. Create a Process Document (digital or printed): Include (1) your concept sketch thumbnail, (2) value study, (3) color palette card/swatch, and (4) finished image side-by-side. Add written annotations for each stage answering:
   - Focal point location: "Primary focal point placed at Rule of Thirds intersection [row 2, col 2], emphasizing [subject detail]."
   - Value decisions: "Used maximum value contrast (pure white highlight + deep black shadow) only in focal area; surrounding areas use mid-range values to recede."
   - Color harmony chosen: "Analogous warm palette (red, orange-red, pale orange) with complementary cyan accent at focal edge to amplify visual tension."
   - Challenges encountered: "Initial composition felt static; added diagonal directional line in mid-ground to guide eye toward focal point."
3. Store this document as "Capstone_Process_Notes_[Date].pdf".

## Critique Checklist & Common Fixes

Use this rubric to assess your own work and that of peers. Score each criterion on a 1–5 scale (5 = excellent, 1 = needs development). Prioritize items marked ** as foundational.

### Critique Rubric

### Common Fixes

**Problem: Focal Point Unclear; Composition Feels Static**

*Diagnosis:* Focal area lacks value contrast or sits in compositionally ambiguous location (center, edge, or non-aligned with grid).

*Fix:*
1. Measure focal point location numerically (pixels from top-left corner). Compare to Rule of Thirds intersections: (W/3, H/3), (2W/3, H/3), (W/3, 2H/3), (2W/3, 2H/3). If focal point deviates by >50 pixels, reposition it.
2. Apply a value contrast boost: add pure white highlight directly adjacent to deep shadow (black or very dark value) at focal point. This creates maximum visual pull.
3. If color is involved, add a small complementary accent (opposite hue, full saturation) at focal edge to amplify visual disruption and draw eye.

*Example:* In Exercise 3, your focal area was orange-red but lacked accompanying bright white. Add a white line or small shape at the edge of the orange-red focal module. Instantly, contrast increases and eye fixates on focal point.

---

**Problem: Composition Reads Flat; No Sense of Depth**

*Diagnosis:* All elements use similar value; no value recession from foreground to background. Color temperature is uniform (all warm or all cool) across composition.

*Fix:*
1. Introduce atmospheric perspective: systematically lighten and cool colors as they recede. Foreground = warm, saturated, dark. Middle ground = intermediate. Background = cool, desaturated, light.
2. Use size relationships: smaller elements typically sit farther back; larger elements in foreground. Proportionally increase foreground element size by 30–50%.
3. Apply soft focus or reduced detail to background; sharp focus or fine detail to foreground. In digital tools, apply Gaussian Blur (Gaussian Blur > 2–4 pixel radius) to background layers only.
4. Test with greyscale check: convert finished composition to greyscale. If depth vanishes, reestablish value separation.

*Example:* Your landscape had a distant mountain and near trees all painted in similar mid-tone brown. Repaint mountain in pale blue-gray (cool, light); trees in deep warm brown (foreground). Depth emerges.

---

**Problem: Too Many Colors; Palette Feels Chaotic**

*Diagnosis:* Work includes 6+ dominant hues; colors appear scattered across spectrum without relationship.

*Fix:*
1. Reduce palette to three hues: one dominant (60% of composition), one secondary (30%), one accent (10%).
2. For each hue, use only two or three value/saturation variations. E.g., if primary hue is red: use deep red (100% sat, 50% brightness), medium red (80% sat, 65% brightness), pale red (40% sat, 85% brightness). Eliminate outliers.
3. Convert work to HSL and inspect hue distribution (Tools > Hue Saturation or equivalent). Identify any hues outside your planned scheme and repaint with harmonious alternatives.
4. Create a color palette reference card and place it beside your work during refinement. Cross-check every new color against the reference before applying.

*Example:* Your digital painting had red, purple, cyan, yellow, and green scattered throughout. Restrict to: deep red (focal), orange (mid-tone), pale yellow (background), cyan accent. Repaint purple and green areas with restricted palette. Harmony increases immediately.

---

**Problem: Edges Too Uniform; Composition Lacks Visual Variety**

*Diagnosis:* All edges are hard (crisp and uniform) or all soft (blurred and diffuse). No contrast between edge types.

*Fix:*
1. Scan composition and mark every edge (boundary between two value or color zones). Count: target minimum 2–3 hard edges, 2–3 soft edges, rest intermediate.
2. For each focal area edge, enforce hardness: use sharp brush strokes, high opacity, no blending.
3. For depth-recession edges, enforce softness: use soft-edged brush, reduce opacity to 50–70%, drag along boundary line to create gradation.
4. Use a physical mirror or flip-canvas check (Image > Transform > Flip Horizontally) to view composition reversed. Fresh perspective reveals edge monotony.

*Example:* Your acrylic study had all crisp edges where color shapes met. Select 2–3 boundaries and blend them: load brush with minimal paint, drag across edge line using dry-brush technique. Softened edges suggest spatial transition; crisp edges remain at focal point for emphasis.

---

**Problem: Focal Point Competes with Background; Too Much Visual Clutter**

*Diagnosis:* Background is as detailed or colorful as focal area, creating visual ambiguity. No clear visual hierarchy.

*Fix:*
1. Simplify background: reduce detail (smooth out textures), reduce color saturation (desaturate or shift to greys), reduce value variation (use uniform mid-tone rather than alternating light and dark).
2. Reserve maximum contrast (black + white), maximum saturation (vivid hues), and finest detail for focal point only.
3. Create soft gradient background: use a single background color shifted gradually from one edge to another (e.g., pale yellow at top grading to white at bottom), creating visual recession without distraction.
4. Apply white space strategically: frame focal area with unfilled (white/negative) space to visually isolate it.

*Example:* Your landscape had a detailed texture-filled sky competing with focal foreground figure. Repaint sky as simple pale blue gradient. Eliminate foreground texture clutter by blocking areas into simple value masses. Figure now dominates visually.

---

**Problem: Composition Feels Static or Rigid; No Visual Flow**

*Diagnosis:* Elements are arranged symmetrically or in grid pattern without directional continuity. Eye does not travel through composition; it stalls.

*Fix:*
1. Introduce directional line: add a subtle curved or diagonal line that guides eye from edge of composition through focal point and toward background. This can be a path, horizon line, figure orientation, or color gradient direction.
2. Break symmetry: if elements are evenly spaced (e.g., four equal modules), reposition one or two to create imbalance and tension. Asymmetry generates visual energy.
3. Use size gradation: make foreground elements progressively smaller as they recede, creating sense of movement through space.
4. Add rhythm through repetition: repeat a shape or color pattern at decreasing intensity as it recedes, creating visual momentum.

*Example:* Your still life had three spheres arranged in a horizontal line, rigid and static. Reposition: one sphere in foreground (large), two smaller spheres receding diagonally toward background. Add a curved shadow line connecting them. Composition now flows.

---

**Problem: Value Study Strong, But Finished Color Version Fails**

*Diagnosis:* Greyscale study reads as cohesive; color version feels disjointed. Likely cause: color harmony strategy not followed, or color is too saturated globally.

*Fix:*
1. Convert finished color work to greyscale (Image > Mode > Greyscale or desaturate). Compare side-by-side to value study. If value relationships differ, color application violated the value structure. Repaint colored areas to match greyscale value intentions.
2. Reduce global saturation by 20–30%: in digital tools, apply Hue-Saturation adjustment (Hue: 0, Saturation: −30). Re-evaluate. Oversaturated color disrupts harmonic balance; desaturation restores unity.
3. Verify color palette adherence: use color picker on five representative areas of finished work. Record HSV values. Ensure all hues fall within ±30 degrees of your planned palette center hues. If not, repaint outlier areas.

*Example:* Your color study had analogous warm palette but finished painting included vibrant saturated yellows and oranges scattered throughout. Desaturate globally by 25%, reducing to warm earth tones at 60–70% saturation. Harmony restored; focal point re-emerges.

### Peer Critique Protocol

Conduct peer reviews in small groups (2–3 artists) using this framework:

**Peer Review Structure (20 minutes per artwork):**

1. **Silent Observation (3 minutes):** Viewer examines work without comment, taking notes on immediate impressions.

2. **Artist Statement (2 minutes):** Artist describes intention (focal point, color harmony chosen, compositional challenge attempted).

3. **Positive Feedback (5 minutes):** Peer identifies specific strengths:
   - "Your focal point is immediately clear because of the high value contrast between the dark red and surrounding pale tones."
   - "The soft edge between mid-ground and background creates convincing spatial recession."
   - Avoid vague praise ("nice colors"); be specific about what works and why.

4. **Diagnostic Questions (5 minutes):** Peer asks clarifying questions to deepen artist's understanding:
   - "What was your Rule of Thirds focal point location, and does the eye actually land there?"
   - "Walk me through your color palette choices—which hues are analogous and which is the accent?"
   - "If I squint, does the focal point still read as dominant?"

5. **Constructive Suggestion (3 minutes):** Peer offers one actionable suggestion referencing the critique rubric:
   - "If you softened the edge between the focal red and surrounding orange, it might feel more spatially cohesive while keeping focal emphasis."
   - Avoid directives ("you should change X"); frame as experiments ("what if you tried…?").

6. **Artist Reflection (2 minutes):** Artist responds to feedback, noting ideas to explore in revision or next study.

**Documentation:** Each artist records peer feedback on a critique form (provided below) and stores with their finished work and process notes.

### Diagnostic Tools Reference

**Digital Value Check (Krita / GIMP / Photoshop):**
1. Convert image to greyscale: Image > Mode > Greyscale (GIMP) or Desaturate (Photoshop/Krita, Options > Desaturate).
2. Open Histogram: Windows > Dockable Dialogs > Histogram (Krita) or Window > Histogram (Photoshop).
3. Inspect pixel distribution: pixels should concentrate at three peaks—dark (0–30% brightness), mid (40–60%), light (70–100%)—not centered at 50%.

**Focal Point Verification (All Media):**
1. Photograph your work under neutral lighting.
2. Convert to thumbnail size (200×150 pixels) and zoom out.
3. Where does your eye fixate first? If it lands within your intended focal area, focal hierarchy succeeds. If eye drifts elsewhere, strengthen focal contrast.

**Color Palette Audit (Digital):**
1. Use color picker to sample 10 representative points across image.
2. Record HSV for each. Create a histogram of Hue values: plot each sampled hue on a 360-degree wheel.
3. If sampled hues concentrate in one 90-degree arc of the wheel, harmonic unity succeeds. If scattered across entire wheel, break harmony.

**Edge Quality Audit (All Media):**
1. Mark every boundary between distinct value or color zones with a small numbered sticker or digital annotation.
2. Categorize each edge: S (soft/blurred), H (hard/crisp), I (intermediate/gradated).
3. Target ratio: S 40%, I 30%, H 30%. Adjust if distribution is skewed.

## Additional Resources & Extended Practice

### Extended Exercise: Series Development (Optional)

After completing Exercise 3, develop a series of five related studies exploring variations on a single compositional concept. This deepens decision-making and reveals the range of harmonic relationships within one framework.

**Assignment:** Repaint or redesign your Exercise 3 capstone piece five times, each with a different compositional variable:

1. **Variant A: Focal Point Repositioning**
   - Move primary focal point to each of five locations (Rule of Thirds intersections, center, edge, offset).
   - Keep all other variables constant (value palette, color harmony, subject).
   - Compare which location generates strongest visual pull.

2. **Variant B: Value Restructuring**
   - Invert your value hierarchy: what was darkest becomes lightest, and vice versa.
   - Maintain all spatial/color decisions.
   - Assess whether focal point remains readable or shifts to unexpected location.

3. **Variant C: Color Harmony Shift**
   - Repaint using a different harmonic strategy (if original was analogous, now use complementary; if complementary, now use triadic).
   - Preserve value and focal structure.
   - Note which harmony strategy amplifies or diminishes focal emphasis.

4. **Variant D: Edge Hardness Reversal**
   - Soften all previously hard edges; harden all previously soft edges.
   - Evaluate impact on spatial depth and focal clarity.

5. **Variant E: White Space Manipulation**
   - Reduce background breathing room by 50% (crop or frame more tightly around subject).
   - Increase background by 50% (expand canvas or zoom out).
   - Note psychological impact of framing density on viewer attention and comfort.

Document each variant photographically and store alongside process notes. This series becomes a research record of compositional variables and their perceptual effects.

### Supplementary Reading & Reference Masters

Study the following historical and contemporary masters to deepen intuitive understanding of harmonic framing:

**Foundational Studies (Value & Composition):**
- Rembrandt, *The Night Watch* (1642): Study value separation between focal figures and background; note how minimal highlight area commands attention.
- Caravaggio, *The Calling of Saint Matthew* (1599–1600): Analyze light-to-dark ratio; observe maximum contrast at focal point (saint's hand and face).

**Spatial Ordering & Grid:**
- Piet Mondrian, *Composition with Red, Yellow, and Blue* (1921): Examine proportional grid beneath abstract composition; note how Rule of Thirds and Golden Division underlie seemingly random rectangles.
- Paul Klee, *Ancient Sound* (1925): Study subtle color harmony within restricted palette; observe graduated value transitions suggesting depth.

**Color Harmony & Focal Emphasis:**
- Mark Rothko, *Untitled (1949)* or *Orange and Yellow* (1956): Analyze color field relationships; note how narrow hue ranges generate visual resonance.
- Paul Cézanne, *Mont Sainte-Victoire* (series, 1885–1906): Compare multiple compositional studies of same subject; observe focal point repositioning across variants and resulting spatial effects.

**Contemporary Practice:**
- Hilma af Klint, *Temple* series (1906–1915): Study non-representational harmonic framing; observe how pure form and color relationships generate compositional hierarchy.
- Contemporary digital artists (social media: ArtStation, Dribbble, Instagram #conceptart): Seek process videos showing digital painters' spatial decision-making in real time.

## Conclusion

This lab sequence has trained you in the core mechanics of harmonic visual framing: spatial ordering through proportional grids, value hierarchy through contrast and focal emphasis, color harmony through deliberate palette restriction, and integrated synthesis through finished studies. You have moved from passive observation (Exercise 1) through controlled technical drill (Exercises 2A–2B) to autonomous creative execution (Exercise 3), and critically reflected on your decisions through self- and peer critique.

Harmonic framing is not formulaic; Rule of Thirds, Golden Division, and color harmony are frameworks, not laws. The deepest compositional mastery emerges from understanding *why* these frameworks generate visual resonance, then trusting your eye to know when to break them. Continue practicing: sketch daily, compare your work against master studies, and refine your diagnostic eye through persistent measurement and reflection. Each finished study is a record of decision-making; each revision is a step toward intuitive mastery.

Your next practice: select one finished study from this sequence and develop it into a series (as outlined in Extended Exercise above), or choose a new subject and apply the full workflow from concept thumbnail through finished piece, integrating spatial ordering, value control, and color harmony with greater sophistication. Build a portfolio of process documentation—concept sketches, value studies, finished images, and critique notes—as evidence of deliberate compositional thinking.

The visual craft demands slow, sustained attention. Studio practice is the only teacher. Return to these exercises, return to master studies, and return to your work with fresh eyes. Harmonic framing will become not a conscious checklist but an intuitive language you speak fluently through your medium.
