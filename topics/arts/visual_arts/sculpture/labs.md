# Operational Verification Labs: Sculpture

## Objective & DAW / Studio Setup

Sculpture as a compositional and sonic practice sits at the intersection of three foundational disciplines: harmonic architecture (the orchestration of pitch relationships across time), timbral design (the sculpting of frequency content and dynamic envelopes), and spatial staging (the placement and movement of sound in a three-dimensional listening environment). For the audio practitioner moving from abstract musical notation into the tangible domain of digital synthesis and mixing, mastering sculpture requires developing both a theoretical framework for voice leading and harmonic progression, and a pragmatic command of the tools that bring those ideas to audible form.

This lab sequence assumes you are working within a professional Digital Audio Workstation (Ableton Live, Logic Pro X, or Reaper), equipped with a MIDI keyboard controller, and access to both soft-synth instruments (Native Instruments Massive X, Serum, or comparable) and analysis tools (Voxengo SPAN spectrum analyzer, Ableton's Spectral Time, or built-in metering). Your goal across these three exercises is to internalize the discipline of sculpting harmonic progressions into coherent timbral gestures, verifying your work through both critical listening and objective measurement.

Before beginning, establish your studio environment with the following baseline configuration. Launch your DAW and create a new session at 96 kHz sample rate with a buffer size of 256 samples (5.3 ms latency at 96 kHz). Set your session tempo to 120 BPM and confirm your audio interface is correctly assigned as both input and output device. Insert a MIDI track and route it to a soft-synth instrument (Wavetable in Ableton Live, or Sculpture in Logic Pro X serves as an ideal entry point). Insert a return track labeled "Analysis" and load Voxengo SPAN as a stereo analyzer on that track's input, leaving it in paused mode until you require measurement. Create a master bus chain with a loudness meter (Ableton Live's utility meter set to LUFS mode, or a dedicated metering plugin) and a safety limiter at −3 dB to protect against clipping during synthesis experimentation. Confirm your monitoring path by routing the master output directly to your audio interface's main stereo output without any additional processing or compression.

## Exercise 1: Practical Composition & Voice-Leading Realization

The first operational challenge is to take a seven-chord progression defined in Roman numeral analysis and realize it as a four-voice chorale with strict voice-leading rules, then synthesize it as a sculptural harmonic block using four distinct timbral voices. This exercise trains both your harmonic intuition and your ability to map abstract voice relationships onto concrete synthesizer parameters.

Your assignment is to compose and realize a progression in the key of C major following this harmonic architecture: I (tonic triad) → vi (relative minor) → IV (subdominant) → V (dominant) → I (tonic resolution). In Roman numeral terms within C major, this translates to C major triad → A minor triad → F major triad → G major triad → C major triad. You will voice each triad using the Fuxian four-voice texture: soprano, alto, tenor, and bass, adhering to the following constraints: maintain the bass voice as the root of each chord; limit melodic motion in the soprano to stepwise motion (seconds) or common tones whenever possible; ensure that inner voices (alto and tenor) move by step or common tone; avoid parallel perfect fifths or octaves between any two adjacent voices; and resolve any tendency tones (such as the leading tone B in a G major chord) by step to the chord tone it gravitates toward.

Begin by opening MuseScore or Dorico and creating a new four-voice score in C major, 4/4 time, with a moderate tempo of quarter note = 90 BPM. Enter the progression using standard notation on a grand staff with treble and bass clefs split across two systems. For the soprano voice (highest line in the treble clef), use the following note sequence: C5 (whole note) → A4 (whole note, stepwise descent) → A4 (whole note, common tone held) → B4 (whole note, leading tone a step below the resolution) → C5 (whole note, resolution to tonic). For the alto voice (lower line in the treble clef), notate: E4 (whole note) → C4 (whole note, stepwise descent) → F4 (whole note, skip acceptable as inner voice) → D4 (whole note, stepwise descent toward resolution) → E4 (whole note). For the tenor voice (higher line in the bass clef), notate: G3 (whole note) → E3 (whole note, stepwise descent) → C3 (whole note, stepwise descent) → G3 (whole note, common tone from the V chord) → G3 (whole note). For the bass voice (lowest line in the bass clef), notate: C3 (whole note) → A2 (whole note) → F2 (whole note) → G2 (whole note) → C2 (whole note). Play back this four-voice arrangement using MuseScore's built-in playback engine to aurally confirm the harmonic progression. Listen carefully for any harsh parallel fifths or octaves; if you hear them, adjust the inner voices by semitone intervals until the progression sounds smooth and consonant.

Export this score as a MIDI file (File → Export → MIDI Format). Open your DAW and create a new MIDI track. Import the exported MIDI file onto that track. You will now sculpt this harmonic skeleton into a timbral object by assigning each voice its own synthesizer instance with distinct sonic characteristics. Create four new MIDI tracks and label them "Bass Voice," "Tenor Voice," "Alto Voice," and "Soprano Voice." Load an instance of your soft-synth onto each track—use Wavetable in Ableton Live or any polyphonic subtractive synthesizer. On the Bass Voice track, configure your synth with the following parameters: select a sawtooth wave as the oscillator source; set the filter cutoff frequency to 350 Hz with a 24 dB/octave low-pass filter slope; set the filter resonance (Q) to 0.7 (slightly damped); configure the amplitude envelope with an attack of 50 ms, decay of 200 ms, sustain level at 0.8, and release of 300 ms; enable a low-frequency oscillator (LFO) set to a sine wave at 0.5 Hz with 15% modulation depth applied to the filter cutoff to create subtle timbral breathing. Manually transcribe or copy-paste the bass voice MIDI notes from the original four-voice MIDI file into this Bass Voice track, ensuring that each note aligns temporally with its corresponding entry in the full progression.

For the Tenor Voice track, use a sine wave as the oscillator source for a pure, hollow character; set the filter cutoff to 800 Hz with a 12 dB/octave slope and resonance of 1.2 (slightly peaked); configure the amplitude envelope with attack of 80 ms, decay of 150 ms, sustain at 0.75, and release of 250 ms; add a second LFO modulating the oscillator pitch at 2 Hz with ±20 cents modulation depth to introduce subtle pitch variation. Copy the tenor voice notes into this track. For the Alto Voice track, use a square wave with 30% pulse-width modulation (PWM); set the filter cutoff to 1200 Hz with a 24 dB/octave slope and resonance of 0.9; configure the envelope with attack of 100 ms, decay of 180 ms, sustain at 0.65, and release of 350 ms; modulate the PWM parameter with an LFO at 1.3 Hz with 25% modulation depth. Copy the alto notes into this track. For the Soprano Voice track, use a triangle wave for a bright, clear quality; set the filter cutoff to 2000 Hz with a 24 dB/octave slope and resonance of 1.5 (peaked for articulation); configure the envelope with attack of 30 ms, decay of 100 ms, sustain at 0.8, and release of 200 ms; apply no additional LFO modulation to preserve clarity in the highest voice.

Route each of these four tracks to a group/bus channel labeled "Harmonic Block." On this group channel, insert a stereo reverb plugin (Valhalla Room, Ableton's Operator, or Reaper's ReaVerb) with the following settings: pre-delay of 20 ms, decay time of 1.8 seconds, dry/wet mix at 25% to create a sense of spatial cohesion without obscuring the harmonic definition. Set the overall volume of the group to −6 dB to leave headroom for dynamics processing. On your master channel, insert a loudness meter and confirm that the combined four-voice sculpture reaches approximately −12 LUFS when all voices sustain simultaneously at their steady-state level. Play back the complete arrangement; as you listen, pay attention to how the timbral differences between the four voices create perceptual separation while the harmonic relationships bind them into a unified gesture. Solo each voice in sequence and listen to how its individual character contributes to the whole. This exercise verifies your ability to map abstract voice-leading rules onto concrete synthesizer designs, and demonstrates how timbral differentiation can enhance the clarity of harmonic architecture.

## Exercise 2: Timbre Synthesis, Waveform Shaping & Modulation Routing

The second operational challenge is to design a single complex synthesizer patch that morphs between three distinct timbral characters in response to MIDI note velocity and keyboard position, then use that patch to re-synthesize the harmonic progression from Exercise 1 with dynamic timbral variation. This exercise develops your understanding of how oscillator harmonics, filter topology, and modulation architecture sculpt the perceptual identity of a sound.

Open your DAW and create a new MIDI track. Load an instance of a capable subtractive synthesizer with modulation matrix capabilities (Serum, Massive X, or Wavetable in Ableton). You will design a morphing patch that transitions between three sonic states based on external control. Begin with the oscillator section. Configure Oscillator 1 to a sawtooth wave; leave Oscillator 2 set to a sine wave at a pitch offset of +7 semitones (perfect fifth above the fundamental). Enable oscillator mix blending and set the initial mix point to 60% saw / 40% sine. The sawtooth contributes bright, harmonically rich content (all harmonic partials present in descending amplitude: fundamental, 2x fundamental, 3x, 4x, et cetera), while the fifth-offset sine adds harmonic complexity and slight pitch ambiguity.

In the filter section, configure a 24 dB/octave low-pass filter as your primary shaping element. Set the cutoff frequency to 4000 Hz and resonance (Q) to 1.8. Add a second filter stage in series: a 12 dB/octave high-pass filter with cutoff at 80 Hz to remove sub-bass rumble while preserving fundamental clarity. In your modulation matrix, route MIDI velocity to the low-pass filter cutoff with a bipolar range of ±1500 Hz, such that striking the keys with high velocity opens the filter cutoff to 5500 Hz (bright, sizzling timbral character), while soft strikes close it to 2500 Hz (muted, filtered character). Route MIDI note number (keyboard position) to the oscillator mix parameter with a scaling factor such that playing low notes (C2–C3) places the mix at 80% saw / 20% sine (full harmonic brightness), middle notes (C4–C5) place it at 50/50 (balanced hybrid), and high notes (C6–C7) place it at 20% saw / 80% sine (pure, thin character). This creates an intuitive velocity and pitch-responsive instrument.

Configure the amplitude envelope with attack 40 ms, decay 150 ms, sustain 0.7, release 200 ms. Add a second envelope (ENV 2) dedicated to filter modulation: attack 20 ms, decay 300 ms, sustain 0, release 100 ms. Route ENV 2 to the low-pass filter cutoff with a modulation depth of +800 Hz; this creates a dynamic articulation where each note strike triggers a brief brightening before the filter settles to its velocity-determined steady state. Enable an LFO set to a triangle wave at 1.5 Hz with bipolar amplitude ±200 Hz applied to the low-pass filter cutoff; this introduces slow timbral undulation independent of note timing.

Add a second LFO (LFO 2) set to a sawtooth wave at 5.2 Hz with unipolar amplitude −15 cents applied to Oscillator 1 pitch; this creates subtle inharmonic beating and prevents the patch from sounding overly static or sterile. Enable unison mode (voice stacking) with 5 voices detuned ±30 cents and spread across the stereo field with 60% spatial width. This fattens the sound and creates natural stereo width without requiring explicit effects processing. Set the unison voice spread to use a polynomial detune curve rather than linear so that the detuning is perceptually even across the frequency range. Configure portamento (glide) with a time constant of 80 ms, so that moving between notes produces smooth pitch slides rather than abrupt pitch jumps.

Now load the four-voice MIDI progression from Exercise 1 back into your DAW. Delete or silence the individual voice tracks from Exercise 1. On a new MIDI track, paste the complete four-voice progression (all soprano, alto, tenor, and bass notes stacked into a single polyphonic MIDI clip). Route this track to your newly designed morphing synthesizer patch. Play back the progression at the same tempo (90 BPM) and listen carefully to how the velocity-responsive filter opening and the pitch-responsive oscillator mixing create timbral evolution as you move from the low-register bass notes of the I chord through the middle-register harmonies of the vi and IV chords, arriving at the high-register soprano voices of the V and final I resolutions. The progression should sound like a single coherent sculptural object that breathes and morphs rather than a static harmonic block.

Press Play and allow the progression to loop continuously. Activate the Voxengo SPAN analyzer on your return track and route the synth output into that track; enable the analyzer's real-time mode and observe the frequency spectrum as the progression evolves. You should see the spectral energy concentrate in different frequency bands as the velocity-responsive filter opens and closes, and as the oscillator mix transitions from harmonic-rich saws (full spectral presence) to sine-dominated (narrow spectral content). Pause the analyzer at a moment when the V chord (G major) is sustaining with high velocity to capture the maximum brightness, and take a screenshot or note the spectral distribution. Then pause at a moment when the relative minor vi chord (A minor) sustains with low velocity to capture the minimum brightness, and compare the two spectral snapshots. The high-velocity V chord should show spectral energy extending to at least 8–10 kHz with visible peaks at the 2nd and 3rd harmonic overtones of the sawtooth, while the low-velocity vi chord should show energy concentrated below 4 kHz with attenuated overtones. This spectral contrast verifies that your modulation routing is functioning as intended and that the synthesizer patch is responding dynamically to MIDI input.

Now introduce a performance variation: manually re-record the progression by playing the MIDI notes on your keyboard controller while varying your strike velocity in a musically expressive pattern. Play the I chord with medium velocity (65), the vi chord with soft velocity (45), the IV chord with medium-light velocity (55), the V chord with maximum velocity (127), and the final I chord resolution with medium-heavy velocity (85). Record this take as a new MIDI clip. Play back this performed version alongside a second instance of the original programmed clip; you should hear a palpable difference in timbral character driven by your velocity variations, demonstrating how performer control maps onto synthesizer responsiveness. This exercise verifies your command of subtractive synthesis architecture and modulation matrix design, and demonstrates how to create expressive, sculptural timbral behavior within a single patch.

## Exercise 3: Dynamic Mixing, Spectrum Analysis & Audio Stress Testing

The third operational challenge is to take your synthesized harmonic progressions from Exercises 1 and 2, process them through a mixing chain that includes dynamic range processing, equalization, and spatial effects, then subject your mix to objective frequency analysis and loudness metering to verify that your sculpted harmonies translate clearly across different playback systems and maintain proper headroom and loudness standards.

Create a new session in your DAW and import both the four-separate-voice arrangement from Exercise 1 and the morphing-patch performance from Exercise 2 as two separate stereo stems. Route each stem to its own return track: label them "Four-Voice Block" and "Morphing Performance." On each of these return tracks, insert a parametric equalizer (Ableton's EQ Eight, Logic's Channel EQ, or Reaper's ReaEQ). On the Four-Voice Block return, create an equalization curve that emphasizes the presence band (2–4 kHz) where vocal-like clarity resides: boost the 3 kHz center frequency by +3 dB with a Q of 2.0 (moderately narrow). Add a gentle high-frequency lift at 8 kHz (+2 dB, Q of 1.5) to restore air and brilliance that may be obscured by the reverb processing. Add a subtle low-frequency cut at 60 Hz (−2 dB, Q of 0.7, very wide) to remove rumble below the range of the fundamental bass notes. On the Morphing Performance return, apply a more aggressive presence peak at 2.5 kHz (+4 dB, Q of 2.2) because the morphing patch's variable filtering creates less inherent presence, and boost the high-frequency shelf above 10 kHz by +3 dB to ensure the sine-wave-dominated high notes remain articulate and audible.

Below the EQ stage on each return track, insert a dynamic range compressor (Ableton's Compressor, FabFilter Pro-C, or Reaper's ReaComp). On the Four-Voice Block, set the compressor threshold to −20 dBFS (a threshold where the steady-state sustaining level of the harmonies will pass through unaffected but any transient peaks will be gently controlled), ratio to 4:1 (moderate compression), attack time to 30 ms (allowing transients to pass through mostly uncompressed for initial impact), release time to 150 ms (returning to unity gain within a short musical beat after transients subside), and makeup gain to +4 dB to compensate for gain reduction. This compression prevents the softer vi chord and the brighter V chord from having wildly disparate apparent loudness, creating timbral cohesion. On the Morphing Performance return, set threshold to −18 dBFS, ratio to 2.5:1 (gentler compression to preserve the patch's dynamic responsiveness), attack to 50 ms, release to 200 ms, and makeup gain to +2 dB.

Below the compressor on each return, insert a reverb plugin. On the Four-Voice Block, load a algorithmic reverb (Valhalla Room, Fabfilter Pro-R, or Reaper's ReaVerb) and set it to "Medium Hall" preset with the following adjustments: pre-delay 25 ms, decay time 2.0 seconds, wet/dry mix 30%, diffusion 0.8 (high), early reflection density at 70%, and modulation depth at 20% to add subtle pitch variation within the reverb tail. On the Morphing Performance return, use "Bright Plate" preset with pre-delay 15 ms, decay 1.5 seconds, wet/dry 35%, diffusion 0.9, and modulation 25%. The reverb adds spatial dimensionality and masks the digital nature of the synthesis, creating the illusion of a performed ensemble in an acoustic space.

Now route both return tracks to your master bus. On the master bus, insert a seven-band graphic equalizer or a parametric EQ in linear-phase mode (to prevent phase distortion). Create a gentle loudness contour that mimics the Fletcher-Munson equal-loudness curves: apply a subtle bass boost at 100 Hz (+2 dB, wide Q of 0.5) and a treble boost at 12 kHz (+1.5 dB, wide Q of 0.6) to compensate for human hearing's reduced sensitivity to extreme low and high frequencies at moderate listening levels. Keep the midrange (500 Hz – 5 kHz) flat or with a gentle −1 dB broad cut to reduce boxiness. Below this EQ, insert a multiband compressor or insert a series of three parallel compression chains configured as follows:

Low-band chain (all frequencies below 200 Hz): Compressor with threshold −15 dBFS, ratio 3:1, attack 40 ms, release 300 ms, makeup gain +3 dB. This chain controls bass bloom from the four-voice block's lowest notes and prevents the low frequencies from muddying the mix.

Mid-band chain (200 Hz – 4 kHz): Compressor with threshold −16 dBFS, ratio 2:1, attack 25 ms, release 200 ms, makeup gain +2 dB. This chain prevents the presence-heavy synthesizer patches from overwhelming the mix during forte passages.

High-band chain (above 4 kHz): Compressor with threshold −18 dBFS, ratio 1.5:1, attack 50 ms, release 250 ms, makeup gain +1 dB. This chain gently controls sibilance and prevents digital harshness.

Set the blend of these three chains such that the low-band contributes 40% of its output to the main mix, mid-band 60%, and high-band 30% (i.e., 100% of the uncompressed signal always passes through, and the compressed signals blend in at these percentages to create a "upward compression" or "New York" style mixing architecture that preserves dynamics while controlling peaks).

Insert your loudness meter on the master bus output and set it to measure integrated LUFS, short-term LUFS, true peak dBFS, and loudness range (LR). Play back the Four-Voice Block return in isolation: allow it to loop for 30 seconds and observe the integrated LUFS reading. It should stabilize in the range of −14 to −12 LUFS for a typical sustained harmonic progression. Play back the Morphing Performance in isolation; due to the velocity variations and dynamic timbral morphing, it may range from −16 to −10 LUFS depending on how aggressively you played the notes. Now enable both returns simultaneously and adjust their individual return-track faders so that when played together, the integrated loudness reaches −13 LUFS (a standard target for music production). The loudness range (LR) measurement should be in the range of 4–8 LU (loudness units), indicating that the mix has meaningful dynamic variation rather than being brick-wall limited.

Activate the Voxengo SPAN analyzer on a fourth return track (or route the master output into SPAN's input). Set SPAN to "Stereo" mode with a 6 dB/octave linear frequency response curve (to see the true spectral content without artificial loudness compensation). Play back the combined mix and observe the real-time frequency spectrum. Pause the analyzer after approximately 15 seconds of playback to capture an integrated spectral snapshot. The spectrum should show the following characteristics: a well-defined fundamental peak at the frequency of the bass voice's root notes (65 Hz for the C2 low tonic note, 82.4 Hz for the A2 vi chord root, et cetera); harmonic series peaks at integer multiples of these fundamentals (2×, 3×, 4×, et cetera), with decreasing amplitude as frequency increases; spectral energy concentrated primarily in the 100 Hz – 8 kHz range with minimal energy above 15 kHz (a sign that high-frequency noise and aliasing are well-controlled); and visible dips in the spectrum corresponding to the frequencies where your compressors and equalizers are operating. Take a screenshot of this spectral measurement.

Now perform a frequency-response headroom stress test. Create a new MIDI clip with a sequence of rapid arpeggios that covers the full frequency range of your progression: begin with arpeggiated open strings on the C2 note (16.35 Hz fundamental, but your synth will be filtering this to approximately 80 Hz minimum), then skip upward in octaves and major thirds through C3 (32.70 Hz), E4 (82.4 Hz), G4 (98.0 Hz), C5 (130.8 Hz), E5 (164.8 Hz), G5 (196.0 Hz), C6 (261.6 Hz), and E6 (329.6 Hz), playing eight sixteenth-note pulses on each pitch at 120 BPM (each arpeggio cycle taking approximately 8 beats or 4 seconds). Use high velocity (120) for all notes to maximize spectral brightness. Record this arpeggio clip through your morphing-patch synth and route it to a new return track labeled "Stress Test." Monitor the true peak dBFS reading on your loudness meter as the arpeggio plays: the true peak should not exceed −0.3 dBFS (leaving at least 0.3 dB of headroom before clipping in 32-bit floating-point digital audio). If the true peak reaches −0.1 dBFS or higher, reduce the master fader by 0.5 dB and re-test. This headroom stress test verifies that your mixing chain can accommodate dynamic performance variations and synthesizer brightness without digital clipping.
