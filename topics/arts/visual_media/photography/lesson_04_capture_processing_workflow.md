# Lesson 4: Capture Workflow, RAW Processing, Output Encoding

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Photography is fundamentally a problem of *light capture, interpretation, and translation*. When you press the shutter, your camera's sensor records photons—but not as a finished image. Instead, it captures raw electrical voltages that must be decoded, demosaiced, color-corrected, and encoded into a viewable format. Understanding this pipeline is essential because every choice you make during capture directly constrains what you can recover and optimize in post-production.

The capture workflow begins with a critical decision: *what information do you preserve at the source?* A JPEG file discards color information immediately through destructive compression and baked-in color science. A RAW file, by contrast, is a nearly lossless recording of what the sensor actually saw—a mathematically faithful recording of photon counts per photosite. This preservation of dynamic range, color latitude, and tonal separation is why professionals shoot RAW. You are not shooting RAW for the sake of format; you are shooting RAW because it grants you creative and technical control downstream.

Once the raw data is in hand, the processing pipeline unfolds across three stages. First, *demosaicing*—the algorithmic reconstruction of full-color information from the sensor's color filter array. Second, *nondestructive editing*—the application of exposure, white balance, saturation, and localized corrections using mathematical transforms that never destroy the underlying data. Third, *output encoding*—the final compression and color space transformation that converts your edits into a deliverable file format (JPEG, ProRes, DNG, TIFF).

The invariant principle underlying this entire workflow is *preserve optionality*. Each stage should maintain the maximum amount of information and reversibility. A RAW file preserves more than a JPEG. A 16-bit TIFF preserves more than an 8-bit JPEG. A nondestructive adjustment layer preserves more than a destructive pixel edit. The further you progress through the pipeline toward final output, the more irreversible your choices become. Conversely, the earlier in the pipeline you work with data, the more freedom you retain to change direction.

This lesson focuses on three interconnected domains: understanding sensor characteristics and their impact on capture; navigating the technical and aesthetic choices within RAW demosaicing and nondestructive editing; and encoding your final output with full knowledge of compression tradeoffs, color space transformations, and format constraints.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

### Sensor Architecture and Photon-to-Electron Conversion

Your camera's sensor is an array of photodiodes, typically arranged in a grid of millions of photosites. Each photosite accumulates electrical charge proportional to the number of photons it receives during the exposure. This charge is then converted to a voltage, amplified by the camera's gain circuitry, and quantized into a digital value by the analog-to-digital (ADC) converter. The sensor's architecture directly determines what information you capture and what you lose.

Most modern digital cameras use a Bayer pattern color filter array: a checkerboard of red, green, and blue filters placed over the photosites. This is economical and ubiquitous, but it means each photosite captures only one color channel. At position (row, column), you might have a green photosite, while the adjacent pixel captures red. This spatial separation of color information is the core reason demosaicing algorithms exist—they must intelligently reconstruct the missing color channels at each pixel location.

The Bayer array has a known weakness: it oversamples the green channel (roughly 50% of photosites are green) compared to red and blue (each ~25%). This mimics human vision, where our eyes are more sensitive to luminance (which correlates with green) than to chrominance. However, this imbalance means green channels typically carry lower noise and higher detail, while red and blue channels are noisier and less resolute. When you white-balance in post-production or adjust color temperature, you are essentially amplifying the noisier red and blue channels to compensate, which is why shooting under correct white balance or custom white balance is tactically important—it minimizes downstream noise amplification.

Sensor sensitivity is measured in ISO (International Organization for Standardization). Native ISO corresponds to the sensor's output without electronic gain amplification. When you raise ISO above native, the camera amplifies the voltage signal before quantization, which amplifies both signal and noise together. This is why higher ISO images are noisier: the noise floor itself is boosted. Conversely, lowering ISO below native (if your camera supports it) reduces amplification, yielding cleaner shadows but potentially clipping highlights more easily if your exposure is imprecise.

Dynamic range—the ratio of the brightest reproducible value to the darkest—is a hard physical limit of your sensor. Most modern DSLRs and mirrorless cameras offer 12 to 14 stops of dynamic range in RAW. This means the brightest value the sensor can record is roughly 4,000 to 16,000 times brighter than the darkest. In-camera JPEG compression often reduces this to 8 to 10 stops because it applies tone mapping to fit the wide range into an 8-bit per-channel format. By shooting RAW, you preserve the full sensor dynamic range, deferring the tone mapping decision to post-production where you have creative control.

### RAW File Format and Demosaicing

A RAW file is a container for the sensor's native output. The most common formats are Canon's CR2, Nikon's NEF, and the open-source DNG (Digital Negative), which Adobe championed as a vendor-agnostic standard. Inside the RAW container is the raw sensor data (the Bayer array as integer values), metadata (white balance, ISO, exposure time, lens information), and often a small embedded JPEG preview for quick thumbnail display.

The raw sensor data itself is typically stored as 12-bit or 14-bit per channel, meaning each photosite's value ranges from 0 to 4,095 (12-bit) or 0 to 16,383 (14-bit). This is fundamentally different from the 8-bit per channel (0–255) of a standard JPEG. The extra bit depth provides finer tonal gradation and, critically, greater flexibility for adjusting exposure and recovering shadow or highlight detail in post-production.

Demosaicing is the algorithmic process of inferring the missing color channels at each pixel. At a red photosite, the demosaicing algorithm must estimate what the green and blue values should be by interpolating from neighboring photosites. Simple demosaicing methods (bilinear interpolation) average nearby pixels of the target color. More sophisticated methods (edge-aware, directional) analyze local edge structure to avoid blurring across boundaries. The choice of demosaicing algorithm affects sharpness, color accuracy, and artifact patterns, particularly in areas with fine detail or high color contrast (like red fabric against cyan sky).

Modern RAW processing software (Adobe Lightroom, Capture One, Darktable) implements several demosaicing algorithms. Linear demosaicing is fast but produces artifacts. Edge-aware demosaicing (sometimes called "refined" or "pixel-aware") preserves fine detail better. High-end software uses machine learning–based demosaicing that can recover more detail and suppress aliasing. The choice is typically automatic, but understanding the tradeoff is useful: faster demosaicing sacrifices quality for speed; sophisticated demosaicing yields cleaner, sharper results but demands more processing power.

Once demosaiced, the image exists in linear RGB space—a mathematical representation where pixel values are proportional to the actual light intensities. This linear space is crucial for post-production because all your tonal and color adjustments (curves, levels, hue shifts) are mathematically coherent in linear space. Applying adjustments in linear space means that a doubling of the input value yields a proportional doubling of the output, maintaining physical consistency.

### Nondestructive Editing Pipeline

A nondestructive editing pipeline preserves the original RAW data and stores all adjustments as mathematical instructions rather than modifying pixels directly. This is analogous to layers and adjustment layers in Photoshop, but applied to the RAW workflow from the outset.

The editing pipeline typically unfolds in this order: white balance correction, exposure and black point adjustment, highlights and shadows recovery, vibrance and saturation, local adjustments (brushed corrections, graduated filters), and finally, output color space mapping.

**White Balance** is the first step. The camera records raw sensor values without assuming a particular light temperature. A tungsten bulb emits reddish light; daylight is bluer; fluorescent light is greenish. The white balance setting tells the demosaicing software how to scale the red, green, and blue channels relative to each other so that a neutral gray in the scene renders as neutral gray in the image. If you shoot with incorrect white balance, the color channels will be misaligned, and correcting it later requires amplifying one channel more than others, which amplifies channel-specific noise. Shooting with custom white balance (using a gray card reference) or Kelvin-specific white balance minimizes this noise penalty.

**Exposure and Black Point** adjustments shift the tonal range. Exposure compensation brightens or darkens the entire image by multiplying the linear RGB values by a constant factor. Black point adjustment sets the point below which values are clipped to zero, effectively raising the shadow floor. In linear space, exposure multiplication is clean and reversible; you can always undo it. However, extreme exposure changes can amplify noise or lose highlight detail, so the goal is to expose correctly at capture time and use post-production exposure correction only for minor adjustments (typically ±1 stop).

**Highlights and Shadows Recovery** leverages the sensor's full dynamic range. If your exposure clipped highlights during capture, those photosites were saturated and no recovery is possible—the data is lost. However, if highlights retained detail (raw values below sensor saturation), you can compress them back into the displayable range by applying a curve or tone mapping. Similarly, shadow detail that appears black in-camera may contain recoverable information in the RAW file. By lifting the black point or applying a shadow lift curve, you can reveal detail without amplifying noise excessively.

**Vibrance and Saturation** adjust color intensity. Vibrance is typically a perceptually intelligent saturation boost that increases color intensity while protecting skin tones and already-saturated colors from oversaturation. Saturation uniformly scales the chrominance (U and V channels in YUV color space, or equivalently, the distance from gray in HSL space). In nondestructive editing, these adjustments are stored as mathematical parameters, not as pixel modifications, so you can adjust them endlessly without quality loss.

**Local Adjustments** (graduated filters, brush strokes, radial masks) apply corrections to specific regions. A graduated neutral-density filter might darken the sky without affecting the foreground. A brush stroke might selectively increase saturation in a flower while leaving the background unchanged. These local adjustments are stored as layer masks and parameter sets, allowing you to modify or remove them later.

**Output Color Space Mapping** is the final transformation before export. The edited image in linear RGB must be transformed into a perceptually uniform color space (sRGB for screen display, ProPhoto RGB for archival TIFF, Adobe RGB for print) and compressed into the target bit depth (8-bit for JPEG, 16-bit for TIFF).

The nondestructive pipeline's power lies in its *reversibility and parametric flexibility*. You can adjust white balance without destroying color information because the adjustment is stored as a scale factor, not as modified pixels. You can change exposure without quality loss because the adjustment scales the entire linear dataset uniformly. Only when you export to a final format do you commit to specific pixel values, and by that point, you have explored all tonal and color possibilities while preserving the original RAW data intact.

### File Formats and Compression Tradeoffs

Your final output format encodes a fundamental tradeoff between file size, quality retention, and compatibility.

**JPEG (Joint Photographic Experts Group)** is ubiquitous and highly compressed. JPEG uses 8-bit per-channel quantization (256 levels per color), lossy Huffman coding, and chroma subsampling (typically 4:2:0, meaning color information is stored at 1/4 the spatial resolution of luminance). A typical JPEG at 90% quality compresses a megapixel image to 100–200 KB. This compression is aggressive but perceptually acceptable for most screen viewing. However, JPEG is destructive: once saved, you cannot recover the original RAW data. Repeated JPEG saves degrade quality further due to quantization errors accumulating. JPEG is appropriate for web distribution, social media, and final delivery to clients who do not require archival quality. It is inappropriate for your own master files or situations where you might need to re-export with different settings.

**TIFF (Tagged Image File Format)** is a flexible, largely uncompressed or losslessly compressed container. TIFF supports 8-bit, 16-bit, or 32-bit per-channel data, allowing you to preserve tonal gradation and color depth. A 16-bit TIFF of a megapixel image is roughly 6 MB uncompressed, or 2–3 MB with lossless LZW compression. TIFF is ideal for archival, print preparation, and handoff to other professionals because it preserves full color depth and is non-destructive. Many photographers use 16-bit TIFF as their intermediate master format: export from RAW software as a 16-bit TIFF, perform final touch-ups in Photoshop if needed, then export to JPEG or other final formats.

**DNG (Digital Negative)** is Adobe's open-source RAW container format, designed for long-term archival. A DNG file contains the raw sensor data, embedded color profile, and metadata in a standardized, well-documented format. Shooting and archiving in DNG (or converting your camera's native RAW format to DNG) provides future-proofing: if your camera manufacturer stops supporting their proprietary RAW format, DNG will remain readable because the specification is public. DNG files are larger than JPEG (typically 20–40 MB for a full-frame image) but smaller than uncompressed TIFF, and they preserve all the flexibility of RAW editing. For serious photographers, DNG archival is a best practice.

**ProRes (Apple ProRes)** and **DNxHR (Avid DNxHR)** are professional video container formats, but they are increasingly used for photographic workflows, especially in cinematography-adjacent work. ProRes supports 10-bit and 12-bit per-channel color with various compression profiles (ProRes 422 HQ for near-lossless, ProRes 422 for standard editing, ProRes 422 LT for reduced file size). These formats are optimized for fast playback and editing on professional hardware and are standard in color grading pipelines. If you are working in DaVinci Resolve or other video-centric grading software, exporting to ProRes maintains color precision and editing flexibility.

**H.265 (HEIC/HEIF)** is Apple's modern image container, offering superior compression to JPEG at equivalent perceived quality. An H.265 image at 70% quality often matches or exceeds a JPEG at 90% quality in subjective appearance while occupying 30–50% less disk space. H.265 uses modern entropy coding and intra-frame compression techniques. However, H.265 adoption outside the Apple ecosystem is slower than JPEG, and not all software supports it yet. H.265 is appropriate for screen delivery and modern digital workflows but less universal than JPEG for client handoff.

The file format decision hinges on your intended use. For archival, use 16-bit TIFF or DNG. For print preparation, use 16-bit TIFF in the appropriate color space (Adobe RGB or ProPhoto RGB). For screen delivery and social media, use JPEG at 85–92% quality (or H.265 at 60–75% if your audience supports it). For professional video color grading, use ProRes. For intermediate editing workflows where you might re-export, use 16-bit TIFF to preserve flexibility.

### Color Space Transformations and Gamut Mapping

Your final output must exist in a defined color space—a mathematical specification of how RGB values map to perceived colors. The three most common spaces are sRGB, Adobe RGB, and ProPhoto RGB.

**sRGB** is the standard for screen display and the internet. It was designed to match the color gamut and gamma response of typical consumer monitors from the 1990s. sRGB is a narrower color space than what modern cameras can capture; many saturated reds, greens, and cyans fall outside sRGB's reproducible range. When you export from RAW to sRGB JPEG for web distribution, colors outside sRGB are clipped or desaturated to fit within the gamut, resulting in visible color shifts. This is often acceptable because web viewers expect sRGB. However, for archival or print, you should preserve wider color spaces.

**Adobe RGB** is a wider color space designed for print production. It encompasses more saturated greens and cyans than sRGB, better matching what inkjet printers can reproduce. If your camera captures colors outside sRGB but within Adobe RGB, exporting to Adobe RGB TIFF preserves that color information for the print workflow. Displaying Adobe RGB on a typical sRGB monitor will appear desaturated because the monitor cannot display the full gamut, but the color data is preserved for the printer.

**ProPhoto RGB** is the widest standard color space, encompassing nearly all colors your camera can capture and more. ProPhoto is ideal for archival TIFF files because it ensures minimal color clipping. However, ProPhoto is rarely used for display or print delivery because its gamut extends beyond what any standard output device can reproduce.

The transformation from one color space to another involves mathematical matrix operations. If you export from ProPhoto to sRGB, out-of-gamut colors must be mapped into sRGB's reproducible range using a rendering intent. Perceptual rendering intent maps the entire ProPhoto range into sRGB while preserving relative hue shifts—useful for photographic images. Colorimetric rendering intent aims for exact color preservation for in-gamut colors but clips out-of-gamut colors abruptly—useful for logos or precise color matching.

Gamma is a related but distinct concept. Linear RGB (as stored in RAW files) has a gamma of 1.0, meaning doubling the input value doubles the output intensity. However, human vision perceives brightness nonlinearly; we are more sensitive to shadow changes than highlight changes. Display gamma (typically 2.2 for sRGB or around 2.0 for print media) compresses the linear signal so that equal steps in perceived brightness correspond to equal steps in pixel values. When you export to a standard color space, the software automatically applies the appropriate gamma curve, so you do not need to think about it explicitly. However, understanding gamma helps explain why shadows appear to shift more than highlights when you adjust exposure in post-production: gamma curves compress shadow values more densely than highlight values in perceptual space.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

### Optimizing the RAW Processing Workflow

Efficiency in RAW processing begins at capture. Every decision you make during shooting reduces the amount of correction needed in post-production, which accelerates processing and improves quality.

**Expose to the Right** is a core principle. Histograms show the distribution of tonal values. In RAW, the histogram extends from pure black (0) to sensor saturation (typically around 90% of the ADC maximum, to leave headroom). To maximize dynamic range and minimize noise, you should expose so that the histogram's right edge just barely touches saturation without clipping. This means the brightest important highlights (not necessarily the brightest pixel, which might be a specular reflection) occupy values near saturation. In post-production, you can compress the highlights downward to recover detail while keeping shadow noise minimal because the shadow detail occupies higher raw values (more signal relative to noise). Conversely, underexposing to preserve highlights throws valuable signal into the shadows where noise dominates. Expose Right leverages the nonlinear nature of sensor noise: doubling the signal quadruples the signal-to-noise ratio.

**White Balance Accuracy** matters because incorrect white balance requires amplifying the least-represented channel (usually blue or red). Shooting with a custom white balance on a gray card in the actual lighting ensures minimal channel imbalance and minimal noise amplification later. If shooting in mixed lighting (e.g., window light and tungsten), exposing for the dominant light source and correcting the secondary light source in post (via local adjustments) is more efficient than compromising white balance globally.

**Metadata and Naming Conventions** accelerate batch processing. If you shoot a series of portraits in the same lighting, correcting the first image's white balance and applying those settings to the entire batch via RAW processing software's sync or auto-sync features saves enormous time. This requires consistent naming and organization: segregate images by shoot date, location, and lighting condition so that batches are coherent.

**Selective Sharpening and Noise Reduction** should be deferred until after demosaicing and color correction. Most RAW software allows you to apply sharpening and noise reduction as post-demosaicing steps. Applying these selectively—perhaps only to the luminance channel, not to color channels—minimizes artifacts. Luminance-based noise reduction (which blurs color information minimally while reducing luminance grain) typically yields subjectively cleaner results than reducing noise across all channels uniformly.

### Handling Failure Modes and Edge Cases

**Blown Highlights and Shadow Noise** are the two primary failure modes of RAW capture and processing. Blown highlights occur when the scene's brightest areas exceed sensor saturation. Once clipped, no amount of post-processing can recover highlight detail—the sensor recorded only binary 1s (saturation) with no information. The solution is to expose more conservatively (expose Left) or use graduated neutral-density filters during capture to reduce the dynamic range of the scene. In-camera exposure bracketing (capturing the same scene at multiple exposures) is a tactical fallback: you can merge the correctly-exposed highlights from one frame with the correctly-exposed shadows from another via exposure fusion or HDR merging.

Shadow noise manifests as luminance grain or color noise in underexposed areas. RAW files preserve the noise structure, which becomes visible when you lift shadows in post-production. Noise reduction algorithms (typically bilateral filters or wavelet-based denoising) can suppress shadow grain, but aggressive noise reduction blurs fine detail. The balance is to apply gentle noise reduction, accepting some residual grain as a natural artifact of photography. Alternatively, shooting at higher ISO during the original exposure (despite higher inherent noise) often produces subjectively cleaner results than shooting at low ISO and then amplifying underexposed shadows, because the higher ISO amplifies the signal along with the noise, yielding better SNR.
