# Lesson 4: Illumination Models

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Rendering a three-dimensional scene onto a two-dimensional display fundamentally requires solving a problem of light transport: how do photons emitted from light sources interact with surfaces, bounce through an environment, and ultimately converge onto a virtual camera's sensor plane? The illumination model serves as the mathematical contract between physical optics and computational efficiency, trading strict adherence to electromagnetic wave propagation for numerically tractable approximations that produce visually plausible images at interactive frame rates.

At the heart of every illumination model lies the bidirectional reflectance distribution function, or BRDF, denoted $f_r(\omega_i, \omega_o)$, which describes the ratio of outgoing radiance in direction $\omega_o$ to incoming irradiance from direction $\omega_i$ at a surface point. The BRDF encapsulates material appearance: whether a surface is rough metal, polished glass, or diffuse clay. Energy conservation demands that the integral of the BRDF over the hemisphere of outgoing directions must not exceed unity, ensuring that surfaces cannot emit more light than they receive. This constraint forms an inviolable invariant across all physically plausible shading models.

The history of real-time illumination models follows a trajectory from local to global light transport. Local illumination models, pioneered in the 1970s and dominant in interactive graphics until the mid-2000s, evaluate lighting independently at each surface point without considering inter-reflections or occlusion from distant geometry. The Phong reflection model, introduced by Bui Tuong Phong in 1975, partitioned surface reflection into three additive components: ambient (constant environmental contribution), diffuse (Lambertian scattering proportional to $\cos\theta_i$), and specular (mirror-like highlights governed by the angle between the reflection vector and view direction). Blinn-Phong, proposed by Jim Blinn in 1977, substituted the halfway vector between light and view directions for the reflection vector, improving numerical stability and providing a closer approximation to microfacet theory at glancing angles.

Cook-Torrance, published in 1982, elevated BRDF modeling to a physically motivated framework grounded in microfacet theory. Every macroscopic surface comprises countless microscopic facets oriented according to a statistical distribution, typically modeled by the Beckmann or GGX (Trowbridge-Reitz) functions. The Cook-Torrance BRDF decomposes specular reflection into three multiplicative terms: the normal distribution function $D(\omega_h)$ describing facet orientation statistics, the geometric attenuation term $G(\omega_i, \omega_o)$ accounting for shadowing and masking among facets, and the Fresnel term $F(\omega_i, \omega_h)$ governing the fraction of incident light reflected versus refracted at each microfacet interface. This physically based rendering (PBR) paradigm has become the foundation of modern production pipelines, from game engines like Unreal and Unity to film renderers like Pixar's RenderMan and Industrial Light & Magic's Manuka.

Global illumination transcends local shading by solving the recursive problem of light bouncing among all surfaces in a scene. The rendering equation, formalized by James Kajiya in 1986, expresses outgoing radiance $L_o(\mathbf{p}, \omega_o)$ at point $\mathbf{p}$ as the sum of emitted radiance $L_e$ and the integral over the hemisphere $\Omega$ of incoming radiance $L_i$ weighted by the BRDF and the cosine term:

$$L_o(\mathbf{p}, \omega_o) = L_e(\mathbf{p}, \omega_o) + \int_{\Omega} f_r(\mathbf{p}, \omega_i, \omega_o) \, L_i(\mathbf{p}, \omega_i) \, (\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n}) \, d\omega_i$$

The recursion emerges because incoming radiance $L_i(\mathbf{p}, \omega_i)$ equals outgoing radiance $L_o(\mathbf{p}', -\omega_i)$ from the nearest surface point $\mathbf{p}'$ visible along direction $\omega_i$. Solving this Fredholm integral equation of the second kind for arbitrary scenes remains computationally intractable in closed form, necessitating numerical methods. Monte Carlo path tracing stochastically samples ray paths through the scene, accumulating contributions from light sources and indirect bounces until rays escape or terminate via Russian roulette. Radiosity, applicable to scenes with purely diffuse surfaces, discretizes the environment into surface patches and solves a linear system of radiant exitance values, capturing view-independent inter-reflections at the cost of geometric inflexibility.

Shadow algorithms determine visibility between surface points and light sources, partitioning lit regions from occluded ones. Hard shadows arise from infinitesimal point or directional lights, producing binary visibility. Soft shadows, generated by area lights, exhibit smooth penumbra transitions where partial occlusion gradually attenuates illumination. Shadow mapping, the dominant real-time technique, renders the scene from the light's perspective to a depth texture, then during final shading compares each fragment's light-space depth against the stored value to determine visibility. Percentage-closer filtering (PCF) averages multiple binary comparisons across a filter kernel to approximate soft shadow penumbrae, while variance shadow maps and moment shadow maps encode statistical depth distributions to accelerate wide-kernel filtering.

Ambient occlusion approximates the accessibility of ambient environmental lighting by measuring the fraction of the hemisphere above a surface point that remains unoccluded by nearby geometry. In the absence of explicit ambient light sources, ambient occlusion serves as a proximity cue, darkening surface creases, corners, and crevices to enhance depth perception and geometric detail. Screen-space ambient occlusion (SSAO) samples depth buffer values within a pixel's neighborhood to estimate local occlusion, trading global accuracy for per-frame performance suitable for real-time engines. Ray-traced ambient occlusion, by contrast, casts hemispherical ray queries against the full scene geometry, providing ground-truth occlusion at the expense of ray traversal overhead.

The dichotomy between local and global illumination reflects a broader tension in real-time graphics: the trade-off between physical correctness and temporal budget. Local models execute in microseconds per pixel, enabling 60 Hz or 144 Hz frame rates on consumer hardware, but sacrifice indirect lighting, color bleeding, and caustics. Global illumination delivers cinematic realism by simulating light's recursive propagation, yet naive path tracing requires thousands of samples per pixel to converge, far exceeding real-time budgets. Modern hybrid pipelines synthesize both paradigms: pre-baking static radiosity into lightmaps or spherical harmonic irradiance probes, applying screen-space reflections and ambient occlusion for localized effects, and allocating remaining compute budget to sparse ray-traced samples denoised via temporal accumulation and spatial filtering.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The canonical local illumination model in production renderers remains Blinn-Phong, expressed as the sum of three additive terms evaluated per light source per shaded fragment. Let $\mathbf{L}$ denote the normalized direction from surface point $\mathbf{p}$ to the light source, $\mathbf{N}$ the surface normal, $\mathbf{V}$ the view direction toward the camera, and $\mathbf{H} = \text{normalize}(\mathbf{L} + \mathbf{V})$ the halfway vector. The diffuse component follows Lambert's law, $k_d \, (\mathbf{N} \cdot \mathbf{L})$, scaling the material's diffuse albedo $k_d$ by the cosine of the incident angle. The specular highlight depends on the alignment between the surface normal and the halfway vector, $k_s \, (\mathbf{N} \cdot \mathbf{H})^{\alpha}$, where $\alpha$ controls the shininess or specular exponent. The ambient term, $k_a \, I_a$, adds a constant contribution independent of geometry. The complete Blinn-Phong equation for a single light source with intensity $I$ becomes:

$$L_o = k_a \, I_a + I \left[ k_d \, (\mathbf{N} \cdot \mathbf{L}) + k_s \, (\mathbf{N} \cdot \mathbf{H})^{\alpha} \right]$$

Negative dot products clamp to zero, ensuring surfaces facing away from the light receive no illumination. Multiple light sources sum their contributions additively, though this linearity assumption breaks down under high dynamic range conditions where tone mapping introduces non-linear response curves.

A production Blinn-Phong fragment shader in GLSL dissects these components into modular operations, facilitating permutation variants for directional, point, and spot lights. The shader inputs include interpolated per-fragment normals, view-space or world-space positions, material parameters, and light structure arrays. The output writes to framebuffer color attachments, optionally contributing to deferred shading G-buffers or forward-plus light accumulation.

```glsl
#version 450 core

layout(location = 0) in vec3 fragPosition;
layout(location = 1) in vec3 fragNormal;
layout(location = 2) in vec2 fragTexCoord;

layout(location = 0) out vec4 outColor;

struct Light {
    vec3 position;
    vec3 color;
    float intensity;
    int type; // 0=directional, 1=point, 2=spot
};

layout(std140, binding = 0) uniform CameraBlock {
    mat4 viewMatrix;
    mat4 projectionMatrix;
    vec3 cameraPosition;
};

layout(std140, binding = 1) uniform MaterialBlock {
    vec3 diffuseAlbedo;
    vec3 specularAlbedo;
    float shininess;
    float ambientStrength;
};

layout(std140, binding = 2) uniform LightBlock {
    Light lights[8];
    int numLights;
    vec3 ambientColor;
};

layout(binding = 0) uniform sampler2D diffuseTexture;

vec3 computeBlinnPhong(vec3 position, vec3 normal, vec3 viewDir, Light light) {
    vec3 lightDir;
    float attenuation = 1.0;
    
    if (light.type == 0) { // Directional
        lightDir = normalize(-light.position);
    } else if (light.type == 1) { // Point
        vec3 lightVector = light.position - position;
        float distance = length(lightVector);
        lightDir = lightVector / distance;
        attenuation = 1.0 / (1.0 + 0.09 * distance + 0.032 * distance * distance);
    } else { // Spot
        vec3 lightVector = light.position - position;
        float distance = length(lightVector);
        lightDir = lightVector / distance;
        attenuation = 1.0 / (1.0 + 0.09 * distance + 0.032 * distance * distance);
    }
    
    float NdotL = max(dot(normal, lightDir), 0.0);
    
    vec3 halfwayDir = normalize(lightDir + viewDir);
    float NdotH = max(dot(normal, halfwayDir), 0.0);
    float specular = pow(NdotH, shininess);
    
    vec3 diffuse = diffuseAlbedo * NdotL;
    vec3 spec = specularAlbedo * specular;
    
    return (diffuse + spec) * light.color * light.intensity * attenuation;
}

void main() {
    vec3 normal = normalize(fragNormal);
    vec3 viewDir = normalize(cameraPosition - fragPosition);
    
    vec3 texColor = texture(diffuseTexture, fragTexCoord).rgb;
    vec3 ambient = ambientStrength * ambientColor * texColor;
    
    vec3 lighting = ambient;
    for (int i = 0; i < numLights; i++) {
        lighting += computeBlinnPhong(fragPosition, normal, viewDir, lights[i]) * texColor;
    }
    
    outColor = vec4(lighting, 1.0);
}
```

Cook-Torrance microfacet BRDF refines specular reflection through physically motivated terms. The normal distribution function, typically GGX, defines the probability density of microfacet normals aligned with the halfway vector. GGX, parameterized by roughness $\alpha$, exhibits longer tails than Beckmann, producing more realistic highlights on rough surfaces:

$$D_{GGX}(\mathbf{H}) = \frac{\alpha^2}{\pi \left[ (\mathbf{N} \cdot \mathbf{H})^2 (\alpha^2 - 1) + 1 \right]^2}$$

The geometry function $G$ approximates the fraction of microfacets unoccluded by neighboring facets for both incoming and outgoing directions. Smith's shadowing-masking function, separable into independent terms $G_1(\omega_i)$ and $G_1(\omega_o)$, pairs with GGX as:

$$G_{Smith}(\mathbf{L}, \mathbf{V}, \mathbf{H}) = G_1(\mathbf{L}) \, G_1(\mathbf{V})$$

$$G_1(\omega) = \frac{2 (\mathbf{N} \cdot \omega)}{(\mathbf{N} \cdot \omega) + \sqrt{\alpha^2 + (1 - \alpha^2)(\mathbf{N} \cdot \omega)^2}}$$

Fresnel reflectance, derived from Fresnel equations for dielectric and conductor interfaces, governs the angle-dependent balance between reflection and refraction. Schlick's approximation provides a computationally efficient polynomial fit:

$$F_{Schlick}(\mathbf{L}, \mathbf{H}) = F_0 + (1 - F_0)(1 - \mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{H})^5$$

where $F_0$ represents the specular reflectance at normal incidence, typically 0.04 for dielectrics and higher for metals. The complete Cook-Torrance specular BRDF assembles these terms as:

$$f_{spec}(\mathbf{L}, \mathbf{V}) = \frac{D(\mathbf{H}) \, G(\mathbf{L}, \mathbf{V}, \mathbf{H}) \, F(\mathbf{L}, \mathbf{H})}{4 (\mathbf{N} \cdot \mathbf{L}) (\mathbf{N} \cdot \mathbf{V})}$$

The denominator's normalization factor ensures energy conservation. Diffuse reflection typically uses Lambertian $f_{diff} = \frac{\rho}{\pi}$, though Disney's principled BRDF and Oren-Nayar models add roughness-dependent retroreflection for improved realism.

A production Cook-Torrance implementation in GLSL structures material properties as a metallic-roughness workflow, where metallic values interpolate between dielectric and conductor Fresnel curves, and roughness directly parameterizes $\alpha$.

```glsl
vec3 fresnelSchlick(float cosTheta, vec3 F0) {
    return F0 + (1.0 - F0) * pow(clamp(1.0 - cosTheta, 0.0, 1.0), 5.0);
}

float distributionGGX(vec3 N, vec3 H, float roughness) {
    float a = roughness * roughness;
    float a2 = a * a;
    float NdotH = max(dot(N, H), 0.0);
    float NdotH2 = NdotH * NdotH;
    
    float num = a2;
    float denom = (NdotH2 * (a2 - 1.0) + 1.0);
    denom = 3.14159265359 * denom * denom;
    
    return num / denom;
}

float geometrySchlickGGX(float NdotV, float roughness) {
    float r = (roughness + 1.0);
    float k = (r * r) / 8.0;
    
    float num = NdotV;
    float denom = NdotV * (1.0 - k) + k;
    
    return num / denom;
}

float geometrySmith(vec3 N, vec3 V, vec3 L, float roughness) {
    float NdotV = max(dot(N, V), 0.0);
    float NdotL = max(dot(N, L), 0.0);
    float ggx2 = geometrySchlickGGX(NdotV, roughness);
    float ggx1 = geometrySchlickGGX(NdotL, roughness);
    
    return ggx1 * ggx2;
}

vec3 computeCookTorrance(vec3 position, vec3 normal, vec3 viewDir, Light light, 
                         vec3 albedo, float metallic, float roughness) {
    vec3 lightDir;
    float attenuation = 1.0;
    
    if (light.type == 0) {
        lightDir = normalize(-light.position);
    } else {
        vec3 lightVector = light.position - position;
        float distance = length(lightVector);
        lightDir = lightVector / distance;
        attenuation = 1.0 / (1.0 + 0.09 * distance + 0.032 * distance * distance);
    }
    
    vec3 H = normalize(viewDir + lightDir);
    
    vec3 F0 = vec3(0.04);
    F0 = mix(F0, albedo, metallic);
    
    float NDF = distributionGGX(normal, H, roughness);
    float G = geometrySmith(normal, viewDir, lightDir, roughness);
    vec3 F = fresnelSchlick(max(dot(H, viewDir), 0.0), F0);
    
    vec3 kS = F;
    vec3 kD = vec3(1.0) - kS;
    kD *= 1.0 - metallic;
    
    vec3 numerator = NDF * G * F;
    float denominator = 4.0 * max(dot(normal, viewDir), 0.0) * max(dot(normal, lightDir), 0.0) + 0.0001;
    vec3 specular = numerator / denominator;
    
    float NdotL = max(dot(normal, lightDir), 0.0);
    vec3 radiance = light.color * light.intensity * attenuation;
    
    return (kD * albedo / 3.14159265359 + specular) * radiance * NdotL;
}
```

Shadow mapping transforms visibility determination into a depth comparison problem. The light-space transformation projects scene geometry onto the light's view frustum, rasterizing depth values into a shadow map texture. During shading, each fragment's world-space position transforms into light-space coordinates, yielding a depth value that compares against the shadow map lookup. Depth values closer than the stored reference indicate occlusion, while farther values confirm visibility.

```glsl
// Shadow map generation vertex shader
#version 450 core

layout(location = 0) in vec3 position;

layout(std140, binding = 0) uniform LightSpaceBlock {
    mat4 lightSpaceMatrix;
};

void main() {
    gl_Position = lightSpaceMatrix * vec4(position, 1.0);
}

// Shadow map generation fragment shader
#version 450 core

void main() {
    // Depth written automatically to gl_FragDepth
}

// Main rendering with shadow sampling
float sampleShadowMap(sampler2D shadowMap, vec4 fragPosLightSpace) {
    vec3 projCoords = fragPosLightSpace.xyz / fragPosLightSpace.w;
    projCoords = projCoords * 0.5 + 0.5;
    
    if (projCoords.z > 1.0)
        return 0.0;
    
    float closestDepth = texture(shadowMap, projCoords.xy).r;
    float currentDepth = projCoords.z;
    
    float bias = 0.005;
    float shadow = currentDepth - bias > closestDepth ? 1.0 : 0.0;
    
    return shadow;
}

float sampleShadowMapPCF(sampler2D shadowMap, vec4 fragPosLightSpace, vec2 texelSize) {
    vec3 projCoords = fragPosLightSpace.xyz / fragPosLightSpace.w;
    projCoords = projCoords * 0.5 + 0.5;
    
    if (projCoords.z > 1.0)
        return 0.0;
    
    float currentDepth = projCoords.z;
    float shadow = 0.0;
    float bias = 0.005;
    
    for (int x = -1; x <= 1; ++x) {
        for (int y = -1; y <= 1; ++y) {
            vec2 offset = vec2(x, y) * texelSize;
            float pcfDepth = texture(shadowMap, projCoords.xy + offset).r;
            shadow += currentDepth - bias > pcfDepth ? 1.0 : 0.0;
        }
    }
    shadow /= 9.0;
    
    return shadow;
}
```

Screen-space ambient occlusion samples the depth buffer in a hemispherical kernel around each fragment, accumulating occlusion based on the number of samples lying closer to the camera than the surface tangent plane would predict. The original Crytek SSAO algorithm, and its improved variant HBAO (horizon-based ambient occlusion), cast rays in screen space and compare depth discontinuities to estimate local geometric complexity.

```glsl
#version 450 core

layout(location = 0) in vec2 texCoord;
layout(location = 0) out float outOcclusion;

layout(binding = 0) uniform sampler2D depthTexture;
layout(binding = 1) uniform sampler2D normalTexture;
layout(binding = 2) uniform sampler2D noiseTexture;

layout(std140, binding = 0) uniform SSAOKernel {
    vec3 samples[64];
    int kernelSize;
    float radius;
    float bias;
};

layout(std140, binding = 1) uniform CameraBlock {
    mat4 projection;
    mat4 view;
    mat4 invProjection;
};

vec3 reconstructPositionFromDepth(vec2 uv, float depth) {
    vec4 clipSpace = vec4(uv * 2.0 - 1.0, depth * 2.0 - 1.0, 1.0);
    vec4 viewSpace = invProjection * clipSpace;
    return viewSpace.xyz / viewSpace.w;
}

void main() {
    float depth = texture(depthTexture, texCoord).r;
    vec3 fragPos = reconstructPositionFromDepth(texCoord, depth);
    vec3 normal = normalize(texture(normalTexture, texCoord).xyz * 2.0 - 1.0);
    
    vec2 noiseScale = textureSize(depthTexture, 0) / 4.0;
    vec3 randomVec = normalize(texture(noiseTexture, texCoord * noiseScale).xyz * 2.0 - 1.0);
    
    vec3 tangent = normalize(randomVec - normal * dot(randomVec, normal));
    vec3 bitangent = cross(normal, tangent);
    mat3 TBN = mat3(tangent, bitangent, normal);
    
    float occlusion = 0.0;
    for (int i = 0; i < kernelSize; ++i) {
        vec3 samplePos = TBN * samples[i];
        samplePos = fragPos + samplePos * radius;
        
        vec4 offset = projection * vec4(samplePos, 1.0);
        offset.xyz /= offset.w;
        offset.xy = offset.xy * 0.5 + 0.5;
        
        float sampleDepth = texture(depthTexture, offset.xy).r;
        vec3 sampleFragPos = reconstructPositionFromDepth(offset.xy, sampleDepth);
        
        float rangeCheck = smoothstep(0.0, 1.0, radius / abs(fragPos.z - sampleFragPos.z));
        occlusion += (sampleFragPos.z >= samplePos.z + bias ? 1.0 : 0.0) * rangeCheck;
    }
    
    occlusion = 1.0 - (occlusion / float(kernelSize));
    outOcclusion = occlusion;
}
```

The rendering equation's recursive integral structure admits Monte Carlo solution via path tracing. Each camera ray generates a sequence of random scattering events, accumulating light contributions from emissive surfaces and sampling the BRDF to determine bounce directions. Importance sampling aligns random directions with the BRDF's probability distribution, reducing variance by concentrating samples where the integrand contributes most significantly. For diffuse surfaces, cosine-weighted hemisphere sampling generates directions proportional to $\cos\theta$. For specular lobes, importance sampling the GGX distribution directly yields efficient convergence.

```cpp
#include <cmath>
#include <random>
#include <algorithm>

struct Vec3 {
    float x, y, z;
    Vec3(float x = 0, float y = 0, float z = 0) : x(x), y(y), z(z) {}
    Vec3 operator+(const Vec3& v) const { return Vec3(x + v.x, y + v.y, z + v.z); }
    Vec3 operator-(const Vec3& v) const { return Vec3(x - v.x, y - v.y, z - v.z); }
    Vec3 operator*(float t) const { return Vec3(x * t, y * t, z * t); }
    Vec3 operator*(const Vec3& v) const { return Vec3(x * v.x, y * v.y, z * v.z); }
    float dot(const Vec3& v) const { return x * v.x + y * v.y + z * v.z; }
    Vec3 cross(const Vec3& v) const { return Vec3(y * v.z - z * v.y, z * v.x - x * v.z, x * v.y - y * v.x); }
    Vec3 normalize() const { float len = std::sqrt(x * x + y * y + z * z); return Vec3(x / len, y / len, z / len); }
    float length() const { return std::sqrt(x * x + y * y + z * z); }
};

struct Ray {
    Vec3 origin, direction;
    Ray(const Vec3& o, const Vec3& d) : origin(o), direction(d.normalize()) {}
};

struct Material {
    Vec3 albedo;
    float metallic;
    float roughness;
    Vec3 emission;
};

struct HitRecord {
    Vec3 point;
    Vec3 normal;
    float t;
    Material material;
};

std::mt19937 rng(42);
std::uniform_real_distribution<float> uniform(0.0f, 1.0f);

float random_float() {
    return uniform(rng);
}

Vec3 random_in_unit_sphere() {
    while (true) {
        Vec3 p(random_float() * 2.0f - 1.0f, random_float() * 2.0f - 1.0f, random_float() * 2.0f - 1.0f);
        if (p.dot(p) < 1.0f) return p;
    }
}

Vec3 random_cosine_direction() {
    float r1 = random_float();
    float r2 = random_float();
    float z = std::sqrt(1.0f - r2);
    float phi = 2.0f * 3.14159265359f * r1;
    float x = std::cos(phi) * std::sqrt(r2);
    float y = std::sin(phi) * std::sqrt(r2);
    return Vec3(x, y, z);
}

Vec3 sample_ggx(Vec3 N, float roughness) {
    float a = roughness * roughness;
    float r1 = random_float();
    float r2 = random_float();
    
    float phi = 2.0f * 3.14159265359f * r1;
    float cosTheta = std::sqrt((1.0f - r2) / (1.0f + (a * a - 1.0f) * r2));
    float sinTheta = std::sqrt(1.0f - cosTheta * cosTheta);
    
    Vec3 H(std::cos(phi) * sinTheta, std::sin(phi) * sinTheta, cosTheta);
    
    Vec3 up = std::abs(N.z) < 0.999f ? Vec3(0, 0, 1) : Vec3(1, 0, 0);
    Vec3 tangent = up.cross(N).normalize();
    Vec3 bitangent = N.cross(tangent);
    
    return (tangent * H.x + bitangent * H.y + N * H.z).normalize();
}

float distribution_ggx(Vec3 N, Vec3 H, float roughness) {
    float a = roughness * roughness;
    float a2 = a * a;
    float NdotH = std::max(N.dot(H), 0.0f);
    float NdotH2 = NdotH * NdotH;
    
    float num = a2;
    float denom = NdotH2 * (a2 - 1.0f) + 1.0f;
    denom = 3.14159265359f * denom * denom;
    
    return num / denom;
}

float geometry_schlick_ggx(float NdotV, float roughness) {
    float r = roughness + 1.0f;
    float k = (r * r) / 8.0f;
    return NdotV / (NdotV * (1.0f - k) + k);
}

float geometry_smith(Vec3 N, Vec3 V, Vec3 L, float roughness) {
    float NdotV = std::max(N.dot(V), 0.0f);
    float NdotL = std::max(N.dot(L), 0.0f);
    return geometry_schlick_ggx(NdotV, roughness) * geometry_schlick_ggx(NdotL, roughness);
}

Vec3 fresnel_schlick(float cosTheta, Vec3 F0) {
    float x = 1.0f - cosTheta;
    float x2 = x * x;
    return F0 + (Vec3(1.0f, 1.0f, 1.0f) - F0) * (x2 * x2 * x);
}

Vec3 evaluate_brdf(Vec3 V, Vec3 L, Vec3 N, const Material& mat) {
    Vec3 H = (V + L).normalize();
    
    Vec3 F0(0.04f, 0.04f, 0.04f);
    F0 = F0 * (1.0f - mat.metallic) + mat.albedo * mat.metallic;
    
    float NDF = distribution_ggx(N, H, mat.roughness);
    float G = geometry_smith(N, V, L, mat.roughness);
    Vec3 F = fresnel_schlick(std::max(H.dot(V), 0.0f), F0);
    
    Vec3 kS = F;
    Vec3 kD = (Vec3(1.0f, 1.0f, 1.0f) - kS) * (1.0f - mat.metallic);
    
    float NdotL = std::max(N.dot(L), 0.0f);
    float NdotV = std::max(N.dot(V), 0.0f);
    
    Vec3 numerator = F * (NDF * G);
    float denominator = 4.0f * NdotV * NdotL + 0.0001f;
    Vec3 specular = numerator * (1.0f / denominator);
    
    return kD * mat.albedo * (1.0f / 3.14159265359f) + specular;
}

bool scene_intersect(const Ray& ray, HitRecord& rec);

Vec3 trace_path(const Ray& ray, int depth) {
    if (depth <= 0)
        return Vec3(0, 0, 0);
    
    HitRecord rec;
    if (!scene_intersect(ray, rec))
        return Vec3(0.135f, 0.206f, 0.235f); // Sky color
    
    Vec3 emission = rec.material.emission;
    
    Vec3 V = (ray.direction * -1.0f).normalize();
    Vec3 N = rec.normal;
    
    Vec3 scattered;
    if (rec.material.roughness > 0.5f) {
        Vec3 local_dir = random_cosine_direction();
        Vec3 up = std::abs(N.z) < 0.999f ? Vec3(0, 0, 1) : Vec3(1, 0, 0);
        Vec3 tangent = up.cross(N).normalize();
        Vec3 bitangent = N.cross(tangent);
        scattered = (tangent * local_dir.x + bitangent * local_dir.y + N * local_dir.z).normalize();
    } else {
        Vec3 H = sample_ggx(N, rec.material.roughness);
        scattered = (H * (H.dot(V) * 2.0f) - V).normalize();
    }
    
    Ray scattered_ray(rec.point + N * 0.001f, scattered);
    Vec3 incoming = trace_path(scattered_ray, depth - 1);
    
    Vec3 brdf = evaluate_brdf(V, scattered, N, rec.material);
    float pdf = std::max(N.dot(scattered), 0.0f) / 3.14159265359f;
    
    return emission + brdf * incoming * std::max(N.dot(scattered), 0.0f) * (1.0f / pdf);
}
```

Radiosity formulates global illumination for purely diffuse environments as a linear system. Each surface patch $i$ emits radiant exitance $B_i$, composed of self-emission $E_i$ and reflected irradiance from all other patches:

$$B_i = E_i + \rho_i \sum_{j=1}^{n} F_{ij} B_j$$

The form factor $F_{ij}$ quantifies the fraction of energy leaving patch $i$ that arrives at patch $j$, accounting for geometric visibility, distance attenuation, and projected solid angles. Hemicube rasterization computes form factors by rendering the scene from each patch's center onto the five faces of a hemicube surrounding it, accumulating fractional areas weighted by distance and orientation. The resulting system $\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{E} + \mathbf{R} \mathbf{F} \mathbf{B}$ rearranges to $(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{R} \mathbf{F}) \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{E}$, solvable via Gauss-Seidel iteration or progressive refinement. Hierarchical radiosity accelerates form factor computation by clustering distant patches and exploiting spatial coherence.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Real-time illumination models demand relentless optimization across shader arithmetic, memory bandwidth, and numerical stability. Blinn-Phong's low arithmetic intensity—approximately twenty scalar operations per light per fragment—scales linearly with light count, forcing deferred shading or clustered forward rendering to amortize geometry processing costs. Deferred shading rasterizes geometric attributes (position, normal, albedo) into a geometry buffer, then executes lighting in a fullscreen pass that samples these textures, decoupling lighting complexity from geometric complexity. Bandwidth pressure arises from multiple render targets: a minimal G-buffer comprising RGB albedo, XYZ normal, and depth consumes forty bits per pixel at 1920x1080, totaling 8.3 MB per frame. Compressed normal encodings, such as octahedral or spheremap transforms, reduce storage to sixteen bits without perceptual loss.

Cook-Torrance BRDF evaluation incurs substantially higher arithmetic cost: the GGX normal distribution function requires one division and one costly reciprocal square root for normalization, while the Smith geometry term demands two additional reciprocal operations. Modern GPUs mitigate division latency via pipelined floating-point units, yet divergent execution paths within warps still impose throughput penalties. Specular aliasing emerges when high-frequency normal variation from tangent-space normal maps interacts with low-resolution BRDF sampling, producing flickering highlights that violate Nyquist sampling. LEAN mapping and LEADR techniques pre-filter normal distributions into anisotropic roughness parameters, trading per-pixel normal detail for stable specular responses at distance.

Shadow map resolution dictates penumbra fidelity and aliasing severity. A 2048x2048 shadow map covering a 100-meter square scene yields 4.88 cm texels, insufficient to resolve contact shadows for small objects. Cascaded shadow maps partition the view frustum into distance-dependent slices, allocating higher resolution to near geometry and coarser samples to distant regions. Percentage-closer soft shadows (PCSS) adapt filter kernel size based on blocker distance, approximating physically correct penumbra widening as occluders move away from receivers. The blocker search phase samples a disk of shadow map texels to estimate average blocker depth, then scales the PCF kernel proportionally. This two-pass approach doubles texture fetches, stressing memory bandwidth and cache coherence.

Variance shadow maps (VSM) exploit Chebyshev's inequality to upper-bound occlusion probability from first and second depth moments, enabling hardware-accelerated mipmap filtering for arbitrarily wide kernels. However, light bleeding artifacts occur when bimodal depth distributions violate VSM's single-surface assumption, causing erroneous illumination where multiple occluders overlap. Moment shadow maps extend VSM to four or six moments, reconstructing piecewise distributions that suppress light bleeding at the cost of increased storage and more complex reconstruction.

Screen-space ambient occlusion suffers from three fundamental failure modes: depth discontinuity false occlusion, missing geometry occlusion, and temporal instability. Depth discontinuities at object silhouettes cause background samples to incorrectly occlude foreground surfaces, producing dark halos around characters. Bilateral filtering and depth-aware weighting mitigate this by discarding samples whose depth difference exceeds a threshold, though this introduces its own artifacts at shallow grazing angles. Missing geometry occlusion arises because SSAO operates exclusively on rasterized depth, ignoring off-screen occluders and backfaces. Ground-truth ambient occlusion requires hemispherical ray queries against the full scene, motivating hybrid approaches that trace sparse rays and denoise aggressively. Temporal instability manifests as flickering when camera motion causes sampling kernels to traverse different depth buffer regions frame-over-frame. Temporal accumulation with exponential moving averages stabilizes the result, yet introduces ghosting trails during rapid camera pans or object motion.

Path tracing convergence rates determine feasibility for real-time applications. Naive uniform hemisphere sampling requires thousands of samples per pixel to reduce variance below perceptible thresholds, far exceeding one-sample-per-pixel budgets at 60 Hz. Next event estimation (NEE) explicitly samples light sources each bounce, drastically reducing variance for direct illumination. Multiple importance sampling (MIS) combines BRDF sampling and light sampling via heuristic weighting, ensuring robustness across glossy and rough materials. Denoising algorithms, particularly those leveraging machine learning such as NVIDIA's OptiX AI-Accelerated Denoiser or Intel's Open Image Denoise, reconstruct smooth lighting from one to four samples per pixel by exploiting spatial and temporal coherence. Temporal reprojection accumulates samples across frames, exponentially increasing effective sample count, yet disocclusion introduces ghosting that requires variance-guided clamping and history rejection.

Light transport simulation accuracy depends on correctly modeling Fresnel effects at dielectric interfaces. Omitting Fresnel entirely results in overly bright specular highlights that violate energy conservation, while using constant Fresnel approximations misses angle-dependent reflectance variation crucial for water, glass, and polished plastics. Schlick's approximation introduces minimal error for most dielectrics, but conductors require complex-valued refractive indices and the full Fresnel equations for physically accurate response. Index-of-refraction mismatches at vacuum-dielectric and dielectric-conductor boundaries further complicate layered materials, necessitating multi-layer BRDF models or explicit volumetric path tracing through stratified media.

Numerical precision issues plague illumination calculations throughout the pipeline. Single-precision floating-point depth buffers exhibit insufficient resolution at far clip planes, causing depth fighting and Z-acne in shadow maps. Reversed depth, mapping zero to the far plane and one to the near plane, exploits floating-point's logarithmic precision distribution to improve depth discrimination. Shadow acne, caused by insufficient depth bias, manifests as self-shadowing artifacts where surfaces incorrectly occlude themselves due to rasterization and filtering errors. Slope-scaled bias adapts the offset proportional to the surface's orientation relative to the light, while second-depth shadow maps explicitly store the second closest depth layer to eliminate self-intersection.

Radiosity's $O(n^2)$ form factor computation and $O(n^3)$ matrix inversion prohibit interactive performance for dense scene tessellations. Progressive refinement iterates only the most energetically significant patches each frame, converging toward equilibrium over seconds rather than solving the full system upfront. Hierarchical radiosity clusters distant patches into aggregate nodes, reducing effective resolution and accelerating form factor evaluations through bounding volume culling. Despite these optimizations, radiosity remains impractical for dynamic scenes where geometry or lighting changes per frame, limiting its application to static lightmap pre-computation for architectural visualizations and legacy game engines.

Physically based rendering workflows impose strict energy conservation and reciprocity constraints that artists must respect. Albedo values exceeding physical plausibility—pure white diffuse with high metallic values—produce unrealistic brightening under multiple bounces. Validation tools measure total hemispherical reflectance and clamp materials to ensure BRDF integrals remain below unity. Metallic and roughness authoring demands understanding of conductor versus dielectric Fresnel curves: metals exhibit colored specular reflectance derived from their complex refractive index, while dielectrics maintain achromatic specular with diffuse albedo determining color. Texture compression artifacts, particularly in BC1 (DXT1) and BC3 (DXT5) formats, introduce quantization noise that manifests as banding in smooth roughness gradients and color shifts in albedo maps. BC7 and ASTC offer superior rate-distortion trade-offs for PBR textures, though decompression bandwidth remains a bottleneck on memory-constrained platforms.

Hardware-accelerated ray tracing via DirectX Raytracing (DXR) or Vulkan Ray Tracing fundamentally transforms illumination architecture. Dedicated RT cores in NVIDIA's Turing and Ampere GPUs execute bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) traversal and ray-triangle intersection at fixed-function throughput, offloading these operations from shader cores. A hybrid rendering pipeline casts shadow rays for direct lighting, reflection rays for specular surfaces, and ambient occlusion rays for diffuse surfaces, reserving rasterization for primary visibility. BVH construction and updates consume significant preprocessing time: static geometry leverages high-quality offline builders with surface area heuristic (SAH) optimization, while dynamic objects require incremental refitting or partial rebuilds each frame. Top-level acceleration structures (TLAS) reference bottom-level structures (BLAS) via instance transforms, enabling efficient handling of instanced geometry without duplicating BVH nodes.

Ray divergence within GPU warps remains a critical performance pitfall. Rays originating from neighboring pixels that scatter into vastly different directions suffer warp divergence, serializing BVH traversal and reducing occupancy. Sorting rays by direction before tracing improves coherence, though sorting overhead can exceed traversal savings for incoherent workloads. Inline ray tracing, introduced in Shader Model 6.6, exposes BVH traversal as intrinsic functions within compute shaders, enabling custom traversal loops and early-out optimizations tailored to specific illumination algorithms. The trade-off between fixed-function traversal speed and inline traversal flexibility depends on scene complexity, ray coherence, and material diversity.

Ambient occlusion radius selection involves a perceptual trade-off: small radii emphasize contact shadows and fine geometric detail but miss large-scale occlusion from nearby walls or overhangs, while large radii capture environmental scale but blur local creases into uniform darkness. Adaptive radius scaling based on depth or geometric curvature balances detail and scale, though it introduces additional shader complexity and non-uniform memory access patterns that degrade cache efficiency. HBAO+, NVIDIA's optimized SSAO variant, intersects rays with depth buffer horizon angles to compute occlusion analytically, reducing sample counts from 16-64 to 4-8 per pixel while maintaining comparable quality.

The uncanny valley of illumination occurs when photorealistic materials and geometry combine with approximate lighting, producing images that look almost but not quite physically correct. Subsurface scattering in skin, velvet's retroreflective sheen, and iridescent thin-film interference require specialized BRDF extensions beyond Cook-Torrance microfacets. Diffusion profiles for subsurface scattering integrate light transport through participating media, requiring screen-space convolution or pre-integrated lookup tables. Anisotropic reflectance, prevalent in brushed metals and hair, replaces isotropic GGX with elliptical distributions parameterized by tangent and bitangent roughness. Clearcoat layers, modeling automotive paints and lacquered woods, stack an additional specular lobe atop the base BRDF, each with independent roughness and Fresnel terms.

Production rendering pipelines synthesize local and global illumination through baked and dynamic light splits. Static geometry receives pre-computed radiosity or path-traced lightmaps authored offline in tools like Lightmass (Unreal) or Progressive Lightmapper (Unity), capturing inter-reflections, soft shadows, and color bleeding at bake time. Dynamic objects query irradiance probes—sparse grids of spherical harmonics or octahedral lightmaps—for approximated indirect lighting, then apply real-time direct lighting and shadows. Reflection probes, cubemaps rendered from strategic viewpoints, provide view-independent specular reflections, though parallax correction via proxy volumes reduces misalignment artifacts for non-distant reflectors. The dichotomy between static and dynamic lighting introduces consistency challenges: baked indirect light cannot react to dynamic emitters, and dynamic shadows cannot account for baked occlusion, necessitating artist-driven compromise and hybrid fallback strategies.

Illumination model selection depends on target platform capabilities, aesthetic goals, and performance budgets. Mobile GPUs with limited ALU throughput and bandwidth favor simplified Blinn-Phong or mobile-optimized PBR with pre-integrated BRDF lookup tables. Console and PC platforms with teraflop-scale compute and hardware ray tracing support full Cook-Torrance microfacet models, screen-space reflections, and hybrid ray-traced shadows. Architectural visualization and film rendering prioritize offline path tracing with thousands of samples per pixel, emphasizing physical accuracy over interactive performance. Each domain navigates the fundamental trade-off between computational cost and visual fidelity, leveraging domain-specific priors—static lighting in architecture, controlled camera paths in film, player-driven viewpoints in games—to optimize the solution space.
