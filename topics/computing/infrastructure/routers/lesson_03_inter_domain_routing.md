# Lesson 3: Border Gateway Protocol - Autonomous Systems, Policy, Route Propagation

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The Internet does not operate as a single monolithic network but rather as a federation of independent administrative domains called Autonomous Systems. Each AS—whether a multinational internet service provider, a cloud hyperscaler, or a regional enterprise network—manages its own internal routing infrastructure and makes sovereign decisions about how traffic enters and exits its borders. The Border Gateway Protocol emerged in 1989 precisely to solve the coordination problem inherent in this decentralized architecture: how can tens of thousands of independently operated networks exchange routing information without centralized authority, without trusting each other's internal topologies, and without creating routing loops across administrative boundaries?

BGP fundamentally differs from interior gateway protocols like OSPF or IS-IS because it operates not on link-state flooding or distance-vector metrics, but on policy-based path selection. While OSPF routers within an AS collaborate to compute shortest paths based on link costs, BGP speakers at AS boundaries negotiate reachability based on commercial relationships, traffic engineering objectives, and administrative preferences. A BGP router does not simply advertise "I can reach 203.0.113.0/24 in three hops"—it advertises "I can reach this prefix via the following sequence of autonomous systems, and here are the attributes that describe this path's properties." The receiving AS then applies its own local policy to decide whether to accept that route, prefer it over alternatives, or reject it entirely based on business logic that has nothing to do with hop count or latency.

This policy-centric design creates an elegant invariant: BGP routes carry not just destination prefixes but complete provenance metadata. Every BGP UPDATE message includes the AS_PATH attribute, an ordered list of every AS the route advertisement has traversed. This path vector prevents routing loops through a simple rule—if a BGP speaker sees its own AS number in the AS_PATH of an incoming route, it silently discards the advertisement because accepting it would create a cycle. No distributed loop-detection algorithm is needed; the path vector itself encodes sufficient topological history.

The protocol's stateful nature distinguishes it from ephemeral interior protocols. BGP sessions run over TCP port 179, establishing long-lived connections between peering routers that can persist for months or years. Once the TCP three-way handshake completes and BGP speakers exchange OPEN messages to negotiate capabilities, they transition into the Established state and begin exchanging routing information. The initial convergence involves a full table transfer where each speaker advertises all routes in its Routing Information Base, which for a full internet routing table in 2025 means transmitting roughly 950,000 IPv4 prefixes and 180,000 IPv6 prefixes. After this initial sync, the protocol operates incrementally—routers send UPDATE messages only when routes change, and KEEPALIVE messages every 30-60 seconds to maintain session liveness.

The economic and political topology of AS relationships creates three fundamental peering models that govern how routes propagate across the internet. In a customer-provider relationship, the customer pays the provider for transit—the ability to reach the entire internet through the provider's network. The customer advertises its own prefixes and those of its downstream customers to the provider, and the provider advertises the full internet routing table to the customer. In a peer-to-peer settlement-free relationship, two networks of roughly equivalent size agree to exchange traffic destined for each other's customers without payment, but neither advertises its providers' routes to the other—doing so would allow the peer to use you for free transit to reach the broader internet. In a sibling relationship, typically between different ASes controlled by the same organization, all routes are shared bidirectionally.

These relationship semantics enforce the valley-free routing property that maintains internet economics. A valid BGP path can ascend from customer to provider zero or more times, traverse at most one peer-to-peer link at the top of the hierarchy, and then descend from provider to customer zero or more times—but it cannot "go up" after "going down" because that would imply a provider giving free transit to reach another provider. BGP routers enforce this through export policies: customer routes are advertised to everyone (providers, peers, and other customers), peer routes are advertised only to customers, and provider routes are advertised only to customers. These policies, combined with Local Preference manipulation, create the economic gravity that causes traffic to prefer customer routes over peer routes over provider routes.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

A BGP speaker maintains three distinct databases that together implement the path-selection and policy-application logic. The Adj-RIB-In stores all routes received from each neighbor before any policy processing, preserving the raw information advertised by peers. The Loc-RIB contains the single best route chosen for each prefix after applying the BGP decision algorithm, representing the router's authoritative view of the network topology. The Adj-RIB-Out holds the routes that will be advertised to each neighbor after applying outbound policy filters and attribute modifications. This three-stage architecture cleanly separates policy from protocol mechanics—inbound policy filters transform Adj-RIB-In entries before best-path selection populates the Loc-RIB, and outbound policy filters transform Loc-RIB entries before advertisements populate each neighbor's Adj-RIB-Out.

The BGP UPDATE message format reflects this attribute-centric design. Each UPDATE carries a variable-length sequence of withdrawn routes, a variable-length sequence of path attributes, and a variable-length sequence of reachable Network Layer Reachability Information prefixes. The critical insight is that attributes apply to all NLRI prefixes in the same UPDATE message, enabling efficient batch advertisements—announcing 100 prefixes with identical attributes requires only one attribute set transmission. The ORIGIN attribute indicates whether the route originated from an IGP (IGP origin, most preferred), from EGP (EGP origin, deprecated), or was redistributed into BGP (INCOMPLETE origin, least preferred). The AS_PATH attribute encodes the sequence of autonomous systems as a list of AS_SEQUENCE segments for normal path propagation, and AS_SET segments when routes are aggregated. The NEXT_HOP attribute specifies the IP address of the border router that should be used as the next hop for forwarding, which critically may differ from the IP address of the BGP peer itself when route reflectors or confederation boundaries are involved.

The BGP decision algorithm implements a deterministic, hierarchical tiebreaker chain that ensures every router in an AS converges to consistent forwarding decisions. For a given prefix, the router first filters out routes with unreachable next hops or invalid attributes. Among remaining candidates, it prefers the route with the highest LOCAL_PREF, an attribute that network operators set inbound to encode business relationships—customer routes typically receive LOCAL_PREF 200, peer routes 150, and provider routes 100. If LOCAL_PREF values tie, the router selects the path with the shortest AS_PATH length, implementing the protocol's default distance-vector behavior. When AS path lengths match, the router compares ORIGIN codes, preferring IGP over EGP over INCOMPLETE. Subsequent tiebreakers evaluate MULTI_EXIT_DISC to honor a neighbor's preference for which entry point to use when multiple links connect the ASes, then prefer eBGP-learned routes over iBGP-learned routes to minimize internal path length, then select the path with the lowest IGP cost to the BGP next hop, and finally break remaining ties using the lowest router ID to ensure determinism.

This decision algorithm reveals a subtle but critical design tension. BGP's policy expressiveness allows operators to override default behavior at every stage—LOCAL_PREF can be manipulated based on arbitrary match conditions, AS_PATH can be artificially lengthened through prepending to make a route less attractive, and communities can tag routes with metadata that triggers policy actions at distant ASes. However, this flexibility creates the possibility of persistent route oscillation when conflicting policies interact across AS boundaries. Consider three ASes arranged in a triangle where AS1 prefers to reach a prefix in AS3 via AS2, AS2 prefers to reach it via AS3 directly, but AS3 prefers to route back through AS1. Each router independently selects its best path based on local policy, advertises it to neighbors, receives updated advertisements, recomputes best paths with the new information, and the cycle repeats indefinitely.

The protocol's designers addressed oscillation through two complementary mechanisms: the route flap damping algorithm suppresses routes that exhibit rapid withdrawal-announcement cycles by exponentially increasing suppression penalties until a configurable reuse threshold permits the route to return to consideration, and BGP's inherent message delays create natural convergence dampening since updates propagate at timescales of seconds to minutes rather than milliseconds. More fundamentally, the routing system remains stable in practice because most policy conflicts resolve through economic relationships—the customer-provider hierarchy creates a natural acyclic flow where customers prefer their own announcements, accept provider defaults as last resort, and peers exchange routes only for their respective customer cones. Operators enforce this structure through systematic filtering: providers typically accept only their customers' prefixes plus more-specific routes from the customer's downstream cone, peers filter to accept only the partner's customer routes and reject transit paths, and customers accept default routes or full tables depending on their sophistication. The Gao-Rexford sufficient conditions for safety formalize this intuition: if every AS prefers customer routes over peer routes over provider routes, and exports customers to everyone but exports peers and providers only to customers, then the global routing system converges to a unique stable state without persistent oscillation.

This interplay between protocol mechanics and economic reality demonstrates BGP's design philosophy—rather than attempting to prevent arbitrary policy expression or guarantee convergence through algorithmic constraints, the protocol provides primitives powerful enough to encode real-world business relationships and relies on rational economic incentives to prevent pathological configurations. Route propagation thus becomes an emergent property of distributed policy enforcement: the RIB-IN, filtering, best-path selection, policy application, and RIB-OUT sequence executes independently at every router, yet the composition of these local decisions produces global reachability that reflects the Internet's hierarchical structure. The result is a system where route announcements flow outward from origin ASes through customer-provider relationships, spread laterally through peering relationships at similar tiers, and aggregate toward Tier-1 networks that form the Internet's default-free zone—a collection of ASes that maintain full routing tables and peer with each other rather than purchasing transit.
