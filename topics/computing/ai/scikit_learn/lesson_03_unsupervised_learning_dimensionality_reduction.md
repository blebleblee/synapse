# Lesson 3: Unsupervised Learning, Clustering, Dimensionality Reduction

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Supervised learning is a contract between the engineer and the data: you supply labels, the algorithm learns a mapping. Unsupervised learning dissolves that contract entirely. The dataset presents itself as a raw manifold of points in $\mathbb{R}^d$, and the task is to find structure that was never explicitly declared — clusters of similar observations, low-dimensional surfaces hiding inside high-dimensional ambients, latent factors whose linear combinations reconstruct the observed variance. This is simultaneously more intellectually honest and more computationally treacherous than supervised work, because there is no ground truth to validate against during the learning process itself. The engineer must bring stronger geometric intuition and more disciplined experimental hygiene to compensate.

The unifying invariant across all unsupervised methods is a **distance or similarity measure**. Every clustering algorithm, every manifold learner, every matrix decomposition makes implicit or explicit assumptions about what "close" means in the feature space. Getting that assumption wrong — applying Euclidean distance to count data, or cosine similarity to features with meaningful zero magnitudes — produces mathematically coherent but semantically meaningless structure. The experienced practitioner treats metric selection as a first-class hyperparameter, not a default to accept blindly.

### Distance Metrics: The Geometry of Similarity

The Euclidean metric $d(p, q) = \sqrt{\sum_i (p_i - q_i)^2}$ is the natural choice when features are continuous, homoscedastic, and measured on commensurate scales. Its deep problem is sensitivity to dimension. As $d$ grows, the ratio of the maximum to minimum pairwise distance in a randomly drawn sample converges toward 1 — the phenomenon Robert Hecht-Nielsen called the **curse of dimensionality**. All points begin to look equally far apart. This makes Euclidean clustering in raw, unscaled, high-dimensional spaces nearly useless; PCA or variance normalization is not optional preparation, it is a prerequisite.

The Manhattan metric $d(p, q) = \sum_i |p_i - q_i|$ degrades more gracefully in high dimension because it does not square the per-dimension contributions. It is the natural metric for $L_1$-regularized models and aligns with the K-Medoids objective. The Minkowski family $d_r(p, q) = \left(\sum_i |p_i - q_i|^r\right)^{1/r}$ parameterizes the continuous range from Manhattan ($r=1$) through Euclidean ($r=2$) to Chebyshev ($r \to \infty$), and scikit-learn exposes this through the `minkowski` metric with a configurable `p` parameter.

Cosine similarity $\cos(\theta) = \frac{p \cdot q}{\|p\| \|q\|}$ decouples magnitude from direction, making it appropriate for text embeddings and any representation where the absolute scale of a vector carries no meaning — only the angle between documents matters. Its complement $1 - \cos(\theta)$ is the cosine distance, though strictly it is not a metric (it violates the triangle inequality for certain configurations). The Mahalanobis distance $d_M(p, q) = \sqrt{(p-q)^T \Sigma^{-1} (p-q)}$ corrects for feature correlation and scale simultaneously by whitening the space with the inverse covariance $\Sigma^{-1}$, effectively rendering the distribution spherical before measuring Euclidean distance. It is the ideal metric when features have known covariance structure, but requires $n \gg d$ to estimate $\Sigma$ reliably.

### Clustering: What Structure Are We Really Claiming?

Three distinct geometric hypotheses underlie the major clustering families. The centroid hypothesis assumes that clusters are compact, roughly convex regions separated by empty space, and that each cluster can be summarized by a single prototype point. K-Means and K-Medoids instantiate this hypothesis. The density hypothesis assumes that clusters are contiguous high-density regions separated by low-density corridors — no assumption of convexity, no assumption of similar size or shape. DBSCAN and HDBSCAN instantiate this hypothesis. The probabilistic hypothesis assumes that observations were generated by a mixture of parametric distributions, each with its own mean and covariance. Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) instantiate this, and the EM algorithm is their learning engine.

Each hypothesis is a prior about the shape of the world. A practitioner who applies K-Means to crescent-shaped clusters has not made a computational error — they have made a modeling error, one that no amount of tuning will fix.

### Dimensionality Reduction: Manifold Hypothesis and Linear Algebra

The manifold hypothesis is the foundational claim beneath dimensionality reduction: high-dimensional data, while technically living in $\mathbb{R}^d$, actually concentrates near a lower-dimensional manifold of intrinsic dimension $k \ll d$. A sequence of MNIST images lives in $\mathbb{R}^{784}$, but the generative factors — stroke angle, pen width, digit identity — occupy far fewer degrees of freedom. Dimensionality reduction attempts to find a coordinate system aligned with that manifold.

PCA finds the globally optimal linear manifold. It identifies the $k$ orthogonal directions of maximum variance in the data by computing the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, or equivalently via Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of the centered data matrix. This linear constraint is simultaneously its strength and its limitation: it is exact, deterministic, and interpretable, but it cannot unroll a Swiss Roll or separate concentric rings. Nonlinear methods — t-SNE, UMAP, Isomap — replace the linear constraint with a neighborhood graph and optimize for geometric fidelity at different scales.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

### PCA and the SVD Pipeline

Consider a data matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ where $n$ is the number of samples and $d$ the number of features. The standard PCA procedure centers the data as $\tilde{X} = X - \bar{X}$, then computes the thin SVD:

$$\tilde{X} = U \Sigma V^T$$

where $U \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$ contains the left singular vectors (sample coordinates), $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k}$ is the diagonal matrix of singular values $\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_k > 0$, and $V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ contains the right singular vectors — the **principal components** (the directions in feature space). The explained variance of the $i$-th component is $\sigma_i^2 / (n-1)$, and the projection of $\tilde{X}$ onto the first $k$ components is simply $\tilde{X} V_k = U_k \Sigma_k$.

Scikit-learn's `PCA` uses LAPACK's `_gesdd` driver for exact full SVD when $\min(n, d)$ is small. For large $n$ and $d$, it switches to a randomized SVD (Halko, Martinsson, Tropp 2009) via `svd_solver='randomized'`, which projects the data onto a random Gaussian sketch of size $(k + \text{oversampling})$, runs exact SVD on that small matrix, and back-projects. The error bound is $O(\sigma_{k+1})$ — the approximation quality degrades only at the boundary of the kept spectrum. For streaming or truly massive datasets, `IncrementalPCA` processes data in minibatches, maintaining running estimates of the principal components without ever materializing the full matrix in memory.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA, TruncatedSVD, IncrementalPCA
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.datasets import fetch_openml
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Load MNIST (70,000 samples x 784 features)
mnist = fetch_openml('mnist_784', version=1, as_frame=False, parser='auto')
X, y = mnist.data.astype(np.float32), mnist.target

# Standardize: PCA is variance-based, so scale matters
scaler = StandardScaler()
X_scaled = scaler.fit_transform(X)

# --- Exact PCA (uses randomized SVD by default for d=784) ---
pca = PCA(n_components=50, svd_solver='randomized', random_state=42)
X_pca = pca.fit_transform(X_scaled)

# Cumulative explained variance to choose k
cumvar = np.cumsum(pca.explained_variance_ratio_)
k_95 = np.searchsorted(cumvar, 0.95) + 1
print(f"Components to explain 95% variance: {k_95}")
print(f"X_pca shape: {X_pca.shape}")  # (70000, 50)

# --- Incremental PCA for memory-constrained pipelines ---
ipca = IncrementalPCA(n_components=50, batch_size=1000)
for batch_start in range(0, len(X_scaled), 1000):
    ipca.partial_fit(X_scaled[batch_start:batch_start + 1000])
X_ipca = ipca.transform(X_scaled)

# --- TruncatedSVD: does NOT center, appropriate for sparse matrices ---
# For sparse TF-IDF or count matrices, centering destroys sparsity.
# TruncatedSVD (LSA) operates directly on the sparse structure.
from scipy.sparse import csr_matrix
X_sparse = csr_matrix(X)  # simulate sparse scenario
svd = TruncatedSVD(n_components=50, algorithm='randomized', n_iter=5, random_state=42)
X_svd = svd.fit_transform(X_sparse)
print(f"TruncatedSVD explained variance ratio sum: {svd.explained_variance_ratio_.sum():.3f}")
```

The architectural distinction between `PCA` and `TruncatedSVD` matters deeply in production. `PCA` subtracts the column means before decomposing, which destroys sparsity when the input is a `scipy.sparse` matrix — a 10GB sparse TF-IDF matrix becomes a dense 10GB matrix in memory. `TruncatedSVD` skips centering, making it the standard tool for Latent Semantic Analysis on document-term matrices.

### K-Means: Lloyd's Algorithm and Its Failure Modes

K-Means minimizes the within-cluster sum of squared Euclidean distances (WCSS):

$$\mathcal{L} = \sum_{j=1}^{K} \sum_{x_i \in C_j} \|x_i - \mu_j\|^2$$

Lloyd's algorithm alternates between two steps: assign each point to the nearest centroid, then recompute each centroid as the mean of its assigned points. This is a coordinate descent on $\mathcal{L}$ that is guaranteed to converge but not to find the global minimum. The initialization strategy is therefore critical. The naive random initialization has $O(K)$ probability of placing two initial centroids in the same true cluster, leading to degenerate local minima. K-Means++ (Arthur & Vassilvitskii, 2007) samples the first centroid uniformly, then samples each subsequent centroid with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest already-chosen centroid. This provides an $O(\log K)$ approximation guarantee on the final objective and is scikit-learn's default (`init='k-means++'`).

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans, MiniBatchKMeans
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score, davies_bouldin_score
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Synthetic ground-truth clusters for validation
X, y_true = make_blobs(
    n_samples=5000,
    centers=6,
    cluster_std=[0.8, 1.2, 0.5, 1.0, 0.7, 0.9],
    random_state=42
)

scaler = StandardScaler()
X_scaled = scaler.fit_transform(X)

# K-Means with k-means++ init, multiple restarts
km = KMeans(
    n_clusters=6,
    init='k-means++',
    n_init=20,          # run 20 independent restarts, keep best WCSS
    max_iter=300,
    tol=1e-4,
    algorithm='lloyd',  # exact Lloyd; 'elkan' uses triangle inequality for speed
    random_state=42
)
labels = km.fit_predict(X_scaled)

print(f"Inertia (WCSS): {km.inertia_:.2f}")
print(f"Silhouette Score: {silhouette_score(X_scaled, labels):.4f}")
print(f"Davies-Bouldin Index: {davies_bouldin_score(X_scaled, labels):.4f}")

# --- Elbow Method: find optimal K ---
inertias = []
sil_scores = []
K_range = range(2, 12)
for k in K_range:
    km_k = KMeans(n_clusters=k, init='k-means++', n_init=10, random_state=42)
    km_k.fit(X_scaled)
    inertias.append(km_k.inertia_)
    sil_scores.append(silhouette_score(X_scaled, km_k.labels_))

# --- MiniBatchKMeans for large-scale data (n > 100k) ---
mbkm = MiniBatchKMeans(
    n_clusters=6,
    batch_size=1024,
    init='k-means++',
    n_init=10,
    max_iter=100,
    reassignment_ratio=0.01,  # fraction of centers below which reassignment triggers
    random_state=42
)
mbkm.fit(X_scaled)
print(f"MiniBatch Inertia: {mbkm.inertia_:.2f}")
```

K-Means has three structural assumptions that the practitioner must audit before deploying: clusters are isotropic (spherical) in feature space, all clusters have similar variance, and all clusters contain roughly similar numbers of points. When these assumptions fail — elongated clusters, vastly different densities, ring-shaped structure — the algorithm will segment geometry incorrectly regardless of initialization quality or iteration budget.

### DBSCAN and HDBSCAN: Density-Connected Components

DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) defines a cluster as a maximal set of density-connected points. A point $p$ is a **core point** if its $\varepsilon$-neighborhood $N_\varepsilon(p) = \{q : d(p, q) \leq \varepsilon\}$ contains at least `min_samples` points. A point $q$ is **directly density-reachable** from $p$ if $q \in N_\varepsilon(p)$ and $p$ is a core point. Two points are **density-connected** if there exists a chain of directly density-reachable points linking them. Points that are not density-reachable from any core point are classified as noise (label $-1$).

The algorithm requires no prior specification of $K$, discovers clusters of arbitrary shape, and handles noise naturally. Its critical weakness is uniform $\varepsilon$: in datasets with clusters of variable density, no single $\varepsilon$ correctly identifies all clusters simultaneously. HDBSCAN (Campello, Moulavi, Sander 2013) resolves this by computing a cluster hierarchy over all density levels and extracting the most stable clusters — those that persist across the widest range of density thresholds.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons, make_blobs
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# DBSCAN excels on non-convex geometry that destroys K-Means
X_moons, _ = make_moons(n_samples=1000, noise=0.08, random_state=42)
X_moons_scaled = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X_moons)

# Heuristic: plot k-distance graph (k = min_samples - 1) to find elbow -> epsilon
k = 4  # min_samples - 1
nbrs = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=k).fit(X_moons_scaled)
distances, _ = nbrs.kneighbors(X_moons_scaled)
k_distances = np.sort(distances[:, -1])[::-1]  # descending

# The "elbow" in k_distances visually suggests epsilon
# Here we set epsilon based on inspection of the elbow
db = DBSCAN(eps=0.15, min_samples=5, metric='euclidean', algorithm='ball_tree', n_jobs=-1)
labels_db = db.fit_predict(X_moons_scaled)

n_clusters = len(set(labels_db)) - (1 if -1 in labels_db else 0)
n_noise = np.sum(labels_db == -1)
print(f"DBSCAN clusters found: {n_clusters}")
print(f"Noise points: {n_noise} ({100*n_noise/len(labels_db):.1f}%)")

# --- Mixed density scenario: HDBSCAN via scikit-learn 1.3+ ---
# scikit-learn >= 1.3 ships HDBSCAN natively
from sklearn.cluster import HDBSCAN

X_mixed, _ = make_blobs(n_samples=[300, 300, 50], cluster_std=[0.3, 0.3, 0.1], random_state=42)
X_mixed_scaled = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X_mixed)

hdb = HDBSCAN(
    min_cluster_size=20,       # minimum points to constitute a cluster
    min_samples=5,             # core point threshold (controls robustness to noise)
    cluster_selection_epsilon=0.0,  # 0 = pure HDBSCAN hierarchy; >0 blends with DBSCAN
    metric='euclidean',
    cluster_selection_method='eom'  # 'eom' (Excess of Mass) vs 'leaf'
)
labels_hdb = hdb.fit_predict(X_mixed_scaled)
print(f"HDBSCAN clusters: {len(set(labels_hdb)) - (1 if -1 in labels_hdb else 0)}")
print(f"HDBSCAN noise points: {np.sum(labels_hdb == -1)}")
```

The `algorithm` parameter in DBSCAN controls the spatial index: `'ball_tree'` and `'kd_tree'` reduce neighborhood queries from $O(n^2)$ to $O(n \log n)$ on low-dimensional data, while `'brute'` is necessary when the metric is non-Euclidean or the dimensionality exceeds roughly 20 (where tree structures degenerate toward linear scan anyway).

### Gaussian Mixture Models: Soft Assignment via EM

A GMM models the data as a superposition of $K$ Gaussian components:

$$p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \, \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k)$$

where $\pi_k$ are the mixing weights ($\sum_k \pi_k = 1$), $\mu_k$ are component means, and $\Sigma_k$ are component covariance matrices. The Expectation-Maximization algorithm maximizes the log-likelihood by iterating between the **E-step** — computing posterior responsibilities $r_{ik} = p(z_k \mid x_i) \propto \pi_k \mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k)$ — and the **M-step** — updating parameters as weighted maximum likelihood estimates using those responsibilities. This is a soft generalization of K-Means: K-Means is exactly the degenerate limit of EM with spherical, equal-variance, equal-weight Gaussians as the covariance shrinks to zero.

The `covariance_type` parameter is architecturally significant. `'full'` fits a complete $d \times d$ covariance matrix per component, capturing all correlations but requiring $O(Kd^2)$ parameters and $O(Kd^3)$ inversion cost per iteration. `'diag'` fits only diagonal covariances, forcing independence between features but scaling as $O(Kd)$. `'spherical'` forces a single variance per component, and `'tied'` shares one full covariance across all components. The correct choice depends on the dimensionality, available data, and domain knowledge about cluster geometry.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture, BayesianGaussianMixture
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs

X, y_true = make_blobs(
    n_samples=1500,
    centers=[[-3, -3], [0, 0], [3, 2], [-1, 3]],
    cluster_std=[0.5, 0.8, 0.4, 0.6],
    random_state=42
)
X_scaled = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

# --- GMM with full covariance ---
gmm = GaussianMixture(
    n_components=4,
    covariance_type='full',
    init_params='k-means++',    # initialize means via k-means++ (scikit-learn >= 1.1)
    n_init=5,                   # multiple EM restarts
    max_iter=200,
    tol=1e-4,
    reg_covar=1e-6,             # regularization added to diagonal of covariance
    random_state=42
)
gmm.fit(X_scaled)
labels_gmm = gmm.predict(X_scaled)
proba_gmm = gmm.predict_proba(X_scaled)  # soft assignments (n_samples, n_components)

print(f"GMM converged: {gmm.converged_}")
print(f"Log-likelihood: {gmm.lower_bound_:.4f}")
print(f"BIC: {gmm.bic(X_scaled):.2f}")
print(f"AIC: {gmm.aic(X_scaled):.2f}")

# BIC for model selection: penalizes number of parameters more heavily than AIC
# Lower BIC/AIC = better model (penalized likelihood)
bic_scores = []
for k in range(2, 10):
    g = GaussianMixture(n_components=k, covariance_type='full',
                        n_init=5, random_state=42)
    g.fit(X_scaled)
    bic_scores.append((k, g.bic(X_scaled)))
best_k = min(bic_scores, key=lambda x: x[1])[0]
print(f"BIC-optimal K: {best_k}")

# --- Bayesian GMM: automatic component pruning ---
# Variational Bayes places a Dirichlet prior on mixing weights.
# Components that explain nothing get weight driven to 0.
bgmm = BayesianGaussianMixture(
    n_components=10,            # upper bound; model will prune unused components
    covariance_type='full',
    weight_concentration_prior_type='dirichlet_process',  # infinite mixture
    weight_concentration_prior=1e-2,  # small prior -> aggressive pruning
    max_iter=500,
    n_init=3,
    random_state=42
)
bgmm.fit(X_scaled)
active_components = np.sum(bgmm.weights_ > 0.01)
print(f"BayesianGMM active components: {active_components} (of 10 allocated)")
print(f"Component weights: {np.sort(bgmm.weights_)[::-1].round(3)}")
```

The Bayesian GMM with a Dirichlet process prior eliminates the need to specify $K$ as a hyperparameter. The concentration prior $\alpha$ controls how aggressively unused components are pruned: a small $\alpha$ (like $10^{-2}$) concentrates probability mass on fewer components, while a large $\alpha$ allows more components to survive. In practice this is preferable to exhaustive BIC search when computational budget is limited.

### Manifold Learning: t-SNE, UMAP, and Isomap

The execution model of t-SNE proceeds in two phases. In the first, it computes pairwise conditional probabilities $p_{j|i} = \frac{\exp(-\|x_i - x_j\|^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)}{\sum_{k \neq i} \exp(-\|x_i - x_k\|^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)}$, where $\sigma_i$ is chosen by binary search to match the target `perplexity` (a soft knob for local neighborhood size). The joint probability is symmetrized as $p_{ij} = (p_{j|i} + p_{i|j}) / 2n$. In the second phase, it places points in $\mathbb{R}^2$ randomly and defines a Student-t distribution $q_{ij} \propto (1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2)^{-1}$ over low-dimensional distances. It then minimizes the KL divergence $KL(P \| Q) = \sum_{i \neq j} p_{ij} \log(p_{ij}/q_{ij})$ via gradient descent with momentum. The heavy tail of the Student-t distribution allows moderately dissimilar points to map to much larger low-dimensional distances than the Gaussian high-dimensional distribution would imply, which is what creates the characteristic "spread" between clusters.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.manifold import TSNE, Isomap, LocallyLinearEmbedding
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

digits = load_digits()
X_digits = digits.data.astype(np.float32)
y_digits = digits.target

scaler = StandardScaler()
X_scaled = scaler.fit_transform(X_digits)

# PCA pre-reduction before t-SNE: standard production practice.
# t-SNE's O(n^2) complexity is prohibitive on 64 raw dims;
# PCA to 50 dims removes noise while preserving neighbor structure.
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
X_pca50 = PCA(n_components=50, svd_solver='randomized', random_state=42).fit_transform(X_scaled)

tsne = TSNE(
    n_components=2,
    perplexity=30,          # typical range 5-50; larger = broader neighborhoods
    learning_rate='auto',   # scikit-learn >= 1.2 sets lr = max(n/early_exaggeration, 200)
    n_iter=1000,
    n_iter_without_progress=300,
    early_exaggeration=12,  # amplifies cluster separation in early gradient descent
    metric='euclidean',
    init='pca',             # PCA init (default since 1.2) is more deterministic than 'random'
    n_jobs=-1,
    random_state=42
)
X_tsne = tsne.fit_transform(X_pca50)
print(f"t-SNE KL divergence: {tsne.kl_divergence_:.4f}")

# --- Isomap: geodesic distances via shortest path ---
# Appropriate when the manifold is smooth and the data is densely sampled.
# Computes k-NN graph, then all-pairs shortest-path (Floyd-Warshall or Dijkstra),
# then applies MDS on the geodesic distance matrix.
isomap = Isomap(
    n_neighbors=10,
    n_components=2,
    metric='minkowski',
    p=2,
    n_jobs=-1
)
X_isomap = isomap.fit_transform(X_scaled)
print(f"Isomap reconstruction error: {isomap.reconstruction_error():.4f}")

# --- Locally Linear Embedding ---
# Each point is reconstructed as a linear combination of its neighbors;
# the embedding preserves those coefficients in low-d.
lle = LocallyLinearEmbedding(
    n_neighbors=15,
    n_components=2,
    method='standard',   # 'modified', 'hessian', 'ltsa' are more robust variants
    n_jobs=-1,
    random_state=42
)
X_lle = lle.fit_transform(X_scaled)
print(f"LLE reconstruction error: {lle.reconstruction_error_:.6f}")

# Visualization: compare embeddings
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(18, 5))
scatter_kwargs = dict(c=y_digits, cmap='tab10', s=8, alpha=0.7)
axes[0].scatter(X_tsne[:, 0], X_tsne[:, 1], **scatter_kwargs)
axes[0].set_title('t-SNE (perplexity=30)')
axes[1].scatter(X_isomap[:, 0], X_isomap[:, 1], **scatter_kwargs)
axes[1].set_title('Isomap (k=10)')
axes[2].scatter(X_lle[:, 0], X_lle[:, 1], **scatter_kwargs)
axes[2].set_title('LLE (k=15)')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('manifold_comparison.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
```

A production note that many practitioners learn only after painful debugging: t-SNE distances between clusters are not interpretable. The algorithm does not preserve global geometry; it optimizes local neighborhood structure. Two clusters appearing close in a t-SNE plot are not necessarily similar in the original space. This makes t-SNE valuable for visual exploration and completely invalid as a basis for downstream similarity decisions. UMAP (available through the `umap-learn` package, which integrates seamlessly with scikit-learn's `Pipeline` via the `transform` interface) better preserves global structure at the cost of additional hyperparameters, and is preferred for production embedding pipelines.

### Non-negative Matrix Factorization and Sparse Decompositions

PCA decomposes additively with both positive and negative loadings, which can produce components that are mathematically orthogonal but semantically uninterpretable — a "face" minus a "hat" is not a natural part-based description of anything. Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) constrains both the component matrix $H$ and the coefficient matrix $W$ to be non-negative, forcing a parts-based, additive decomposition. For a data matrix $V \approx WH$ with $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}_+$ and $H \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times d}_+$, the components in $H$ represent features that add together — topics in documents, spectral peaks in audio, facial parts in images.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.decomposition import NMF, SparsePCA, FastICA, FactorAnalysis
from sklearn.preprocessing import MinMaxScaler
from sklearn.datasets import fetch_olivetti_faces
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Olivetti faces: 400 grayscale faces, 64x64 = 4096 dims
faces = fetch_olivetti_faces(shuffle=True, random_state=42)
X_faces = faces.data  # already in [0, 1]

# NMF requires non-negative input
nmf = NMF(
    n_components=20,
    init='nndsvda',         # Non-Negative Double SVD (better than random for small k)
    solver='cd',            # 'cd' (coordinate descent) or 'mu' (multiplicative update)
    beta_loss='frobenius',  # ||V - WH||_F^2; 'kullback-leibler' for count data
    max_iter=400,
    tol=1e-4,
    l1_ratio=0.0,           # 0=L2 only, 1=L1 only, interpolates elastic net
    alpha_W=0.1,            # regularization strength on W
    alpha_H=0.1,            # regularization strength on H
    random_state=42
)
W = nmf.fit_transform(X_faces)   # (400, 20) coefficient matrix
H = nmf.components_              # (20, 4096) component (basis) matrix

print(f"NMF reconstruction error: {nmf.reconstruction_err_:.4f}")
print(f"W shape: {W.shape}, H shape: {H.shape}")

# Components are interpretable face parts (eyes, nose, mouth, hairline)
fig, axes = plt.subplots(4, 5, figsize=(10, 8), subplot_kw={'xticks': [], 'yticks': []})
for ax, component in zip(axes.flat, H):
    ax.imshow(component.reshape(64, 64), cmap='hot', interpolation='nearest')
plt.suptitle('NMF Components (Face Parts)', y=1.02)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('nmf_faces.png', dpi=120, bbox_inches='tight')

# --- Sparse PCA: L1 penalty forces sparse loadings ---
# More interpretable than PCA for high-d feature selection problems
spca = SparsePCA(
    n_components=10,
    alpha=1.0,          # L1 regularization weight; higher = more zeros in components
    max_iter=200,
    n_jobs=-1,
    random_state=42
)
X_spca = spca.fit_transform(X_faces)
sparsity = np.mean(spca.components_ == 0)
print(f"SparsePCA component sparsity: {sparsity:.2%}")

# --- Independent Component Analysis ---
# Recovers statistically independent sources from mixed observations.
# Classic application: blind source separation (cocktail party problem).
ica = FastICA(
    n_components=20,
    algorithm='parallel',
    whiten='unit-variance',
    max_iter=500,
    tol=1e-4,
    random_state=42
)
X_ica = ica.fit_transform(X_faces)
print(f"ICA mixing matrix shape: {ica.mixing_.shape}")
```

### Production Pipeline Integration

In production, these components rarely live in isolation. The scikit-learn `Pipeline` API chains transformers and estimators into a single object that can be cross-validated, serialized, and deployed atomically. Wrapping a dimensionality reduction step inside a pipeline prevents the data leakage that occurs when PCA is fit on the full dataset before train/test splitting.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
from sklearn.base import BaseEstimator, TransformerMixin

# Custom scorer for clustering (silhouette, since there are no labels)
# cross_val_score requires a supervised estimator; for clustering pipelines,
# use a wrapper or evaluate post-fit.

X, y_true = make_blobs(n_samples=3000, centers=5, random_state=42)

# --- Full unsupervised pipeline ---
pipe = Pipeline([
    ('scaler', StandardScaler()),
    ('reducer', PCA(n_components=10, svd_solver='randomized', random_state=42)),
    ('cluster', KMeans(n_clusters=5, init='k-means++', n_init=15, random_state=42))
])

pipe.fit(X)
labels = pipe.named_steps['cluster'].labels_
X_transformed = pipe[:-1].transform(X)  # Apply all steps except final estimator

sil = silhouette_score(X_transformed, labels, metric='euclidean', sample_size=1000)
print(f"Pipeline silhouette (reduced space): {sil:.4f}")

# Access intermediate outputs
pca_step = pipe.named_steps['reducer']
print(f"PCA explained variance (10 components): {pca_step.explained_variance_ratio_.sum():.3f}")

# Persist the full pipeline
import joblib
joblib.dump(pipe, 'unsupervised_pipeline.joblib')
pipe_loaded = joblib.load('unsupervised_pipeline.joblib')
print(f"Loaded pipeline produces same labels: {np.all(pipe_loaded.predict(X) == pipe.predict(X))}")
```

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

### Scaling Laws and Algorithmic Complexity

Understanding where each algorithm's cost is concentrated dictates which optimizations are available. K-Means with Lloyd's algorithm costs $O(nKdt)$ where $t$ is the number of iterations. For $n > 10^5$, the solution is `MiniBatchKMeans`, which processes random subsets of size `batch_size` in each iteration. The convergence rate is slower per data point but the wall-clock time per iteration drops dramatically. The tradeoff is that minibatch inertia is 1–5% higher than full-batch inertia on well-separated clusters; on noisy or high-dimensional data, the gap is negligible.

DBSCAN with a ball-tree index costs $O(n \log n)$ in practice for low to moderate dimensions ($d \leq 20$) but degrades to $O(n^2)$ when the branching factor of the tree shrinks to 1 — which happens as $d$ increases. The `leaf_size` parameter controls the tree's leaf node capacity; larger values reduce tree depth but increase per-leaf linear scan cost. For high-dimensional data ($d > 30$), approximate nearest neighbors via libraries like `annoy` or `faiss` are necessary, with custom metric adapters bridging into scikit-learn's interface.

t-SNE's naive $O(n^2)$ pairwise distance computation becomes $O(n \log n)$ via the Barnes-Hut approximation (`method='barnes_hut'`, the default for $n > 1000$), which groups distant points into spatial cells treated as single repulsion sources. The approximation parameter `angle=0.5` controls the accuracy-speed tradeoff: smaller values approach exact computation; larger values are faster but coarser. Memory consumption for the pairwise probability matrix is $O(k \cdot n)$ where $k$ is determined by perplexity, making it feasible for datasets up to roughly $10^5$ points.

```python
import numpy as np
import time
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans, MiniBatchKMeans
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs

# Benchmark: KMeans vs MiniBatchKMeans at scale
n_samples_list = [5_000, 50_000, 500_000]
results = []

for n in n_samples_list:
    X, _ = make_blobs(n_samples=n, centers=8, random_state=42)
    X_s = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=8, n_init=3, max_iter=100, random_state=42)
    km.fit(X_s)
    t_km = time.perf_counter() - t0

    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    mbkm = MiniBatchKMeans(n_clusters=8, batch_size=2048, n_init=3, max_iter=100, random_state=42)
    mbkm.fit(X_s)
    t_mbkm = time.perf_counter() - t0

    results.append({
        'n': n,
        'km_time': t_km,
        'mbkm_time': t_mbkm,
        'km_inertia': km.inertia_,
        'mbkm_inertia': mbkm.inertia_,
        'inertia_ratio': mbkm.inertia_ / km.inertia_
    })

for r in results:
    print(f"n={r['n']:>8d} | KMeans: {r['km_time']:.2f}s | "
          f"MiniBatch: {r['mbkm_time']:.2f}s | "
          f"Inertia ratio: {r['inertia_ratio']:.4f}")
```

### Failure Mode: Feature Scale Contamination

The single most common production failure in clustering is deploying a model that was developed with scaled features onto a pipeline that skips the scaler during inference. Because scikit-learn transformers are stateful (the scaler stores mean and variance from `fit`), the correct pattern is always to include the scaler inside the `Pipeline` object so that serialization captures both the scaler state and the downstream model atomically. A scaler `fit` on training data and separately persisted will silently produce wrong results if the production data distribution shifts, with no error raised.

A related failure is applying PCA components computed on un-normalized features. PCA maximizes variance, so a feature with a numerical range of $[0, 10000]$ will dominate all components over features with range $[0, 1]$, regardless of their information content. The practical invariant is: `StandardScaler` before PCA, always, unless domain knowledge explicitly warrants otherwise (for example, in natural language processing, TF-IDF already normalizes by document length, and further scaling can distort the sparsity structure).

### Failure Mode: K-Means on Non-Globular Geometry

The following complete diagnostic example demonstrates the structured way to audit whether K-Means is geometrically appropriate before committing to it.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans, DBSCAN
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons, make_circles, make_blobs
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score, silhouette_score

def evaluate_clusterers(X, y_true, name):
    X_s = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

    km = KMeans(n_clusters=len(np.unique(y_true)), n_init=10, random_state=42)
    db = DBSCAN(eps=0.3, min_samples=10, n_jobs=-1)
    gmm = GaussianMixture(n_components=len(np.unique(y_true)),
                          covariance_type='full', n_init=5, random_state=42)

    labels_km = km.fit_predict(X_s)
    labels_db = db.fit_predict(X_s)
    labels_gmm = gmm.fit_predict(X_s)

    # ARI = 1.0 is perfect, 0.0 is random, negative is worse than random
    ari_km = adjusted_rand_score(y_true, labels_km)
    ari_db = adjusted_rand_score(y_true, labels_db)
    ari_gmm = adjusted_rand_score(y_true, labels_gmm)

    print(f"\n--- {name} ---")
    print(f"K-Means  ARI: {ari_km:.3f}")
    print(f"DBSCAN   ARI: {ari_db:.3f}  (noise: {np.sum(labels_db == -1)})")
    print(f"GMM      ARI: {ari_gmm:.3f}")

# Scenario 1: Blobs (K-Means wins)
X_blobs, y_blobs = make_blobs(n_samples=600, centers=3, cluster_std=0.5, random_state=42)
evaluate_clusterers(X_blobs, y_blobs, "Isotropic Blobs")

# Scenario 2: Moons (K-Means fails, DBSCAN wins)
X_moons, y_moons = make_moons(n_samples=600, noise=0.07, random_state=42)
evaluate_clusterers(X_moons, y_moons, "Moons")

# Scenario 3: Circles (K-Means and GMM fail, DBSCAN wins)
X_circles, y_circles = make_circles(n_samples=600, noise=0.05, factor=0.4, random_state=42)
evaluate_clusterers(X_circles, y_circles, "Concentric Circles")

# Scenario 4: Elongated clusters with different orientations (GMM wins)
X_elong, y_elong = make_blobs(n_samples=600, centers=3, cluster_std=1.0, random_state=42)
# Stretch cluster 0 along axis
X_elong[y_elong == 0, 0] *= 3.0
evaluate_clusterers(X_elong, y_elong, "Elongated Clusters")
```

### Failure Mode: Hubness in High Dimensions

A subtle but serious failure mode in high-dimensional nearest-neighbor-based methods is **hubness**: as dimension increases, the distribution of "how many times a point appears as a neighbor of other points" becomes extremely skewed. A small fraction of points (hubs) become the nearest neighbor of a disproportionate number of other points, corrupting DBSCAN's density estimates and t-SNE's neighborhood probabilities. The standard remediation is to apply PCA or UMAP to reduce to a moderate intrinsic dimensionality before computing neighborhoods. The `n_components` for this pre-reduction should be chosen by the explained-variance curve, not by visual inspection of a 2D projection.

### Failure Mode: t-SNE Hyperparameter Misinterpretation

Perplexity controls the effective number of neighbors each point considers during probability estimation. With low perplexity (5–10), t-SNE optimizes very local structure and tends to fragment true clusters into sub-clusters or chains. With high perplexity (50–100), it over-smooths local neighborhoods and can merge distinct clusters. For datasets with 10,000–100,000 points, perplexity values between 30 and 50 are generally stable, but the engineer should always run the embedding at multiple perplexity values and treat consistency of cluster structure across values as evidence of genuine geometry rather than artifact.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.manifold import TSNE
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits

digits = load_digits()
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(digits.data.astype(np.float32))
X_pca = PCA(n_components=30, svd_solver='randomized', random_state=42).fit_transform(X)
y = digits.target

perplexities = [5, 15, 30, 50, 100]
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, len(perplexities), figsize=(25, 5))

for ax, perp in zip(axes, perplexities):
    tsne = TSNE(
        n_components=2,
        perplexity=perp,
        learning_rate='auto',
        n_iter=1000,
        init='pca',
        random_state=42,
        n_jobs=-1
    )
    X_emb = tsne.fit_transform(X_pca)
    ax.scatter(X_emb[:, 0], X_emb[:, 1], c=y, cmap='tab10', s=6, alpha=0.7)
    ax.set_title(f'perplexity={perp}\nKL={tsne.kl_divergence_:.3f}')
    ax.set_xticks([])
    ax.set_yticks([])

plt.suptitle('t-SNE Stability Across Perplexity Values', fontsize=14)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('tsne_perplexity_audit.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
```

Cluster identity in t-SNE is not stable under perplexity change if the structure is artifactual. Genuine clusters — digit classes in MNIST, cell types in single-cell RNA data — will appear separated and compact across the full perplexity range, though their spatial arrangement will differ. This consistency audit costs two minutes of compute and prevents entire research directions being founded on visualization noise.

### Cluster Validation Metrics: When There Are No Labels

When ground-truth labels exist, the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) and Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) provide interpretable, chance-corrected assessments of cluster-to-label alignment. When no labels exist, the practitioner must rely on internal metrics. The **silhouette score** for a sample $i$ is $s(i) = (b(i) - a(i)) / \max(a(i), b(i))$, where $a(i)$ is the mean intra-cluster distance and $b(i)$ is the mean distance to the nearest other cluster. Values near 1 indicate well-separated, compact clusters; values near $-1$ indicate misassignment. The **Davies-Bouldin index** measures the average ratio of within-cluster scatter to between-cluster separation, with lower values indicating better separation. Neither metric is a substitute for domain validation, but both provide principled signals for hyperparameter search.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans, DBSCAN, AgglomerativeClustering
from sklearn.metrics import (silhouette_score, silhouette_samples,
                              davies_bouldin_score, calinski_harabasz_score)
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs

X, y_true = make_blobs(n_samples=2000, centers=5, cluster_std=0.6, random_state=42)
X_s = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

km = KMeans(n_clusters=5, n_init=15, random_state=42)
labels = km.fit_predict(X_s)

# Global silhouette: single summary
sil_global = silhouette_score(X_s, labels, metric='euclidean', sample_size=500, random_state=42)

# Per-sample silhouette: diagnose which clusters are poorly separated
sil_samples = silhouette_samples(X_s, labels, metric='euclidean')
per_cluster_sil = {k: sil_samples[labels == k].mean() for k in range(5)}

print(f"Global silhouette: {sil_global:.4f}")
print(f"Per-cluster silhouette: {per_cluster_sil}")
print(f"Davies-Bouldin Index: {davies_bouldin_score(X_s, labels):.4f}")
print(f"Calinski-Harabasz Index: {calinski_harabasz_score(X_s, labels):.2f}")

# Clusters with silhouette below 0.3 are candidates for merging or re-examination
bad_clusters = [k for k, v in per_cluster_sil.items() if v < 0.3]
print(f"Clusters with poor silhouette (<0.3): {bad_clusters}")

# Agglomerative clustering: compare linkage strategies
for linkage in ['ward', 'complete', 'average', 'single']:
    agg = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=5, linkage=linkage, metric='euclidean')
    agg_labels = agg.fit_predict(X_s)
    sil = silhouette_score(X_s, agg_labels, sample_size=500, random_state=42)
    db = davies_bouldin_score(X_s, agg_labels)
    print(f"Agglomerative ({linkage:>8s}) | Silhouette: {sil:.4f} | DB: {db:.4f}")
```

Ward linkage minimizes the increase in total within-cluster variance at each merge step — the same objective as K-Means — which is why it typically produces the most compact, interpretable clusters among the agglomerative variants. Complete linkage (maximum pairwise distance between clusters) is robust to outliers and tends to produce compact, roughly equal-sized clusters. Single linkage (minimum pairwise distance) is sensitive to noise points and prone to chaining, but is the only linkage that can capture arbitrary-shape clusters through hierarchical structure. Average linkage occupies the middle ground. The correct choice is determined by whether the downstream consumer needs interpretable, balanced partitions (Ward) or structure-preserving hierarchical organization (complete or average).

The practitioner who internalizes the geometry behind each algorithm — that K-Means is Voronoi partitioning, that DBSCAN is connected components of a threshold graph, that GMM is Bayesian inference on a mixture model, that PCA is rotation into the basis of maximum variance, that t-SNE is a neighborhood-preserving stochastic relaxation — will make modeling decisions from first principles rather than from a lookup table of "which algorithm to use when." The algorithms are tools derived from mathematical propositions. Understanding the propositions makes the tool selection obvious, and the failure modes predictable.
