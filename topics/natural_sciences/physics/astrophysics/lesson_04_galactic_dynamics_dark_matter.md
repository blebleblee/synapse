# Lesson 4: Galactic Dynamics, Dark Matter

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The universe at galactic scales operates as a vast gravitational laboratory where luminous matter—stars, gas, and dust—traces only a fraction of the total mass-energy distribution. When Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured rotation curves of spiral galaxies in the 1970s, they uncovered a profound discrepancy: stars in the outer regions of galaxies orbit far too rapidly to be bound by the visible matter alone. Newtonian gravity predicts that orbital velocity should decline with radius as $v \propto r^{-1/2}$ beyond the luminous disk, yet observations reveal flat rotation curves extending well into the galactic halo. This mismatch between observed kinematics and predicted dynamics from baryonic matter constitutes the observational foundation for dark matter—an invisible, non-luminous component comprising approximately 85% of the universe's matter content.

Galactic dynamics operates at the intersection of classical gravitational physics and statistical mechanics. Unlike planetary systems where individual orbits dominate, galaxies contain $10^{11}$ to $10^{12}$ stars whose collective behavior demands statistical treatment. The stellar distribution function $f(\vec{r}, \vec{v}, t)$ describes the probability density of finding a star at position $\vec{r}$ with velocity $\vec{v}$ at time $t$. This six-dimensional phase space density evolves according to the collisionless Boltzmann equation, also known as the Vlasov equation in plasma physics contexts:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \vec{v} \cdot \nabla_{\vec{r}} f - \nabla_{\vec{r}} \Phi \cdot \nabla_{\vec{v}} f = 0$$

The collision term vanishes because direct stellar encounters occur on timescales vastly exceeding the galactic dynamical time. Stars interact gravitationally through the smooth mean-field potential $\Phi(\vec{r}, t)$ generated by the collective mass distribution. This potential satisfies Poisson's equation:

$$\nabla^2 \Phi = 4\pi G \rho(\vec{r}, t)$$

where the density $\rho$ couples back to the distribution function through:

$$\rho(\vec{r}, t) = \int f(\vec{r}, \vec{v}, t) \, d^3v$$

This closure forms a self-consistent integro-differential system governing the gravitational evolution of collisionless stellar systems. The absence of two-body relaxation distinguishes galactic dynamics from thermalized systems—galaxies do not evolve toward Maxwell-Boltzmann equilibrium on observable timescales. Instead, they maintain quasi-steady states characterized by specific phase space structures and velocity dispersions that persist for gigayears.

The virial theorem provides a powerful constraint linking kinetic and potential energy in gravitationally bound systems. For a system in statistical equilibrium, the time-averaged kinetic energy $\langle T \rangle$ and potential energy $\langle U \rangle$ satisfy:

$$2\langle T \rangle + \langle U \rangle = 0$$

This scalar relation emerges from the tensor virial theorem by contracting the moment of inertia tensor and applies when surface pressure terms vanish at the system boundary. For spherical systems with isotropic velocity dispersion $\sigma_r^2$, the virial theorem connects observable kinematics to total mass:

$$M \sim \frac{\sigma_r^2 R}{G}$$

where $R$ represents a characteristic radius. This mass estimator forms the backbone of dynamical mass measurements in galaxy clusters, elliptical galaxies, and globular clusters. When applied to systems with measured velocity dispersions and spatial extents, the virial mass systematically exceeds the mass inferred from luminous tracers by factors of ten to several hundred in galaxy clusters, providing independent evidence for dark matter.

Jeans equations reduce the full phase space dynamics to spatial moments of the distribution function, yielding a hierarchy of coupled differential equations analogous to fluid dynamics. The zeroth moment corresponds to mass continuity, while the first moment yields the Jeans equation for momentum balance in spherical symmetry:

$$\frac{d(\rho \sigma_r^2)}{dr} + 2\beta \frac{\rho \sigma_r^2}{r} = -\rho \frac{GM(r)}{r^2}$$

Here $\beta = 1 - \sigma_\theta^2/\sigma_r^2$ quantifies velocity anisotropy, with $\beta = 0$ corresponding to isotropic dispersions and $\beta > 0$ indicating radial anisotropy. The enclosed mass $M(r)$ directly enters the force balance, enabling mass profile reconstruction from observed stellar kinematics. The degeneracy between mass distribution and velocity anisotropy constitutes a fundamental limitation: multiple combinations of $M(r)$ and $\beta(r)$ can reproduce identical line-of-sight velocity dispersion profiles. Breaking this degeneracy requires additional constraints from higher-order velocity moments, gravitational lensing, or independent tracers.

Dark matter halos emerge naturally from gravitational instability in an expanding universe. Cold dark matter—non-relativistic at decoupling—seeds structure formation through density perturbations that grow via gravitational collapse. N-body simulations reveal that virialized halos follow near-universal density profiles. The Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile represents the canonical parameterization:

$$\rho(r) = \frac{\rho_s}{\left(\frac{r}{r_s}\right)\left(1 + \frac{r}{r_s}\right)^2}$$

where $r_s$ defines the scale radius and $\rho_s$ sets the characteristic density. This profile exhibits a cuspy inner slope $\rho \propto r^{-1}$ and outer decline $\rho \propto r^{-3}$, with a logarithmic slope continuously varying between these asymptotic limits. The scale radius typically lies at $r_s \sim 0.1 r_{200}$, where $r_{200}$ denotes the virial radius enclosing a mean density 200 times the critical density of the universe. Halo concentration $c = r_{200}/r_s$ correlates inversely with halo mass, reflecting formation epochs: smaller halos collapse earlier at higher universal densities, producing steeper inner profiles.

The hierarchical nature of structure formation implies that dark matter halos contain substructure—tidally stripped remnants of smaller halos accreted during cosmic assembly. Simulations predict abundant subhalo populations, yet observations of Milky Way satellites reveal fewer luminous dwarf galaxies than predicted dark matter subhalos. This "missing satellites problem" may indicate either baryonic suppression of star formation in low-mass halos or alternative dark matter physics such as warm dark matter that erases small-scale power. Similarly, the "cusp-core problem" notes that observed rotation curves of dwarf galaxies often favor constant-density cores rather than NFW cusps, potentially implicating baryonic feedback processes or self-interacting dark matter.

Galaxy formation proceeds through hierarchical merging within the cosmic web—a filamentary large-scale structure where dark matter halos accrete along dense filaments and grow through successive mergers. The halo mass function $dn/dM$ describes the comoving number density of halos per unit mass, derived from the extended Press-Schechter formalism or excursion set theory. This function exhibits a power-law slope at low masses and exponential cutoff at high masses, reflecting the competition between gravitational amplification of density perturbations and cosmic expansion. The characteristic halo mass corresponding to the peak of the mass function evolves with redshift, growing from $\sim 10^{11} M_\odot$ at $z \sim 2$ to $\sim 10^{13} M_\odot$ today, consistent with observed galaxy mass assembly histories.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

Rotation curve analysis forms the primary observational probe of galactic mass distributions. The circular velocity $v_c(r)$ at radius $r$ follows from centripetal balance between gravitational force and circular motion:

$$v_c^2(r) = \frac{GM(r)}{r}$$

where $M(r)$ represents the enclosed mass within radius $r$. For an exponential stellar disk with surface density $\Sigma(R) = \Sigma_0 \exp(-R/R_d)$, where $R_d$ denotes the disk scale length, the rotation curve peaks near $2.2 R_d$ and declines thereafter if no dark halo is present. Observations instead show flat or slowly rising rotation curves extending to $10 R_d$ or beyond, demanding a dark matter halo with $M(r) \propto r$ at large radii—corresponding to a density profile $\rho \propto r^{-2}$.

Practical rotation curve construction requires spatially resolved kinematic tracers. In spiral galaxies, HI 21-cm emission provides the most extended tracer, reaching radii where stellar light becomes undetectable. Radio interferometric observations with the Very Large Array or Westerbork Synthesis Radio Telescope map the HI velocity field $v_{\text{los}}(x, y)$ across the galactic disk. The observed line-of-sight velocity combines systematic rotation with random turbulent motions:

$$v_{\text{los}} = v_{\text{sys}} + v_c(r) \sin i \cos \theta + v_{\text{turb}}$$

where $v_{\text{sys}}$ represents the systemic velocity, $i$ denotes the disk inclination angle, and $\theta$ measures azimuthal position angle relative to the major axis. Tilted-ring modeling decomposes the velocity field into circular velocity $v_c(r)$ as a function of radius by fitting concentric annuli with independent kinematic parameters. Asymmetric drift corrections account for pressure support in the gas, though HI typically remains kinematically cold with velocity dispersions $\sigma \sim 10$ km/s, negligible compared to rotation speeds $v_c \sim 200$ km/s.

The resulting rotation curves exhibit characteristic morphologies depending on mass distribution. For a disk-dominated system, $v_c(r)$ rises steeply in the inner regions, reflecting the centrally concentrated stellar bulge, then flattens where the disk contribution dominates. At large radii, the dark halo contribution becomes dominant, maintaining constant velocity. Decomposition into luminous and dark components proceeds by modeling each mass component separately:

$$v_c^2(r) = v_{\text{disk}}^2(r) + v_{\text{bulge}}^2(r) + v_{\text{halo}}^2(r)$$

The disk contribution $v_{\text{disk}}^2(r) = 4\pi G \Sigma_0 R_d^2 y^2 [I_0(y) K_0(y) - I_1(y) K_1(y)]$ involves modified Bessel functions $I_n$ and $K_n$ with argument $y = r/(2R_d)$. The bulge typically follows a Hernquist profile with velocity contribution $v_{\text{bulge}}^2(r) = GM_{\text{bulge}} r / (r + a)^2$. The halo component adopts an NFW profile, yielding:

$$v_{\text{halo}}^2(r) = \frac{GM_{200}}{r} \frac{\ln(1 + cr/r_{200}) - cr/(r_{200} + cr)}{\ln(1 + c) - c/(1 + c)}$$

where $c$ represents the halo concentration and $M_{200}$ the virial mass. Fitting these combined contributions to observed rotation curves determines the dark-to-luminous mass ratio and halo concentration, typically finding $M_{\text{dark}}/M_{\text{lum}} \sim 5$-$10$ within the optical radius for Milky Way-mass galaxies.

Stellar kinematics in elliptical galaxies and galactic bulges demand different observational techniques. Integrated-light spectroscopy measures the line-of-sight velocity distribution (LOSVD) from Doppler-broadened absorption lines. The LOSVD $\mathcal{L}(v_{\text{los}})$ projects the three-dimensional velocity distribution function along the observational line of sight:

$$\mathcal{L}(v_{\text{los}}) = \int \int f(r, v_r, v_\theta, v_\phi) \delta\left(v_{\text{los}} - v_r \cos\theta - v_\phi \sin\theta\right) dv_r dv_\theta dv_\phi$$

where the velocity components refer to spherical coordinates aligned with the line of sight. The LOSVD encodes information about velocity dispersion $\sigma_{\text{los}}(R)$ as a function of projected radius $R$, higher-order velocity moments (skewness $h_3$, kurtosis $h_4$), and mean streaming velocity $\bar{v}(R)$ indicating rotation or radial flows. Gauss-Hermite polynomial parameterizations efficiently represent the LOSVD:

$$\mathcal{L}(v) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} \exp\left[-\frac{(v - \bar{v})^2}{2\sigma^2}\right] \left[1 + \sum_{n=3}^N h_n H_n\left(\frac{v - \bar{v}}{\sigma}\right)\right]$$

where $H_n$ denote Hermite polynomials and $h_n$ quantify deviations from Gaussianity.

Schwarzschild orbit modeling connects observed stellar kinematics to underlying gravitational potentials through forward modeling of stellar orbits. This method constructs a library of orbits in a trial potential $\Phi(\vec{r})$ by integrating the equations of motion:

$$\ddot{\vec{r}} = -\nabla \Phi(\vec{r})$$

Each orbit contributes to observable quantities—surface brightness $I(x, y)$ and LOSVD moments $\mathcal{L}(v_{\text{los}}; x, y)$—depending on its spatial trajectory and velocity structure. A non-negative linear combination of orbit weights reproduces the observed kinematic fields:

$$I_{\text{obs}}(x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{orb}}} w_i I_i(x, y), \quad \mathcal{L}_{\text{obs}}(v; x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{orb}}} w_i \mathcal{L}_i(v; x, y)$$

subject to the constraint $w_i \geq 0$ ensuring physical realizability. The weights $\{w_i\}$ are determined via $\chi^2$ minimization or maximum entropy regularization. By repeating this procedure for a grid of trial potentials with varying dark matter content, one reconstructs the mass profile $M(r)$ consistent with observed stellar dynamics.

Jeans equation analysis provides a computationally lighter alternative to full orbit modeling, directly relating observable velocity dispersion profiles to mass distributions. In spherical systems with isotropic velocities ($\beta = 0$), the radial Jeans equation simplifies to:

$$\frac{d(\nu \sigma_r^2)}{dr} = -\nu \frac{GM(r)}{r^2}$$

where $\nu(r)$ denotes the three-dimensional stellar number density. Multiplying by $r^2$ and integrating yields:

$$\nu(r) \sigma_r^2(r) = \frac{G}{r^2} \int_r^\infty \nu(s) M(s) \, ds$$

For systems with known three-dimensional density profiles $\nu(r)$ deprojected from surface brightness profiles $I(R)$ via Abel inversion, this equation directly solves for the velocity dispersion profile $\sigma_r(r)$ given a mass model $M(r)$. The observable line-of-sight dispersion $\sigma_{\text{los}}(R)$ relates to $\sigma_r(r)$ through projection along the line of sight:

$$I(R) \sigma_{\text{los}}^2(R) = 2 \int_R^\infty \frac{\nu(r) \sigma_r^2(r) r \, dr}{\sqrt{r^2 - R^2}}$$

Practical implementations discretize these integrals and iteratively adjust mass distributions to match observed dispersion profiles, often parameterizing dark matter halos with free concentration and mass parameters.

Galaxy cluster dynamics employ virial mass estimators calibrated through ensemble kinematics of member galaxies. A cluster containing $N$ galaxies with measured redshifts $\{z_i\}$ yields line-of-sight velocity dispersions:

$$\sigma_v^2 = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (v_i - \bar{v})^2$$

where $v_i = c(z_i - \bar{z})/(1 + \bar{z})$ represents the peculiar velocity relative to the cluster mean. The virial mass follows:

$$M_{\text{vir}} = \frac{3\sigma_v^2 R_{\text{vir}}}{G}$$

with virial radius $R_{\text{vir}}$ estimated from the projected galaxy distribution. Corrections for velocity anisotropy and unbound interlopers introduce systematic uncertainties of $\sim 30\%$. X-ray observations provide complementary mass constraints via hydrostatic equilibrium of the hot intracluster medium (ICM). The ICM gas at temperature $T \sim 10^7$-$10^8$ K emits thermal bremsstrahlung with luminosity tracing density squared $L_X \propto \rho_{\text{gas}}^2$. Assuming hydrostatic balance:

$$\frac{dP}{dr} = -\frac{GM(r) \rho_{\text{gas}}}{r^2}$$

where pressure $P = n k_B T$ relates to gas temperature and number density. Measuring radial temperature profiles $T(r)$ from X-ray spectroscopy and density profiles $\rho_{\text{gas}}(r)$ from surface brightness deprojection allows solving for $M(r)$ without dynamical assumptions about galaxy orbits. Comparison between virial, X-ray, and gravitational lensing mass estimates provides consistency checks on mass determination methods and potential evidence for non-thermal pressure support or modified gravity.

The collisionless Boltzmann equation admits equilibrium solutions characterized by conserved integrals of motion. In spherical potentials, energy $E = v^2/2 + \Phi(r)$ and angular momentum $L = r v_\theta$ are conserved. Distribution functions depending solely on these isolating integrals, $f(E, L)$, automatically satisfy the steady-state Boltzmann equation. The ergodic case $f(E)$ produces isotropic velocity dispersions, while $f(E, L)$ generates anisotropic models. The Hernquist distribution function for an isotropic Hernquist density profile:

$$f(E) = \frac{M}{8\sqrt{2}\pi^3 a^3} \frac{\sqrt{E}}{(1 - E)^2} \left[1 - 2E(3 - E)\right]$$

where $E = -\Phi_0 E$ is the dimensionless binding energy and $\Phi_0 = GM/(2a)$ the central potential. This function remains non-negative for all $0 \leq E < 1$, ensuring physical validity. More complex potentials require numerical solutions to the Abel integral equation relating density $\rho(r)$ to distribution function $f(E)$:

$$\rho(r) = 4\pi \int_{\Phi(r)}^\infty f(E) \sqrt{2(E - \Phi)} \, dE$$

Inversion techniques developed by Eddington and generalized by Cuddeford allow constructing $f(E)$ from observed $\rho(r)$ for spherical systems, though uniqueness is not guaranteed for anisotropic systems without additional constraints.

Action-angle variables provide powerful coordinates for analyzing orbital structure in galactic potentials. For integrable potentials admitting three independent isolating integrals, canonical transformations map phase space coordinates $(q_i, p_i)$ to action-angle variables $(J_i, \theta_i)$ where actions $J_i$ are conserved and angles $\theta_i$ evolve linearly with time:

$$\dot{\theta}_i = \Omega_i(J_1, J_2, J_3)$$

The frequencies $\Omega_i$ depend only on actions, characterizing orbital periodicity. Resonances occur when frequency ratios satisfy integer relations $n_1 \Omega_1 + n_2 \Omega_2 + n_3 \Omega_3 = 0$, creating persistent orbital structures like spiral density waves or bars. The actions themselves are adiabatic invariants under slow potential evolution, enabling semi-analytic tracking of stellar populations through galaxy mergers or tidal perturbations. Numerical computation of actions proceeds via Stäckel fudge approximations or torus mapping algorithms that fit orbital trajectories to integrable toy potentials.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Rotation curve measurements face systematic errors that propagate directly into dark matter constraints. Inclination angle uncertainties represent the most pernicious systematic: an error $\Delta i$ in assumed inclination produces fractional velocity error $\Delta v_c / v_c \approx (\tan i) \Delta i$. For nearly face-on galaxies with $i < 30°$, inclination uncertainties dominate measurement precision. Observers mitigate this by selecting moderately inclined systems with $40° < i < 70°$ where errors remain manageable and projection effects do not severely degrade spatial resolution. Kinematic position angle misalignment introduces velocity shear that mimics non-circular motions, biasing mass profile reconstruction. Harmonic decomposition of velocity fields into sine and cosine components isolates these systematics:

$$v(R, \theta) = v_{\text{sys}} + v_c(R) \sin i \cos\theta + v_s(R) \sin i \sin\theta$$

The sine term $v_s(R)$ should vanish for purely circular rotation; significant $v_s$ indicates kinematic misalignment, warps, or bar-driven streaming motions requiring morphological correction.

Beam smearing in radio observations convolves the true velocity field with the telescope point spread function, artificially broadening rotation curve gradients and reducing peak velocities in the inner regions. Deconvolution algorithms such as CLEAN or maximum entropy methods recover higher spatial resolution but introduce correlated noise that biases kinematic fitting. The most robust approach combines high-resolution interferometric observations in the inner regions with single-dish measurements capturing extended emission, then carefully cross-calibrates the flux scale between datasets. Velocity channel width selection balances spectral resolution against signal-to-noise: narrow channels ($\Delta v \sim 2$ km/s) resolve detailed kinematic structure but fragment emission into multiple weak channels, while wide channels ($\Delta v \sim 10$ km/s) improve sensitivity at the cost of blurring velocity gradients.

Asymmetric drift corrections become critical for pressure-supported tracers like molecular gas or old stellar populations. The radial Jeans equation for axisymmetric disks relates circular velocity $v_c$ to observed rotation velocity $v_\phi$ and radial velocity dispersion $\sigma_R$:

$$v_c^2 = v_\phi^2 + \sigma_R^2 \left(1 - \frac{\sigma_\phi^2}{\sigma_R^2} + \frac{d \ln(\Sigma \sigma_R^2)}{d \ln R}\right)$$

The asymmetric drift $v_c - v_\phi$ scales with velocity dispersion squared and density gradient, typically amounting to $10$-$30$ km/s for cool HI gas but reaching $50$-$100$ km/s for warm molecular clouds or old stellar disks. Neglecting this correction systematically underestimates circular velocity and inferred dark matter content, particularly in dwarf galaxies where pressure support contributes substantially to force balance.

Elliptical galaxy mass modeling confronts the mass-anisotropy degeneracy: an infinite family of combinations $(M(r), \beta(r))$ reproduce identical projected dispersion profiles $\sigma_{\text{los}}(R)$. This degeneracy emerges because projection along the line of sight loses information about the three-dimensional velocity tensor. Higher-order velocity moments partially break degeneracies—kurtosis $h_4$ probes radial versus tangential orbital families—but complete reconstruction requires independent constraints. Proper motion measurements from *Gaia* or *Hubble Space Telescope* provide tangential velocity components, directly constraining velocity anisotropy without relying on Jeans modeling assumptions. Gravitational lensing offers orthogonal mass constraints insensitive to velocity structure: strong lensing by foreground galaxies measures enclosed mass within the Einstein radius through image positions and time delays, while weak lensing from background galaxy shape distortions reconstructs projected surface mass density.

The central cusp-core controversy in dwarf galaxies illustrates systematic challenges in low-surface-brightness regime measurements. NFW profiles predict central cusps $\rho \propto r^{-1}$, yet rotation curves of dwarf irregulars often prefer constant-density cores $\rho \approx \rho_0$. Discriminating cusps from cores requires high-resolution kinematics within $\sim 100$ pc, approaching or exceeding typical HI beam sizes. Non-circular motions from stellar feedback-driven turbulence mimic core-like rotation curves if interpreted as circular velocities. Energy injection from supernovae creates expanding bubbles with non-circular outflows reaching $50$-$100$ km/s, comparable to dwarf galaxy rotation speeds. Careful modeling of beam smearing, pressure support, and non-circular motions reduces but does not eliminate the apparent preference for cores. Alternative explanations invoke self-interacting dark matter with velocity-dependent cross sections $\sigma/m \sim 1$ cm$^2$/g, which thermalizes the inner halo through dark matter particle collisions, flattening the central cusp into a core. Distinguishing baryonic feedback from exotic dark matter physics requires comparing rotation curves across galaxies spanning wide ranges in star formation efficiency and halo mass.

Substructure detection and dark matter halo occupation statistics probe the hierarchical formation paradigm. Tidal streams from disrupting satellite galaxies map the gravitational potential through their dynamical coherence. The GD-1 stream in the Milky Way halo exhibits gaps and density perturbations attributed to close encounters with massive subhalos. Reconstructing the perturbing subhalo mass from stream morphology involves forward modeling the tidal disruption and perturbation history:

$$\frac{d^2 \vec{r}_{\text{stream}}}{dt^2} = -\nabla \Phi_{\text{host}}(\vec{r}_{\text{stream}}) - \nabla \Phi_{\text{sub}}(\vec{r}_{\text{stream}} - \vec{r}_{\text{sub}}(t))$$

Numerical integration of stream orbits under time-varying subhalo passages predicts gap widths and density contrast as a function of subhalo mass $M_{\text{sub}}$ and impact parameter $b$. Comparison to observed gap morphology constrains $M_{\text{sub}} \sim 10^6$-$10^7 M_\odot$ for GD-1 perturbers, consistent with cold dark matter subhalo abundance predictions. Pulsar timing perturbations from transiting dark matter substructure offer complementary sensitivity to compact subhalos: a massive subhalo passing near the Earth-pulsar line of sight induces a transient Doppler shift with characteristic timescale $\sim 10$ years and amplitude depending on subhalo mass and impact parameter. Surveys monitoring $\sim 100$ pulsars with nanosecond timing precision constrain subhalo mass functions down to $M \sim 10^{-6} M_\odot$, probing asteroid-mass subhalos predicted in cold dark matter but potentially absent in warm or self-interacting models.

Cosmological hydrodynamic simulations such as IllustrisTNG, EAGLE, and FIRE combine N-body dark matter dynamics with baryonic gas physics, star formation, and feedback processes. These simulations resolve galaxy-scale dynamics within cosmological volumes, enabling statistical comparison to observed galaxy populations. Convergence testing addresses numerical resolution requirements: halo concentration and subhalo abundance converge only when particle mass $m_{\text{DM}} < M_{\text{halo}} / 10^6$, demanding $\sim 10^8$ particles to resolve Milky Way-mass halos. Force softening length $\epsilon$ must satisfy $\epsilon < r_s / 10$ to avoid artificially flattening NFW cusps. Adaptive mesh refinement or moving-mesh hydrodynamics (AREPO code) achieve spatial resolution $\sim 100$ pc in dense regions while maintaining $\sim$ Mpc resolution in voids, spanning six orders of magnitude in spatial scale.

Baryonic feedback implementations dramatically affect simulated galaxy properties. Supernova-driven winds require subgrid models parameterizing mass loading factors $\eta = \dot{M}_{\text{wind}} / \dot{M}_*$ and wind velocities $v_w$. Empirically calibrated models tune $\eta(M_*, z)$ to reproduce observed galaxy stellar mass functions and star formation rate distributions. Black hole feedback in massive galaxies involves both radiative (quasar) and mechanical (radio jet) modes. Mechanical feedback injects thermal energy with efficiency $\epsilon_{\text{BH}} \sim 0.01$ of accreted rest mass energy, heating the hot halo gas and quenching star formation. Simulations without strong feedback overproduce massive galaxies by converting baryons too efficiently into stars, violating observed stellar mass fractions $M_* / M_{\text{halo}} \sim 0.02$ at the peak of star formation efficiency near $M_{\text{halo}} \sim 10^{12} M_\odot$.

Large-scale structure observations test dark matter clustering predictions across cosmic scales. Redshift surveys such as SDSS and DESI measure galaxy positions in three-dimensional comoving coordinates, constructing correlation functions $\xi(r)$ and power spectra $P(k)$ quantifying clustering amplitude as a function of separation. The two-point correlation function relates to the power spectrum via Fourier transform:

$$\xi(r) = \frac{1}{2\pi^2} \int_0^\infty P(k) k^2 \frac{\sin(kr)}{kr} \, dk$$

The observed galaxy power spectrum encodes primordial density fluctuations evolved through gravitational instability and galaxy formation biases. Baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO)—a characteristic scale $r_{\text{BAO}} \sim 150$ Mpc imprinted by sound waves in the pre-recombination plasma—serve as a standard ruler for cosmological distance measurements. The BAO feature appears as a bump in $\xi(r)$ at the sound horizon scale and as regularly spaced oscillations in $P(k)$. Precision measurements of the BAO scale constrain dark energy equation of state parameters and spatial curvature independently of supernovae distance ladders.

Weak gravitational lensing by large-scale structure distorts background galaxy images through tidal shear fields correlated with foreground dark matter distributions. The convergence $\kappa$ and shear $\gamma$ depend on the projected mass distribution:

$$\kappa(\vec{\theta}) = \frac{1}{\Sigma_{\text{crit}}} \int_0^{r_s} \rho(\vec{x}) \frac{D_{\ell s}}{D_\ell D_s} \, dr$$

where $D_\ell$, $D_s$, $D_{\ell s}$ denote angular diameter distances to lens, source, and lens-source respectively. The critical surface density $\Sigma_{\text{crit}} = c^2 D_s / (4\pi G D_\ell D_{\ell s})$ sets the mass scale. Weak lensing shear correlations $\langle \gamma \gamma \rangle(\theta)$ directly probe the dark matter power spectrum convolved with survey geometry and source redshift distribution. Cosmic shear surveys covering thousands of square degrees (Euclid, Rubin Observatory LSST, Roman Space Telescope) will measure $P(k)$ to percent-level precision across scales $0.1 < k < 10 \, h$ Mpc$^{-1}$, constraining neutrino masses, dark energy, and modified gravity theories through their influence on structure growth.

Velocity field reconstruction from peculiar velocity measurements offers an alternative probe of dark matter clustering. Galaxy peculiar velocities arise from gravitational acceleration by large-scale density fluctuations:

$$\vec{v}_{\text{pec}} = \frac{f H_0}{4\pi} \int \frac{\delta(\vec{r}')(\vec{r} - \vec{r}')}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|^3} \, d^3r'$$

where $\delta = (\rho - \bar{\rho})/\bar{\rho}$ represents the density contrast and $f \equiv d\ln D / d\ln a$ the linear growth rate. Measuring peculiar velocities through Tully-Fisher or fundamental plane distance indicators yields $\vec{v}_{\text{pec}} = cz - H_0 d$, though distance measurement uncertainties $\sim 20\%$ limit precision. Wiener filtering optimally reconstructs the three-dimensional velocity and density fields from noisy peculiar velocity catalogs, enabling direct comparison to dark matter simulations and tests of modified gravity models that predict deviations from $f = \Omega_m^{0.55}$.

The observational dark matter evidence rests on multiple independent pillars: rotation curves, virial masses, gravitational lensing, cosmic microwave background anisotropies, and large-scale structure. Each method probes dark matter through different physical mechanisms—dynamical tracers, photon deflection, primordial acoustic physics—yet all converge on consistent dark matter fractions $\Omega_{\text{DM}} h^2 \approx 0.12$ and halo mass profiles. This concordance across diverse observables and cosmic epochs provides compelling empirical support for a collisionless, non-baryonic dark matter component comprising the majority of gravitating mass in the universe. The microscopic identity remains unknown—weakly interacting massive particles, axions, primordial black holes, or other exotic candidates—but the macroscopic gravitational signatures robustly establish dark matter's phenomenological reality within the standard cosmological framework.
