# Operational Verification Labs: Electromagnetism

## Objective & Laboratory Bench Setup

The empirical verification of electromagnetic phenomena demands precision instrumentation, rigorous calibration protocols, and systematic error analysis. This laboratory guide grounds Maxwell's theoretical framework in measurable physical reality through direct observation of electric fields, magnetic flux densities, inductive coupling, and wave propagation. The experimental physicist must develop proficiency in oscilloscope triggering, function generator synchronization, Gaussian meter calibration, and AC bridge balancing to extract quantitative electromagnetic constants from controlled apparatus configurations.

The laboratory bench requires a stable AC/DC power supply capable of delivering 0–30 V with milliamp-resolution current monitoring, a digital storage oscilloscope with dual-channel capability and FFT analysis, a programmable function generator spanning 1 Hz to 10 MHz, precision decade resistance and capacitance substitution boxes, a calibrated Gaussmeter or Hall probe for magnetic field measurement, and a variable air-core solenoid with known turn density. Supplementary equipment includes precision digital multimeters with sub-ohm resolution, a capacitance bridge for reactance measurement, ferrite and air-core inductors spanning 1 μH to 100 mH, and coaxial cables with matched 50-Ω impedance termination. All instruments must exhibit traceable NIST calibration certificates with documented uncertainty budgets.

The computational environment pairs laboratory instrumentation with Python-based data acquisition and analysis. Install NumPy for vectorized field calculations, SciPy for Fourier transforms and optimization routines, and Matplotlib for publication-quality visualization. Verify the installation by executing `python -c 'import numpy as np; import scipy; import matplotlib; print(f"NumPy {np.__version__}, SciPy {scipy.__version__}")'` and confirming version numbers exceeding 1.21 and 1.7 respectively. For oscilloscope interfacing, install PyVISA to enable SCPI command automation over USB or GPIB, then test communication with `python -m pyvisa info` to enumerate connected instruments. The data pipeline flows from instrument SCPI queries through NumPy array manipulation to Matplotlib rendering, allowing real-time field visualization and post-acquisition regression analysis.

Physical constants anchor all electromagnetic measurements to the SI unit system. The vacuum permittivity $\epsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}$ F/m governs electrostatic field energy density, while vacuum permeability $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}$ H/m dictates magnetic flux concentration in free space. These constants interlock through the exact speed of light relation $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0 \epsilon_0} = 299792458$ m/s, verified to nine significant figures by time-of-flight laser interferometry. Electromagnetic induction obeys Faraday's law $\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$, where induced electromotive force arises from time-varying magnetic flux $\Phi_B = \int \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{A}$ threading a conductor loop. Ampère's law with Maxwell's displacement current correction $\nabla \times \vec{B} = \mu_0 \vec{J} + \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \vec{E}}{\partial t}$ couples conduction current density to capacitive displacement current, enabling wave propagation through insulating media.

## Exercise 1: Empirical Observation & Measurement Protocol

The first laboratory exercise establishes direct empirical measurement of magnetic field intensity generated by a long solenoid and validates theoretical predictions from Ampère's circuital law. A solenoid functions as a controlled magnetic field source where axial flux density $B$ depends linearly on current $I$ through the relation $B = \mu_0 n I$, with $n$ representing turn density in turns per meter. This configuration isolates magnetic induction from geometric complexity, permitting quantitative extraction of permeability and verification of field uniformity within the solenoid bore.

Begin by selecting a precision-wound air-core solenoid with known geometry. Measure the solenoid length $L$ using digital calipers to 0.1 mm resolution and count the total number of wire turns $N$ by careful visual inspection with magnification. The turn density follows as $n = N/L$, typically ranging from 500 to 2000 turns/m for laboratory apparatus. Mount the solenoid horizontally on a non-magnetic support frame to prevent ferromagnetic interference from steel bench surfaces. Connect the solenoid terminals to the DC power supply through a precision 1-Ω current-sensing resistor configured in series. Position the resistor remote from the solenoid to avoid self-heating perturbations, and verify terminal resistance using a four-wire Kelvin measurement to subtract lead resistance.

Establish the magnetic field measurement axis by inserting a calibrated Hall effect Gaussmeter probe into the solenoid bore along the central axis. The probe must remain fixed at the axial midpoint where field uniformity maximizes and fringe effects vanish. Secure the probe using a non-magnetic positioning stage with micrometer adjustment, ensuring the Hall element active area aligns perpendicular to the expected field direction. Zero the Gaussmeter with the solenoid de-energized to null ambient geomagnetic fields and stray laboratory flux, typically 0.3–0.5 Gauss in magnitude. Record the zero offset as a systematic bias correction, and confirm probe linearity by exposing it to a calibrated permanent magnet reference field.

Configure the DC power supply for constant current operation, setting the current limit to 2 A maximum to prevent resistive overheating. Increment the solenoid current from 0 to 2 A in 0.2 A steps, allowing 10 seconds settling time at each setpoint for thermal stabilization and eddy current dissipation. At each current level, record the Gaussmeter reading in millitesla and the voltage drop across the 1-Ω sense resistor to verify applied current via Ohm's law. Perform three replicate measurements at each current setpoint to quantify random uncertainty from Gaussmeter digitization noise and power supply ripple. The following Python script automates data acquisition assuming PyVISA instrument communication:

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import linregress

# Experimental parameters
N_turns = 800  # Total solenoid turns
L_solenoid = 0.250  # Solenoid length in meters
n_density = N_turns / L_solenoid  # Turn density (turns/m)
mu_0 = 4 * np.pi * 1e-7  # Vacuum permeability (H/m)

# Simulated measurement data (replace with instrument acquisition)
currents = np.arange(0, 2.1, 0.2)  # Amperes
B_measured = np.array([0.0, 0.81, 1.61, 2.42, 3.22, 4.03, 
                        4.84, 5.64, 6.45, 7.25, 8.06]) * 1e-3  # Tesla

# Theoretical prediction
B_theoretical = mu_0 * n_density * currents

# Linear regression for empirical permeability extraction
slope, intercept, r_value, p_value, std_err = linregress(currents, B_measured)
mu_empirical = slope / n_density
relative_error = 100 * (mu_empirical - mu_0) / mu_0

# Visualization
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(14, 5))

ax1.errorbar(currents, B_measured * 1e3, yerr=0.05, fmt='o', 
             label='Measured', capsize=3, markersize=6)
ax1.plot(currents, B_theoretical * 1e3, 'r--', linewidth=2, 
         label='Theory: $B = \\mu_0 n I$')
ax1.set_xlabel('Solenoid Current $I$ (A)', fontsize=12)
ax1.set_ylabel('Axial Magnetic Field $B$ (mT)', fontsize=12)
ax1.set_title('Solenoid Magnetic Field vs Current', fontsize=13, weight='bold')
ax1.legend(fontsize=11)
ax1.grid(alpha=0.3)

residuals = (B_measured - B_theoretical) * 1e3
ax2.plot(currents, residuals, 'o-', color='darkblue', markersize=6)
ax2.axhline(0, color='red', linestyle='--', linewidth=1.5)
ax2.set_xlabel('Solenoid Current $I$ (A)', fontsize=12)
ax2.set_ylabel('Residual $B_{\\mathrm{meas}} - B_{\\mathrm{theory}}$ (mT)', fontsize=12)
ax2.set_title('Measurement Residuals', fontsize=13, weight='bold')
ax2.grid(alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('solenoid_field_calibration.png', dpi=300)
plt.show()

print(f"Solenoid Geometry: N = {N_turns} turns, L = {L_solenoid:.3f} m")
print(f"Turn Density: n = {n_density:.1f} turns/m")
print(f"Theoretical Permeability: μ₀ = {mu_0:.4e} H/m")
print(f"Empirical Permeability: μₑₘₚ = {mu_empirical:.4e} H/m")
print(f"Relative Error: {relative_error:.2f}%")
print(f"Linear Fit: R² = {r_value**2:.6f}, Std Error = {std_err:.4e}")
print(f"Mean Residual: {np.mean(residuals):.4f} mT")
print(f"Residual Std Dev: {np.std(residuals):.4f} mT")
```

The regression analysis extracts an empirical permeability coefficient from the measured slope of the $B$ vs. $I$ response curve. Ideally, the empirical value matches the vacuum permeability $\mu_0$ within combined instrumental uncertainty, typically 1–2% for calibrated Hall probes and precision current sources. Deviations exceeding 5% indicate systematic errors such as incorrect turn count, probe misalignment, or ferromagnetic contamination from mounting hardware. The coefficient of determination $R^2$ must exceed 0.999 to confirm linear field scaling, validating the assumption of negligible magnetic saturation and hysteresis in the air-core geometry.

Residual analysis quantifies random uncertainty and reveals hidden systematic trends. Plot the difference between measured and theoretical field values as a function of current. Random Gaussmeter noise produces zero-mean residuals with standard deviation below 0.1 mT. Systematic curvature in the residual plot indicates field nonlinearity from winding imperfections or current-dependent resistance changes. A non-zero residual offset points to incomplete geomagnetic nulling or Gaussmeter zero drift. Calculate the mean residual to assess bias and the residual standard deviation to characterize precision. These metrics feed into the total measurement uncertainty budget via root-sum-square combination.

## Exercise 2: Parameter Variation, Calibration & Response Curve Plotting

The second laboratory exercise investigates electromagnetic induction by measuring the induced voltage in a secondary coil subjected to time-varying magnetic flux from a driven primary coil. This transformer configuration implements Faraday's law of induction $\mathcal{E} = -N \frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$, where the induced electromotive force scales with turn count $N$ and the temporal derivative of magnetic flux $\Phi_B$. By varying the primary excitation frequency and amplitude, the experimenter maps the inductive coupling coefficient and validates frequency-dependent reactance predictions from AC circuit theory.

Construct a mutual inductance apparatus using two coaxial solenoids with the secondary winding positioned inside the primary coil bore. The primary coil should contain approximately 1000 turns of enameled copper wire wound on a non-conductive cylindrical former with 5 cm diameter. The secondary coil uses 500 turns wound on a slightly smaller diameter former that slides concentrically within the primary bore, maintaining 2–3 mm radial clearance. Measure both coil resistances using a precision multimeter in four-wire mode, typically yielding 10–50 Ω depending on wire gauge. Connect the primary coil terminals to the function generator output through a series 10-Ω current-limiting resistor to prevent amplifier overdrive at low frequencies.

Attach the secondary coil terminals directly to Channel 1 of the digital oscilloscope configured for AC coupling with 10× passive probe compensation. Set the oscilloscope vertical scale to 100 mV/div and horizontal timebase to 200 μs/div for initial 1 kHz excitation. Configure the function generator to produce a sinusoidal output at 1 kHz with 5 V peak-to-peak amplitude. Enable oscilloscope edge triggering on the function generator sync output to stabilize the waveform display. Verify phase alignment by connecting Channel 2 to monitor the primary coil voltage, confirming the 90° phase shift between driving current and induced voltage characteristic of inductive coupling.

Execute a frequency sweep from 100 Hz to 10 kHz while maintaining constant primary voltage amplitude. The induced secondary voltage magnitude should increase linearly with frequency according to $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{rms}} = 2\pi f M I_{\mathrm{rms}}$, where $M$ denotes the mutual inductance coefficient and $I_{\mathrm{rms}}$ represents the RMS primary current. At each frequency, allow three cycles for transient decay, then freeze the oscilloscope display and measure the peak-to-peak secondary voltage using cursor functions. Record the primary current simultaneously by measuring voltage drop across the series sense resistor. Logarithmic frequency spacing optimizes data density across decades, using steps at 100, 200, 500 Hz, then 1, 2, 5 kHz, concluding at 10 kHz.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.optimize import curve_fit

# Experimental parameters
N_primary = 1000  # Primary turns
N_secondary = 500  # Secondary turns
R_sense = 10.0  # Current sense resistor (Ohms)
V_primary_pp = 5.0  # Primary voltage peak-to-peak (V)

# Frequency sweep data (Hz)
frequencies = np.array([100, 200, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000, 10000])

# Measured secondary voltage peak-to-peak (mV)
V_secondary_pp = np.array([15.2, 30.8, 76.5, 153.0, 305.0, 762.0, 1520.0])

# Convert to RMS values
V_secondary_rms = V_secondary_pp / (2 * np.sqrt(2))  # mV RMS

# Primary current calculation (assuming resistive load dominance)
# I_primary_rms ≈ V_primary_rms / (R_coil + R_sense)
R_primary = 25.0  # Measured primary coil resistance (Ohms)
V_primary_rms = V_primary_pp / (2 * np.sqrt(2))
I_primary_rms = V_primary_rms / (R_primary + R_sense)  # Amperes

# Mutual inductance extraction: E = 2πf M I
# M = E / (2πf I)
mutual_inductance = V_secondary_rms * 1e-3 / (2 * np.pi * frequencies * I_primary_rms)
M_mean = np.mean(mutual_inductance)
M_std = np.std(mutual_inductance)

# Theoretical prediction (exact formula requires geometry)
def induced_voltage_model(f, M):
    """Model: V_induced = 2π f M I"""
    return 2 * np.pi * f * M * I_primary_rms * 1e3  # Convert to mV

# Curve fitting to extract best-fit mutual inductance
popt, pcov = curve_fit(induced_voltage_model, frequencies, V_secondary_rms)
M_fit = popt[0]
M_uncertainty = np.sqrt(pcov[0, 0])

# Visualization
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(14, 5))

# Log-log plot of induced voltage vs frequency
ax1.loglog(frequencies, V_secondary_rms, 'o', markersize=8, 
           label='Measured', color='darkblue')
f_theory = np.logspace(2, 4, 100)
V_theory = induced_voltage_model(f_theory, M_fit)
ax1.loglog(f_theory, V_theory, 'r--', linewidth=2, 
           label=f'Fit: $M = {M_fit*1e3:.2f}$ mH')
ax1.set_xlabel('Frequency $f$ (Hz)', fontsize=12)
ax1.set_ylabel('Induced Voltage $\\mathcal{E}_{\\mathrm{RMS}}$ (mV)', fontsize=12)
ax1.set_title('Faraday Induction: Frequency Response', fontsize=13, weight='bold')
ax1.legend(fontsize=11)
ax1.grid(True, which='both', alpha=0.3)

# Mutual inductance consistency across frequency
ax2.semilogx(frequencies, mutual_inductance * 1e3, 'o-', 
             markersize=8, color='darkgreen', linewidth=1.5)
ax2.axhline(M_mean * 1e3, color='red', linestyle='--', 
            linewidth=2, label=f'Mean: {M_mean*1e3:.2f} mH')
ax2.fill_between(frequencies, (M_mean - M_std) * 1e3, 
                  (M_mean + M_std) * 1e3, alpha=0.2, color='red')
ax2.set_xlabel('Frequency $f$ (Hz)', fontsize=12)
ax2.set_ylabel('Extracted Mutual Inductance $M$ (mH)', fontsize=12)
ax2.set_title('Mutual Inductance Frequency Stability', fontsize=13, weight='bold')
ax2.legend(fontsize=11)
ax2.grid(alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('faraday_induction_response.png', dpi=300)
plt.show()

print(f"Primary Coil: N₁ = {N_primary} turns, R₁ = {R_primary:.1f} Ω")
print(f"Secondary Coil: N₂ = {N_secondary} turns")
print(f"Primary Current: I_RMS = {I_primary_rms*1e3:.2f} mA")
print(f"Mutual Inductance (Mean): M = {M_mean*1e3:.3f} ± {M_std*1e3:.3f} mH")
print(f"Mutual Inductance (Curve Fit): M = {M_fit*1e3:.3f} ± {M_uncertainty*1e3:.3f} mH")
print(f"Frequency Range: {frequencies[0]:.0f} Hz to {frequencies[-1]:.0f} Hz")
print(f"Induced Voltage Range: {V_secondary_rms[0]:.2f} to {V_secondary_rms[-1]:.2f} mV RMS")
```

The mutual inductance coefficient should remain constant across the frequency sweep, demonstrating frequency-independent magnetic coupling in the quasi-static regime below self-resonance. Typical values range from 1 to 10 mH depending on coil geometry and separation distance. Frequency-dependent drift exceeding 5% indicates parasitic capacitance or proximity effects altering the effective coupling. The log-log plot of induced voltage versus frequency must exhibit unit slope, confirming the linear frequency dependence predicted by Faraday's law. Deviations from linearity at high frequencies signal the onset of transmission-line effects or core saturation in ferromagnetic materials.

Extend the investigation by varying the axial separation between primary and secondary coils. Mutual inductance decays approximately as the inverse cube of separation distance $d$ for coaxial coils, following $M \propto 1/d^3$ in the far-field limit. Position the secondary coil at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 cm axial offset from the primary coil center. At each position, measure the induced voltage at a fixed 1 kHz excitation frequency. Plot mutual inductance versus separation on log-log axes to extract the power-law exponent, which should approach −3 for separations exceeding the coil radius. Calibration of the mutual inductance measurement requires independent verification through impedance bridge techniques. The primary self-inductance equals the standard inductance at null, typically 10–100 mH for 1000-turn coils. ## Exercise 3: Boundary Condition Stress Testing & Uncertainty Analysis

The third laboratory exercise probes electromagnetic wave propagation through transmission line structures, measuring impedance discontinuities and reflections at boundary conditions. A coaxial cable terminated with variable loads exhibits standing wave patterns governed by the telegrapher's equations, with reflection coefficient $\Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0)$ determined by load impedance $Z_L$ and characteristic impedance $Z_0$. By sweeping the load impedance from short-circuit to open-circuit conditions, the experimenter maps the complex reflection coefficient on a Smith chart and validates transmission line theory through direct time-domain reflectometry measurements.

Select a 1-meter length of RG-58 coaxial cable with nominal 50-Ω characteristic impedance and BNC connectors at both ends. Verify the cable impedance using a time-domain reflectometer or vector network analyzer calibration. Connect one cable end to the function generator output configured for 10 MHz sinusoidal excitation at 1 V peak-to-peak amplitude. The high frequency ensures cable electrical length exceeds λ/10, producing observable standing wave patterns. Attach the oscilloscope Channel 1 probe to the generator-side connector to monitor incident wave voltage. Position a second probe at the cable midpoint by carefully stripping a small section of outer conductor and attaching a non-invasive inductive pickup loop to avoid impedance perturbation.

Configure three termination scenarios: short circuit (0 Ω), matched load (50 Ω precision resistor), and open circuit (infinite impedance). For each termination, measure the standing wave voltage amplitude along the cable by moving the midpoint probe in 10 cm increments from generator to load. The short-circuit termination produces a voltage null at the load end with maximum current, corresponding to reflection coefficient $\Gamma = -1$. The matched load eliminates reflections entirely, yielding uniform voltage amplitude with $\Gamma = 0$. The open circuit generates a voltage maximum at the load end, corresponding to $\Gamma = +1$. The voltage standing wave ratio $\mathrm{VSWR} = (1 + |\Gamma|)/(1 - |\Gamma|)$ quantifies mismatch severity, ranging from 1.0 for perfect matching to infinity for total reflection.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.optimize import fsolve

# Transmission line parameters
Z_0 = 50.0  # Characteristic impedance (Ohms)
cable_length = 1.0  # meters
velocity_factor = 0.66  # RG-58 velocity factor
c = 3e8  # Speed of light (m/s)
v_prop = velocity_factor * c  # Propagation velocity
f = 10e6  # Frequency (Hz)
wavelength = v_prop / f  # Wavelength in cable
beta = 2 * np.pi / wavelength  # Propagation constant

# Spatial positions along cable
z = np.linspace(0, cable_length, 100)

# Termination impedances
Z_short = 0.1  # Short circuit (small residual)
Z_matched = 50.0  # Matched load
Z_open = 1e6  # Open circuit (large impedance)

def reflection_coefficient(Z_L, Z_0):
    """Calculate voltage reflection coefficient"""
    return (Z_L - Z_0) / (Z_L + Z_0)

def voltage_magnitude(z, Z_L, V_inc=1.0):
    """Standing wave voltage magnitude along transmission line"""
    Gamma = reflection_coefficient(Z_L, Z_0)
    # Voltage: V(z) = V_inc * [exp(-jβz) + Γ exp(+jβz)]
    # Magnitude: |V(z)| = |V_inc| * sqrt(1 + |Γ|² + 2|Γ|cos(2βz + φ))
    phase = np.angle(Gamma)
    return V_inc * np.sqrt(1 + np.abs(Gamma)**2 + 
                           2 * np.abs(Gamma) * np.cos(2 * beta * z + phase))

# Calculate voltage distributions
V_short = voltage_magnitude(z, Z_short)
V_matched = voltage_magnitude(z, Z_matched)
V_open = voltage_magnitude(z, Z_open)

# VSWR calculations
Gamma_short = reflection_coefficient(Z_short, Z_0)
Gamma_matched = reflection_coefficient(Z_matched, Z_0)
Gamma_open = reflection_coefficient(Z_open, Z_0)

def vswr(Gamma):
    """Voltage Standing Wave Ratio"""
    return (1 + np.abs(Gamma)) / (1 - np.abs(Gamma))

VSWR_short = vswr(Gamma_short)
VSWR_matched = vswr(Gamma_matched)
VSWR_open = vswr(Gamma_open)

# Simulated measurement with noise
np.random.seed(42)
measurement_positions = np.linspace(0, cable_length, 11)
V_short_meas = voltage_magnitude(measurement_positions, Z_short) + \
               np.random.normal(0, 0.02, len(measurement_positions))
V_matched_meas = voltage_magnitude(measurement_positions, Z_matched) + \
                 np.random.normal(0, 0.02, len(measurement_positions))
V_open_meas = voltage_magnitude(measurement_positions, Z_open) + \
              np.random.normal(0, 0.02, len(measurement_positions))

# Visualization
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(2, 1, figsize=(12, 10))

# Standing wave patterns
ax1.plot(z * 100, V_short, 'b-', linewidth=2, label=f'Short: $\\Gamma = {Gamma_short:.2f}$')
ax1.plot(z * 100, V_matched, 'g-', linewidth=2, label=f'Matched: $\\Gamma = {Gamma_matched:.2f}$')
ax1.plot(z * 100, V_open, 'r-', linewidth=2, label=f'Open: $\\Gamma = {Gamma_open:.2f}$')
ax1.plot(measurement_positions * 100, V_short_meas, 'bo', markersize=6, alpha=0.6)
ax1.plot(measurement_positions * 100, V_matched_meas, 'go', markersize=6, alpha=0.6)
ax1.plot(measurement_positions * 100, V_open_meas, 'ro', markersize=6, alpha=0.6)
ax1.axhline(1.0, color='black', linestyle='--', alpha=0.3, linewidth=1)
ax1.set_xlabel('Position along Cable (cm)', fontsize=12)
ax1.set_ylabel('Normalized Voltage Magnitude $|V(z)|$', fontsize=12)
ax1.set_title('Transmission Line Standing Waves: Boundary Condition Effects', 
              fontsize=13, weight='bold')
ax1.legend(fontsize=11, loc='upper right')
ax1.grid(alpha=0.3)
ax1.set_xlim(0, cable_length * 100)

# Smith chart representation (simplified rectangular plot)
Z_loads = np.logspace(-1, 3, 50)  # Load impedance sweep
Gamma_mag = np.abs([reflection_coefficient(Z_L, Z_0) for Z_L in Z_loads])
Gamma_phase = np.angle([reflection_coefficient(Z_L, Z_0) for Z_L in Z_loads])
VSWR_sweep = [vswr(reflection_coefficient(Z_L, Z_0)) for Z_L in Z_loads]

ax2_twin = ax2.twinx()
ax2.semilogx(Z_loads, Gamma_mag, 'b-', linewidth=2, label='$|\\Gamma|$')
ax2_twin.semilogx(Z_loads, VSWR_sweep, 'r-', linewidth=2, label='VSWR')
ax2.axvline(Z_0, color='green', linestyle='--', linewidth=2, 
            label=f'Matched: $Z_0 = {Z_0}$ Ω')
ax2.set_xlabel('Load Impedance $Z_L$ (Ω)', fontsize=12)
ax2.set_ylabel('Reflection Coefficient $|\\Gamma|$', fontsize=12, color='blue')
ax2_twin.set_ylabel('Voltage Standing Wave Ratio (VSWR)', fontsize=12, color='red')
ax2.set_title('Reflection Coefficient and VSWR vs Load Impedance', 
              fontsize=13, weight='bold')
ax2.tick_params(axis='y', labelcolor='blue')
ax2_twin.tick_params(axis='y', labelcolor='red')
ax2.grid(alpha=0.3)
ax2.legend(loc='upper left', fontsize=11)
ax2_twin.legend(loc='upper right', fontsize=11)
ax2.set_xlim(Z_loads[0], Z_loads[-1])

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('transmission_line_reflections.png', dpi=300)
plt.show()

print(f"Cable Parameters: Z₀ = {Z_0:.1f} Ω, Length = {cable_length:.2f} m")
print(f"Operating Frequency: f = {f/1e6:.1f} MHz")
print(f"Wavelength in Cable: λ = {wavelength*100:.2f} cm")
print(f"Electrical Length: {cable_length/wavelength:.2f} λ")
print(f"\nShort Circuit Termination:")
print(f"  Reflection Coefficient: Γ = {Gamma_short:.3f}")
print(f"  VSWR = {VSWR_short:.1f}")
print(f"\nMatched Termination:")
print(f"  Reflection Coefficient: Γ = {Gamma_matched:.3f}")
print(f"  VSWR = {VSWR_matched:.2f}")
print(f"\nOpen Circuit Termination:")
print(f"  Reflection Coefficient: Γ = {Gamma_open:.3f}")
print(f"  VSWR = {VSWR_open:.1f}")
```

The standing wave measurement reveals the spatial interference pattern between forward and reflected waves. Short-circuit termination produces voltage nodes spaced λ/2 apart, with the first null located exactly at the load position. Matched termination eliminates standing waves entirely, confirming energy absorption in the resistive load. Open-circuit termination generates voltage antinodes at λ/2 intervals, with maximum amplitude at the load. Measure the spatial distance between adjacent voltage minima using the oscilloscope probe position micrometer, then calculate the guided wavelength via $\lambda_g = 2 \times \Delta z_{\mathrm{null}}$. Compare this wavelength to the theoretical prediction $\lambda_g = v_{\mathrm{prop}}/f$ using the cable velocity factor specification.

Uncertainty analysis quantifies measurement precision through repeated trials and error propagation calculations. The dominant uncertainty sources include oscilloscope voltage quantization (±1% of full scale), probe position error (±2 mm), and cable characteristic impedance tolerance (±2 Ω). Propagate position uncertainty through the wavelength calculation using $\delta \lambda_g / \lambda_g = \delta(\Delta z) / \Delta z$, yielding typical wavelength uncertainty of 3–5%. Voltage amplitude uncertainty affects reflection coefficient extraction through $\delta |\Gamma| = \delta V_{\mathrm{max}} / V_{\mathrm{max}} + \delta V_{\mathrm{min}} / V_{\mathrm{min}}$. Combine these uncertainties in quadrature to establish total measurement uncertainty budgets for each derived quantity.

Perform boundary condition stress testing by introducing reactive terminations. Replace the resistive load with a series combination of 50 Ω resistance and variable capacitance ranging from 10 pF to 1 nF. The complex load impedance $Z_L = R + 1/(j\omega C)$ produces frequency-dependent reflection coefficients with both magnitude and phase variation. Sweep the excitation frequency from 1 to 50 MHz while monitoring the standing wave pattern. The reflection coefficient traces a circular arc on the Smith chart, demonstrating the capacitive reactance contribution. Extract the capacitance value by curve-fitting the measured reflection phase versus frequency to the theoretical impedance model.

Extend the investigation to transmission line losses by measuring attenuation through long cable cascades. Connect ten 1-meter RG-58 segments in series using barrel connectors, forming a 10-meter lossy line. Drive one end with 10 MHz excitation and measure voltage amplitude at 1-meter intervals. The exponential decay envelope $V(z) = V_0 \exp(-\alpha z)$ reveals the attenuation constant $\alpha$, typically 0.1–0.2 dB/m at 10 MHz for RG-58. Plot measured voltage on a logarithmic scale versus distance, then extract the attenuation constant from the linear slope via $\alpha = -(1/z) \ln(V/V_0)$. ## Laboratory Cheat Sheet & Metric Verification Guide

**Fundamental Electromagnetic Constants (SI Units)**

- Vacuum Permittivity: $\epsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}$ F/m (exact: $1/(4\pi \times 10^{-7} c^2)$)
- Vacuum Permeability: $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}$ H/m (exact by definition)
- Speed of Light: $c = 299792458$ m/s (exact by definition)
- Impedance of Free Space: $Z_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0/\epsilon_0} = 376.73$ Ω
- Elementary Charge: $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C
- Electron Rest Mass: $m_e = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg

**Maxwell's Equations (Differential Form in Vacuum)**

$$\nabla \cdot \vec{E} = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0} \quad \text{(Gauss's law)}$$

$$\nabla \cdot \vec{B} = 0 \quad \text{(No magnetic monopoles)}$$

$$\nabla \times \vec{E} = -\frac{\partial \vec{B}}{\partial t} \quad \text{(Faraday's law)}$$

$$\nabla \times \vec{B} = \mu_0 \vec{J} + \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \vec{E}}{\partial t} \quad \text{(Ampère-Maxwell law)}$$

**Instrumentation Quick Reference**

Digital Storage Oscilloscope Configuration:
- Bandwidth requirement: ≥5× maximum signal frequency to avoid waveform distortion
- Sample rate: ≥10× bandwidth per Nyquist criterion (100 MS/s minimum for 10 MHz signals)
- Vertical resolution: 8-bit minimum (±0.4% quantization), 12-bit preferred for precision work
- Probe compensation: Adjust 10× probe capacitance to eliminate square wave overshoot
- Triggering: Edge trigger on rising edge at 50% amplitude, normal mode (not auto)
- AC coupling: Engages 0.1 μF high-pass filter, blocking DC components below ~1 Hz

Function Generator Output Impedance:
- Standard output: 50 Ω source impedance (requires matched load for correct amplitude)
- Open-circuit voltage doubles when driving high-impedance loads (oscilloscope input)
- Use BNC tee adapter and 50-Ω terminator for impedance-matched measurements

Hall Effect Gaussmeter Calibration:
- Zero field nulling: Shield probe in μ-metal enclosure, adjust zero offset to < 0.1 mT
- Linearity verification: Test with calibrated permanent magnet (0.1–0.5 T range)
- Temperature compensation: Allow 5-minute thermal stabilization before measurements
- Probe orientation: Active Hall element perpendicular to field direction (±5° alignment)

**Solenoid Magnetic Field Calculations**

For a long solenoid (length $L \gg$ diameter $D$) carrying current $I$:

$$B_{\mathrm{axis}} = \mu_0 n I = \mu_0 \frac{N}{L} I$$

Edge correction factor for finite-length solenoid:

$$B(z) = \frac{\mu_0 n I}{2} \left[ \frac{L/2 + z}{\sqrt{(L/2 + z)^2 + R^2}} + \frac{L/2 - z}{\sqrt{(L/2 - z)^2 + R^2}} \right]$$

where $z$ is axial position measured from solenoid center and $R$ is solenoid radius. At the center ($z = 0$), this reduces to:

$$B(0) = \mu_0 n I \frac{L}{\sqrt{L^2 + 4R^2}}$$

**Faraday Induction and Mutual Inductance**

Induced EMF in a coil with $N$ turns subjected to flux change:

$$\mathcal{E} = -N \frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$

For sinusoidal flux $\Phi_B = \Phi_0 \sin(\omega t)$:

$$\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{peak}} = N \omega \Phi_0 = 2\pi f N \Phi_0$$

Mutual inductance between primary (subscript 1) and secondary (subscript 2) coils:

$$M = k \sqrt{L_1 L_2}$$

where $k$ is the coupling coefficient (0 ≤ $k$ ≤ 1). For coaxial solenoids with secondary inside primary:

$$M \approx \mu_0 \frac{N_1 N_2 A}{L_1}$$

where $A$ is the cross-sectional area and $L_1$ is the primary length.

**Transmission Line Equations**

Characteristic impedance for coaxial cable:

$$Z_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{\mu_r}{\epsilon_r}} \ln\left(\frac{D}{d}\right) \times 60 \, \Omega$$

where $D$ is the inner diameter of outer conductor, $d$ is the outer diameter of center conductor, $\mu_r$ is relative permeability (≈1 for air), and $\epsilon_r$ is the relative permittivity of dielectric.

Reflection coefficient at a load:

$$\Gamma = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}$$

Voltage standing wave ratio:

$$\mathrm{VSWR} = \frac{1 + |\Gamma|}{1 - |\Gamma|} = \frac{V_{\mathrm{max}}}{V_{\mathrm{min}}}$$

Return loss (dB):

$$\mathrm{RL} = -20 \log_{10}|\Gamma|$$

**Error Propagation and Uncertainty Analysis**

For a derived quantity $Q = f(x, y, z, \ldots)$ with independent measured variables:

$$\delta Q = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\delta x\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\delta y\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\delta z\right)^2 + \cdots}$$

Relative uncertainty in product/quotient $Q = xy/z$:

$$\frac{\delta Q}{Q} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\delta x}{x}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\delta y}{y}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\delta z}{z}\right)^2}$$

Standard error of the mean for $N$ repeated measurements:

$$\sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\sigma_x}{\sqrt{N}}$$

where $\sigma_x$ is the sample standard deviation.

**Standard Operating Procedures for Safe Laboratory Practice**

Electromagnetic laboratory work involves high-voltage power supplies, high-frequency RF signals, and sensitive precision instrumentation. Observe these mandatory safety protocols:

1. **Electrical Safety**: De-energize all circuits before making physical connection changes. Use insulated tools rated for the working voltage. Verify power supply current limits before energizing inductive loads. Ground all metal instrument chassis to earth through three-wire power cords.

2. **RF Exposure Limits**: Maintain 30 cm minimum separation from transmitting antennas operating above 100 mW. Use shielded enclosures for high-power oscillator circuits. Monitor field strength with calibrated RF survey meter when testing above 1 W output power.

3. **Magnetic Field Exposure**: Gaussmeter readings exceeding 1 mT (10 Gauss) near solenoid bores warrant increased vigilance. Remove ferromagnetic objects (watches, tools, implanted devices) from high-field zones. Post signage warning of strong magnetic fields exceeding 5 mT.

4. **Instrument Protection**: Never exceed oscilloscope input voltage ratings (typically ±400 V peak on 1× setting). Use attenuator probes for signals exceeding 10 V. Verify function generator load impedance before connecting to prevent amplifier damage. Store precision instruments in temperature-controlled, low-humidity environments.

5. **Data Integrity**: Record all raw measurements in bound laboratory notebooks with permanent ink. Include date, time, instrument serial numbers, and calibration dates. Archive digital data with ISO 8601 timestamps and descriptive filenames. Maintain chain-of-custody documentation for critical measurements.

**Troubleshooting Common Experimental Pathologies**

*Solenoid Field Measurement Shows Nonlinear Current Dependence*: Check for ferromagnetic contamination in mounting hardware (steel screws, iron bench clamps). Verify Gaussmeter probe remains fixed at the solenoid geometric center. Confirm power supply operates in constant-current mode, not constant-voltage. Inspect solenoid windings for short circuits between adjacent turns.

*Induced Voltage Measurement Exhibits Frequency-Independent Amplitude*: Verify function generator drives primary coil directly, not through unintended series capacitance. Confirm oscilloscope AC coupling properly passes lowest test frequency (100 Hz requires >1 μF coupling capacitor). Check for open secondary coil connections or broken winding continuity. Re-measure primary current to ensure constant drive amplitude.

*Transmission Line Standing Wave Pattern Absent or Irregular*: Confirm function generator frequency produces electrical length exceeding λ/4 (cable length > 5 m at 10 MHz for RG-58). Verify coaxial connector integrity using TDR or cable tester. Check termination impedance accuracy with precision ohmmeter. Replace suspect barrel connectors introducing impedance discontinuities.

*Oscilloscope Waveform Displays Excessive Noise or Instability*: Enable oscilloscope bandwidth limiting (20 MHz filter) to suppress aliasing from out-of-band interference. Use twisted-pair or coaxial cables for all signal connections to reduce pickup. Confirm oscilloscope trigger couples to stable signal edge, not noise floor. Ground probe shield directly to DUT ground reference point, not remote earth ground.

*Mutual Inductance Extraction Shows Large Frequency Variation*: Recalculate primary current accounting for coil reactance ($X_L = 2\pi f L$) in addition to DC resistance. Verify secondary coil remains centered within primary bore during frequency sweep. Check for parasitic capacitance between primary and secondary windings causing resonance peaks. Measure self-resonant frequency of both coils using impedance analyzer.
