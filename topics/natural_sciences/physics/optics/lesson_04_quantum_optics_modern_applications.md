# Lesson 4: Quantum Optics Principles, Laser Physics, Modern Systems

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The classical wave theory of light, while extraordinarily successful in describing interference, diffraction, and polarization phenomena, encounters insurmountable obstacles when confronted with experimental observations at the atomic scale. The photoelectric effect, first systematically investigated by Philipp Lenard and subsequently explained by Einstein in 1905, demonstrates that electromagnetic radiation exhibits discrete, particle-like energy packets rather than continuous waves. When monochromatic light illuminates a metal surface, electrons are ejected only if the light frequency exceeds a threshold value, regardless of intensity. This immediate, threshold-dependent response cannot be reconciled with classical wave mechanics, which predicts that any intensity should eventually accumulate sufficient energy to liberate electrons.

Einstein's radical hypothesis posited that light consists of quantized energy packets—photons—each carrying energy $E = h\nu$ where $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s represents Planck's constant and $\nu$ denotes the electromagnetic wave frequency. The photon momentum follows from relativistic invariants as $p = E/c = h\nu/c = h/\lambda$, establishing the wave-particle duality that permeates quantum mechanics. This quantization fundamentally alters our understanding of light-matter interactions, transforming the continuous electromagnetic field into a quantum field where excitations manifest as discrete photons occupying quantized energy states.

The quantum mechanical treatment of the electromagnetic field begins with the vector potential formulation, where the electric and magnetic fields derive from potentials through $\vec{E} = -\nabla\phi - \partial\vec{A}/\partial t$ and $\vec{B} = \nabla \times \vec{A}$. Imposing the Coulomb gauge condition $\nabla \cdot \vec{A} = 0$ and working in a quantization volume $V$ with periodic boundary conditions, the vector potential expands as a Fourier series over plane wave modes characterized by wave vector $\vec{k}$ and polarization states. Each mode behaves as an independent quantum harmonic oscillator with frequency $\omega = c|\vec{k}|$, permitting the introduction of creation and annihilation operators $\hat{a}^\dagger_{\vec{k},\lambda}$ and $\hat{a}_{\vec{k},\lambda}$ that respectively add or remove a photon from mode $(\vec{k}, \lambda)$. These operators satisfy the bosonic commutation relation $[\hat{a}_{\vec{k},\lambda}, \hat{a}^\dagger_{\vec{k}',\lambda'}] = \delta_{\vec{k},\vec{k}'}\delta_{\lambda,\lambda'}$, encoding the fundamental indistinguishability of photons and their capacity to occupy identical quantum states without restriction.

The quantized electromagnetic Hamiltonian decomposes into a sum over independent harmonic oscillators:

$$\hat{H}_{\text{field}} = \sum_{\vec{k},\lambda} \hbar\omega_{\vec{k}}\left(\hat{a}^\dagger_{\vec{k},\lambda}\hat{a}_{\vec{k},\lambda} + \frac{1}{2}\right)$$

The number operator $\hat{n}_{\vec{k},\lambda} = \hat{a}^\dagger_{\vec{k},\lambda}\hat{a}_{\vec{k},\lambda}$ counts photons in each mode, with eigenvalues $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ corresponding to Fock states $|n\rangle_{\vec{k},\lambda}$ of definite photon number. The zero-point energy contribution $\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega$ per mode, though formally divergent when summed over all modes, manifests physically in phenomena such as the Casimir effect and spontaneous emission, where vacuum fluctuations mediate observable forces and decay processes.

Light-matter interactions fundamentally alter atomic energy level populations through three distinct quantum processes elucidated by Einstein in his 1917 derivation of Planck's blackbody radiation law. Consider a two-level atom with ground state $|g\rangle$ at energy $E_g$ and excited state $|e\rangle$ at energy $E_e$, separated by transition energy $\hbar\omega_0 = E_e - E_g$. When immersed in a radiation field of spectral energy density $\rho(\omega)$, atoms undergo spontaneous emission, wherein an excited atom decays to the ground state by emitting a photon into any available electromagnetic mode, proceeding at rate $A_{eg}$ independent of the ambient field. This process originates from vacuum field fluctuations coupling the atomic dipole moment to empty electromagnetic modes, compelling the excited state to decay with characteristic lifetime $\tau_{\text{sp}} = 1/A_{eg}$.

Stimulated emission and absorption represent field-dependent processes wherein interaction with photons at frequency $\omega \approx \omega_0$ induces transitions between atomic levels. Absorption transfers energy from the field to the atom, promoting ground state atoms to the excited state at rate $B_{ge}\rho(\omega)$, while stimulated emission forces excited atoms to decay coherently, adding photons to the incident mode at rate $B_{eg}\rho(\omega)$. The Einstein B coefficients relate through detailed balance arguments applied to thermal equilibrium, yielding $B_{ge} = B_{eg} \equiv B$ for non-degenerate levels. Crucially, spontaneous and stimulated emission rates relate through $A_{eg} = (\hbar\omega_0^3/\pi^2 c^3)B$, establishing that spontaneous emission dominates at high frequencies while stimulated processes govern low-frequency behavior.

The quantum coherence properties of stimulated emission distinguish it from spontaneous decay. When a photon stimulates an excited atom to emit, the emitted photon emerges with identical frequency, phase, polarization, and propagation direction as the stimulating photon—perfect coherence preservation that enables optical amplification. This coherent amplification forms the physical basis of laser operation, requiring population inversion where the excited state population $N_e$ exceeds the ground state population $N_g$, violating thermal equilibrium to achieve net optical gain.

Population inversion cannot occur in a simple two-level system under continuous wave illumination because absorption and stimulated emission rates balance when $N_e = N_g$, clamping the inversion at zero. Practical laser systems exploit three-level or four-level energy structures where an external pumping mechanism selectively populates an upper state that rapidly decays to a metastable level exhibiting slow spontaneous emission. In a three-level scheme pioneered by Maiman in the first ruby laser, optical pumping excites ground state atoms to a short-lived pump band that quickly relaxes to a metastable excited state. This excited state accumulates population because its decay rate remains small compared to the pump rate, eventually exceeding the ground state population despite continuous spontaneous emission losses. Four-level systems improve efficiency by introducing a lower laser level distinct from the ground state, requiring only that the excited laser level population exceed this lower level population rather than the much larger ground state population.

The optical gain coefficient $\alpha(\omega)$ quantifies amplification strength, defined through the intensity evolution $dI/dz = \alpha(\omega)I$ where $I$ denotes optical intensity propagating through the gain medium. Quantum mechanical perturbation theory applied to the atomic dipole transition yields the gain spectrum:

$$\alpha(\omega) = \frac{\sigma(\omega)(N_e - N_g)}{1 + I/I_{\text{sat}}}$$

Here $\sigma(\omega)$ represents the stimulated emission cross-section capturing the transition strength and lineshape, typically Lorentzian for homogeneously broadened transitions with width determined by dephasing processes or Gaussian for inhomogeneously broadened ensembles. The saturation intensity $I_{\text{sat}}$ marks the point where stimulated emission depletes the population inversion, reducing gain as $I_{\text{sat}} = h\nu/(\sigma\tau_{\text{sp}})$. This saturation mechanism provides automatic gain stabilization in laser oscillators, clamping the intracavity intensity at the level required to balance gain and loss.

A laser oscillator emerges when an optical gain medium resides within a resonant cavity providing optical feedback through mirrors that recirculate photons through the gain region. The cavity sustains longitudinal electromagnetic modes at discrete frequencies $\omega_q = q\pi c/L$ where $q$ represents an integer mode number and $L$ denotes the cavity length, with adjacent modes separated by the free spectral range $\Delta\omega_{\text{FSR}} = \pi c/L$. Oscillation initiates when the round-trip optical gain exceeds round-trip loss, expressed through the threshold condition:

$$\exp(2\alpha L) \cdot R_1 R_2 \geq 1$$

where $R_1$ and $R_2$ denote mirror reflectivities and $L$ represents the gain medium length. At threshold, spontaneous emission noise seeds the cavity modes, which grow exponentially until gain saturation establishes steady-state operation balancing gain and loss. The output power coupling through a partially transmitting mirror scales as $P_{\text{out}} = (T/2L)[(\alpha_0/\alpha_{\text{th}}) - 1]AI_{\text{sat}}$ where $T$ represents output mirror transmission, $\alpha_0$ denotes small-signal gain, $\alpha_{\text{th}}$ marks threshold gain, and $A$ represents mode area.

Transverse electromagnetic modes define the spatial field distribution within the cavity, characterized by Hermite-Gaussian or Laguerre-Gaussian basis functions for stable resonators with spherical mirrors. The fundamental Gaussian mode $\text{TEM}_{00}$ exhibits a radial intensity profile $I(r) = I_0\exp(-2r^2/w^2)$ where $w$ denotes the beam waist radius, minimizing diffraction losses and providing maximum overlap with the gain medium. Higher-order transverse modes $\text{TEM}_{mn}$ display $(m+n)$ nodes along orthogonal transverse directions, oscillating at slightly shifted frequencies due to Gouy phase accumulation. Mode selection through intracavity apertures restricting diffraction loss or spatial gain profiling enforces single transverse mode operation essential for applications requiring high beam quality.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

Practical laser construction demands careful integration of the gain medium, optical resonator, and pump source into a coherent system maintaining population inversion while preserving optical mode quality. The gain medium selection determines operational wavelength, output power scaling, efficiency, and pulse characteristics. Gas lasers such as helium-neon, argon-ion, and carbon dioxide systems exploit atomic or molecular transitions in low-pressure discharges, achieving high beam quality and wavelength stability through homogeneous gain profiles and large mode volumes. Solid-state crystalline hosts doped with rare-earth ions (Nd:YAG, Yb:YAG, Ti:sapphire) or transition metals provide compact, high-power operation with optical or diode laser pumping schemes. Semiconductor laser diodes based on direct bandgap III-V compounds (GaAs, InP, GaN) integrate gain and cavity within micron-scale active regions, enabling mass production and electrical pumping at unprecedented wall-plug efficiency exceeding 50%.

The helium-neon laser exemplifies three-level gas laser operation, utilizing neon atomic transitions near 632.8 nm wavelength within a sealed glass tube containing He:Ne mixture at approximately 1:5 ratio and total pressure 1-3 Torr. Radio frequency or DC electrical discharge excites helium atoms to metastable $2^3S_1$ and $2^1S_0$ states through electron impact, with these long-lived states accumulating population due to forbidden radiative decay. Collisional energy transfer from excited helium to neon atoms in near-resonant $5s$ and $4s$ states selectively pumps upper laser levels while leaving lower levels relatively unpopulated. The 632.8 nm red transition proceeds from the $5s$ to $3p$ neon manifold, with rapid radiative decay from $3p$ to $3s$ and collisional relaxation clearing the lower level. Brewster angle windows terminating the plasma tube eliminate p-polarization reflection losses while transmitting s-polarization, enforcing linear polarization and minimizing intracavity loss. External dielectric mirrors with reflectivities $R_1 > 99.9\%$ (high reflector) and $R_2 \approx 99\%$ (output coupler) complete the resonator, yielding typical output powers of 1-50 mW with exceptional beam quality and frequency stability limited by thermal cavity length fluctuations.

Solid-state laser systems based on Nd:YAG (neodymium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet) crystals demonstrate four-level operation near 1064 nm wavelength, achieving continuous wave or pulsed operation with output powers ranging from milliwatts to kilowatts. The YAG host crystal lattice substitutes Nd³⁺ ions at 1% atomic concentration, providing broadband optical absorption around 808 nm that matches commercially available InGaAs diode laser arrays. Optical pumping excites Nd³⁺ ions from the ground $^4I_{9/2}$ manifold to pump bands near $^4F_{5/2}$ that rapidly relax through multiphonon emission to the metastable $^4F_{3/2}$ upper laser level with 230 μs radiative lifetime. Laser action proceeds to the $^4I_{11/2}$ lower manifold, which promptly decays to the ground state, maintaining the lower level nearly empty and enabling efficient four-level operation. End-pumped rod geometries focus diode pump light into the fundamental cavity mode volume, optimizing spatial overlap and achieving slope efficiencies exceeding 50% (optical output power versus absorbed pump power).

The Ti:sapphire laser exploits the extraordinarily broad gain bandwidth spanning 650-1100 nm, enabling femtosecond pulse generation through mode-locking techniques. Titanium ions (Ti³⁺) substituted into sapphire (Al₂O₃) crystal lattice sites couple strongly to lattice phonons, producing homogeneously broadened gain with over 200 THz bandwidth centered near 790 nm. This broad spectrum supports transform-limited pulses as short as 5 fs containing only a few optical cycles. Optical pumping typically employs frequency-doubled Nd:YAG or Nd:YVO₄ lasers at 532 nm green wavelength, exciting Ti³⁺ from the ground $^2T_2$ state to the excited $^2E$ manifold. Kerr lens mode-locking, wherein the optical Kerr effect induces intensity-dependent refractive index changes creating self-focusing for high-intensity pulses, discriminates against continuous wave operation by enhancing intracavity aperture transmission for pulsed modes. Dispersion compensation through prism pairs or chirped mirrors maintains transform-limited pulse duration by canceling group velocity dispersion accumulated during cavity round-trips.

Semiconductor laser diodes achieve population inversion through electrical current injection across a forward-biased p-n junction in direct bandgap materials. The active region typically consists of quantum well structures with thickness 5-10 nm, providing carrier confinement and discrete energy levels enhancing radiative recombination. Separate confinement heterostructures surround the quantum wells with wider bandgap cladding layers, confining optical modes through refractive index steps while maintaining electrical injection. Cleaved facets perpendicular to the waveguide axis provide Fresnel reflection (≈30% for GaAs-air interface), forming a compact cavity with length 300-500 μm yielding free spectral range 100-200 GHz. Current injection above threshold density $J_{\text{th}} \approx 1$ kA/cm² establishes population inversion, with output power scaling linearly as $P = \eta(I - I_{\text{th}})$ where $\eta$ represents differential quantum efficiency typically 0.3-0.7 W/A.

Distributed feedback (DFB) lasers incorporate a periodic Bragg grating within the waveguide structure, providing wavelength-selective feedback through Bragg reflection at wavelengths satisfying $\lambda = 2n_{\text{eff}}\Lambda/m$ where $n_{\text{eff}}$ denotes effective refractive index, $\Lambda$ represents grating period, and $m$ marks the diffraction order. This distributed feedback mechanism eliminates cavity facet reflections, enforcing single longitudinal mode operation with linewidths below 1 MHz crucial for coherent optical communications. Vertical-cavity surface-emitting lasers (VCSELs) orient the cavity perpendicular to the substrate surface, utilizing distributed Bragg reflector (DBR) mirrors formed by alternating quarter-wave optical thickness layers. The short cavity length (≈1 μm) yields enormous mode spacing (≈100 THz) guaranteeing single longitudinal mode operation, while the circular emission aperture produces symmetric Gaussian beams ideal for fiber coupling.

Nonlinear optical phenomena emerge when intense electromagnetic fields induce medium responses beyond the linear polarization $\vec{P} = \epsilon_0\chi^{(1)}\vec{E}$. The nonlinear polarization expands as a power series:

$$\vec{P} = \epsilon_0\left(\chi^{(1)}\vec{E} + \chi^{(2)}\vec{E}\vec{E} + \chi^{(3)}\vec{E}\vec{E}\vec{E} + \cdots\right)$$

The second-order susceptibility $\chi^{(2)}$ vanishes in centrosymmetric media but drives three-wave mixing processes in non-centrosymmetric crystals such as lithium niobate (LiNbO₃), potassium titanyl phosphate (KTP), and beta barium borate (BBO). Second harmonic generation (SHG) converts two photons at fundamental frequency $\omega$ into a single photon at $2\omega$, doubling the optical frequency while conserving energy and momentum. The conversion efficiency scales as $\eta_{\text{SHG}} \propto |d_{\text{eff}}|^2L^2I_\omega\text{sinc}^2(\Delta kL/2)$ where $d_{\text{eff}}$ represents the effective nonlinear coefficient, $L$ denotes crystal length, and $\Delta k = k_{2\omega} - 2k_\omega$ quantifies phase mismatch arising from material dispersion.

Phase matching techniques synchronize the phase velocities of fundamental and harmonic waves, maximizing conversion efficiency by maintaining constructive interference throughout propagation. Birefringent phase matching exploits crystal anisotropy, orienting the fundamental wave as ordinary polarization and harmonic as extraordinary polarization (or vice versa) such that refractive index dispersion cancels: $n_o(\omega) = n_e(2\omega, \theta)$ where $\theta$ denotes propagation angle relative to the crystal optic axis. Quasi-phase matching employs periodically poled ferroelectric domains with period $\Lambda = 2\pi/\Delta k$, introducing $\pi$ phase shifts compensating accumulated phase mismatch and enabling larger effective nonlinearity by permitting collinear propagation along maximum $d_{\text{eff}}$ crystal axes.
