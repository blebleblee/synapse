# Lesson 1: First-Principles Physical Laws & Conservation Invariants

## Curriculum Roadmap

This lesson establishes the axiomatic foundation from which all orbital mechanics, stellar dynamics, and space mission design flows. Before a spacecraft can be steered, before a transit light curve can be interpreted, and before a launch window can be calculated, the physicist must have an intimate command of the invariants that the universe refuses to violate. Those invariants are not abstract mathematical conveniences. They are the load-bearing walls of every physical model in astronomy and spaceflight, and treating them as such is the difference between a practitioner who can derive solutions under pressure and one who can only recall memorized formulas.

The curriculum proceeds in three tightly coupled stages. The first establishes the historical and phenomenological context: why these laws were needed, what observational crisis each one resolved, and how their experimental signatures appear in real data. The second stage builds the mathematical machinery with full dimensional rigor, connecting each conservation law to its underlying symmetry through Noether's theorem, and showing how those laws are measured, tested, and exploited in modern observatories and mission control. The third stage assembles these pieces into a unified phenomenological model of a gravitationally bound system, culminating in a Mermaid diagram that maps the causal flow from physical symmetry to observable quantity.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to derive the equation of motion for a two-body gravitational system from the Lagrangian, identify which physical quantity is conserved at each step of a spacecraft maneuver, propagate measurement uncertainty through a conservation-law calculation, and read a real ephemeris table with enough physical intuition to flag anomalies. These are practitioner skills, and they will be exercised with concrete numerical examples and working Python throughout.

## 1. First-Principles Problem Space & Historical Phenomenon

The history of celestial mechanics is a history of anomalies forcing deeper invariants into the open. When Tycho Brahe spent two decades between 1576 and 1601 accumulating naked-eye positional data on Mars from his island observatory at Uraniborg, his goal was vindication of a modified geocentric cosmology. What his data actually delivered, through Kepler's ten-year struggle with its residuals, was the first empirical proof that planetary orbits are conic sections, not circles, and that the speed of a planet is not constant but is instead governed by a hidden conserved quantity. Kepler's second law, that a line drawn from the Sun to a planet sweeps equal areas in equal times, is a statement of conservation of angular momentum written in the language of geometry. Kepler did not know that. He had no concept of angular momentum. He simply fit the data and reported what it demanded.

Newton's achievement in the *Principia* of 1687 was to recognize that Kepler's three empirical laws were not independent facts but were all consequences of a single force law: the inverse-square gravitational attraction between any two masses. The practical test of Newton's synthesis was not philosophical but predictive. Edmond Halley used Newton's framework to retrodict the appearances of a comet in 1531, 1607, and 1682 as the same object on a 75-year period, and to predict its return in 1758. The comet arrived on Christmas Day of that year, sixteen years after Halley's death, and in doing so it demonstrated that conservation of energy and momentum applied not just to laboratory pendulums but to objects traversing the entire solar system. That is the kind of experimental validation that earns an axiom its status.

The specific observational crisis that demanded conservation of energy in its full thermodynamic form came later and from a different direction. By the mid-nineteenth century, astronomers were troubled by the question of what powers the Sun. The chemical combustion hypothesis was demolished by simple calculation: burning hydrogen at the Sun's observed luminosity would exhaust a solar mass of fuel in roughly 5,000 years, far too short to be consistent with geological evidence for an ancient Earth. Helmholtz and Kelvin independently proposed that the Sun was slowly contracting under its own gravity, converting gravitational potential energy into radiated heat — the Kelvin-Helmholtz mechanism. This was the correct application of energy conservation, and it extended the solar lifetime to roughly 20 million years. It was still too short, as Rutherford would eventually point out at a famous 1904 lecture where he held a sample of radioactive ore and noted dryly that nuclear energy had extended the Sun's budget considerably. Nuclear fusion, confirmed as the solar energy source by Hans Bethe in 1939, is itself governed by conservation of mass-energy through Einstein's $E = mc^2$, the relativistic completion of the energy invariant.

These historical episodes share a common structure. An observational anomaly or an inadequate predictive model exposed a gap. That gap was closed by identifying a new conserved quantity or by extending an existing one to a broader domain. The conserved quantity was not invented; it was discovered by demanding internal consistency between measurements and the evolving model. This is exactly the epistemic posture the working astronomer must maintain. When a spacecraft's trajectory deviates from the predicted ephemeris, the first question is always: which conservation law is being violated, and by what physical mechanism? Is it atmospheric drag dissipating energy? A radiation pressure asymmetry transferring momentum? An unmodeled gravitational gradient coupling orbital and rotational angular momentum? The conservation invariants are not just theoretical ornaments. They are the first diagnostic tools.

**Practical Checkpoint: Reading a Horizons Ephemeris**

Before proceeding to the mathematical formalism, the reader should have a live ephemeris in hand. NASA's JPL Horizons system (accessible at `https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/horizons/`) provides state vectors — position and velocity in Cartesian coordinates — for every solar system body at any epoch. Navigate to the web interface, set the target body to `Mars [499]`, the observer location to `@sun` (heliocentric), the time range to any 30-day window at 1-day steps, the output reference frame to `ICRF`, and the output quantities to include state vectors. The resulting table will give you columns in the form `X Y Z VX VY VZ` with units of AU and AU/day. The specific numbers are less important than what they represent: a complete mechanical state from which every conserved quantity in this lesson can be computed directly. Download this table and keep it open. Numerical examples throughout this lesson will refer back to it.

The position vector $\vec{r}$ and velocity vector $\vec{v}$ in that ephemeris table are the two fundamental state variables of classical orbital mechanics. Everything else — angular momentum, energy, the orbital elements, the period — is a function of these two vectors evaluated at a single moment. That is the operational meaning of the conservation laws: they collapse a six-dimensional state to a small set of scalar and vector invariants that do not change as the body moves along its orbit.
