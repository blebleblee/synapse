# Lesson 4: Alkanes, Alkenes, Alkynes, Aromatic Compounds

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The architecture of every hydrocarbon reaction in organic chemistry descends from a single organizing principle: the hybridization state of carbon determines its geometry, its bond strengths, and its susceptibility to attack. When carbon is $sp^3$-hybridized, four sigma bonds arranged tetrahedrally at $109.5°$ produce the saturated, chemically resistant framework of alkanes. The promotion of one $s$ and two $p$ orbitals into $sp^2$ hybrids collapses the geometry to trigonal planar at $120°$ and liberates an unhybridized $p$ orbital perpendicular to the plane; two such orbitals on adjacent carbons overlap sideways to form a $\pi$ bond, creating the electron-rich, nucleophilic character of alkenes. Push further to $sp$ hybridization—one $s$ orbital mixed with one $p$ orbital, leaving two $p$ orbitals free—and the linear $180°$ geometry of alkynes emerges, with two orthogonal $\pi$ bonds constituting a cylindrical electron cloud of remarkable density and, paradoxically, remarkable stability.

These hybridization differences carry direct thermodynamic consequences that govern every reaction in this chapter. The $s$-character of a hybrid orbital correlates inversely with its orbital radius: an $sp$ orbital holds electrons closer to the nucleus than an $sp^2$ orbital, which in turn holds them closer than an $sp^3$ orbital. This means an $sp$-hybridized carbon is effectively more electronegative than its $sp^2$ or $sp^3$ counterparts, and the C–H bond of a terminal alkyne ($pK_a \approx 25$) is acidic enough to be deprotonated by amide bases such as sodium amide ($NaNH_2$, $pK_a$ of $NH_3 \approx 38$), whereas an alkane C–H bond ($pK_a \approx 50$) is essentially inert to anything short of a radical abstraction. Bond dissociation energies (BDEs) tell the same story from the radical perspective: a typical primary C–H bond in an alkane costs approximately $423 \text{ kJ/mol}$ to homolyze, a secondary C–H about $414 \text{ kJ/mol}$, and a tertiary C–H about $406 \text{ kJ/mol}$. This ordering—tertiary bonds weakest, primary bonds strongest—will reappear as the central selectivity argument in radical halogenation and as the driving force for carbocation rearrangements.

The thermodynamic engine behind all of these reactions is the Gibbs free energy relation, which determines whether a transformation is spontaneous under given conditions:

$$\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$$

where $\Delta G$ is the change in Gibbs free energy ($\text{kJ/mol}$), $\Delta H$ is the enthalpy change ($\text{kJ/mol}$), $T$ is the absolute temperature ($\text{K}$), and $\Delta S$ is the entropy change ($\text{J/(mol·K)}$).
