# Lesson 5: Pragmatics, Discourse, Language Use

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Language exceeds the boundaries of syntax and semantics. When a speaker utters "Can you pass the salt?" at a dinner table, the grammatical structure encodes an interrogative about ability, yet no competent interlocutor responds with a mere "Yes" and continues eating. The utterance functions as a request, not a question about physical capacity. This divergence between encoded meaning and communicated intention constitutes the fundamental puzzle of pragmatics: how do language users systematically derive speaker meaning from sentence meaning, and what principles govern the interpretation of utterances in context?

Pragmatics investigates language as action embedded in social interaction. Where phonology examines sound patterns, morphology dissects word-internal structure, syntax maps hierarchical phrase architecture, and semantics computes truth-conditional meaning, pragmatics confronts the dynamic, context-dependent dimension of linguistic communication. The core insight derives from J.L. Austin's revolutionary observation in the 1950s that utterances constitute performative acts. To say "I pronounce you married" in the appropriate institutional setting does not describe a pre-existing state of affairs but rather brings that state into being through the speech act itself. This recognition destabilized the logical positivist assumption that all meaningful language must be verifiable or falsifiable, opening theoretical space for analyzing language as social action.

The invariants governing pragmatic interpretation rest on a foundational tension between linguistic underdetermination and communicative success. Natural language systematically underdetermines speaker meaning. The sentence "The bank is around the corner" leaves the referent of "bank" radically ambiguous between financial institution and river edge, yet successful communication rarely founders on such ambiguities. Interlocutors resolve reference, recover implicit content, detect indirect speech acts, and navigate conversational implicatures with remarkable fluency. This inferential machinery operates through context-sensitive reasoning about speaker intentions, shared knowledge, relevance relations, and cooperative principles structuring interaction.

Three theoretical pillars anchor contemporary pragmatic theory. First, speech act theory provides taxonomic structure for utterance types as actions: locutionary acts (producing meaningful sounds), illocutionary acts (performing social actions through utterances), and perlocutionary acts (achieving effects on hearers). Second, Gricean implicature theory articulates how hearers derive non-literal meanings through reasoning about cooperative maxims governing quantity, quality, relation, and manner of contribution. Third, relevance theory reformulates pragmatic inference as a cognitive process optimizing the balance between processing effort and contextual effects, grounded in the presumption that every ostensive communicative act carries a guarantee of optimal relevance.

Discourse pragmatics extends these principles to multi-utterance sequences, investigating how information packages across clauses, how topic and focus structure guide attention, how coherence relations link propositions, and how turn-taking mechanisms regulate conversational interaction. Where sentence-level pragmatics asks how utterances convey speaker intentions, discourse pragmatics examines how sequences of utterances construct coherent text, manage reference across spans, and coordinate joint attention in dialogue. The shift from isolated sentences to discourse units parallels the transition from static structural analysis to dynamic processing models, foregrounding temporal sequencing, incremental interpretation, and interactional co-construction of meaning.

The empirical foundation for pragmatic theory derives from systematic observation of language use in naturalistic contexts. Conversational analysis emerged from ethnomethodology in the 1960s, pioneered by Harvey Sacks, Emanuel Schegloff, and Gail Jefferson, who discovered that seemingly chaotic everyday talk exhibits intricate orderliness at the micro-level of turn construction, sequence organization, and repair mechanisms. This bottom-up, data-driven approach revealed universal conversational structures such as adjacency pairs (question-answer, greeting-greeting, offer-acceptance/refusal), preference organization (dispreferred responses marked by delay, hedging, and accounts), and transition relevance places where speaker change becomes appropriate. These discoveries demonstrated that pragmatic competence involves not merely propositional content recovery but mastery of complex interactional choreography.

Politeness theory, developed by Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson, integrates pragmatic analysis with sociological face theory, examining how speakers navigate the tension between communicative efficiency and social relationship management. All competent language users possess positive face (the desire to be appreciated and approved) and negative face (the desire for autonomy and freedom from imposition). Many speech acts inherently threaten face: requests impose on negative face, criticism attacks positive face, apologies admit face damage to the speaker. Languages provide systematic resources for mitigating face threats through indirect formulation, hedging, honorifics, and deference strategies. The cross-linguistic variation in politeness systems correlates with cultural parameters of social distance, power asymmetry, and ranking of imposition, yet the underlying face concerns appear to be pragmatic universals.

Deixis constitutes another pragmatic universal, albeit with language-specific morphosyntactic realizations. Deictic expressions anchor utterance interpretation to features of the speech situation: personal deixis (pronouns like "I," "you," "we"), spatial deixis (demonstratives "this," "that," adverbs "here," "there"), temporal deixis (tenses, adverbs "now," "yesterday," "next week"), and discourse deixis (anaphoric reference "the former," "the latter," "as mentioned above"). The interpretation of deictic terms requires identifying contextual parameters: who is speaking, to whom, where, when, and what prior discourse is salient. Deictic center typically defaults to the speaker's spatiotemporal location in face-to-face conversation, but narrative and free indirect discourse permit complex center shifts. The ubiquity of deixis across languages underscores that all human communication is indexically grounded, inherently tied to contexts of use rather than floating free as abstract propositional content.

Information structure governs how propositional content maps onto prosodic and syntactic form to reflect discourse status. Every utterance packages information according to what the speaker presumes is shared common ground versus new, what constitutes the topic under discussion versus comment about that topic, and what element receives focal stress. These distinctions manifest through word order variation, clefting constructions, prosodic prominence, and specialized particles. In English, focal stress typically falls on the rightmost constituent, and deviations signal contrastive focus. The sentence "Mary bought a CAR" (with nuclear stress on "car") presupposes that Mary bought something and asserts that the thing was a car, suitable as an answer to "What did Mary buy?" By contrast, "MARY bought a car" (with stress on "Mary") presupposes someone bought a car and asserts that the buyer was Mary, answering "Who bought a car?" These intuitions about felicitous contexts for alternative prosodic contours reveal that information structure constraints govern utterance production and interpretation as rigorously as syntactic well-formedness conditions.

The explanatory adequacy of pragmatic theory demands accounting for both universals and variation. Speech act typology appears cross-linguistically stable: all languages permit speakers to assert, question, command, promise, apologize, and perform other core illocutionary acts, though the conventional means for performing these acts vary. Gricean maxims have been criticized as culturally parochial, reflecting Anglo-Saxon conversational norms rather than universal rationality. Relevance theory claims greater universality by grounding pragmatic inference in cognitive architecture rather than social convention, yet the ranking of contextual assumptions available for deriving relevance surely varies across cultures and individuals. Politeness strategies exhibit systematic cross-linguistic patterns, but the threshold for what counts as face-threatening varies dramatically. Coherence relations and discourse markers show both universal inventory (causation, contrast, elaboration, temporal succession) and language-specific grammaticalization paths. The challenge for pragmatic theory is to articulate the universal cognitive and social mechanisms that generate cross-linguistic pragmatic competence while accounting for the parametric variation in how languages encode pragmatic distinctions.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

Speech act theory provides the foundational taxonomy for utterances as actions. Austin distinguished three dimensions of every speech act. The locutionary act comprises the production of phonetic, phatic, and rhetic acts: producing sounds, assembling those sounds into grammatical structures according to language-specific rules, and using those structures with determinate sense and reference. The illocutionary act constitutes the social action performed in uttering the locution: asserting, questioning, commanding, promising, warning, declaring. The perlocutionary act encompasses the effects achieved through the illocutionary act: persuading, frightening, amusing, inspiring. The analytical focus of speech act theory falls on illocutionary acts, which are conventional in nature. Perlocutionary effects are causal and contingent, while locutionary acts are purely linguistic. Illocutionary acts sit at the interface between linguistic form and social function.

John Searle refined Austin's taxonomy, proposing five master categories of illocutionary acts differentiated by direction of fit between words and world. Representatives (or assertives) carry word-to-world direction of fit, purporting to describe states of affairs: assertions, descriptions, claims, reports. Directives carry world-to-word direction of fit, attempting to get the hearer to make the world match the propositional content: requests, commands, advice, invitations. Commissives also carry world-to-word fit but commit the speaker rather than the hearer to future action: promises, pledges, vows, threats. Expressives have null direction of fit, expressing psychological states about propositions presupposed to be true: apologies, thanks, congratulations, condolences. Declarations alter institutional reality through their utterance, simultaneously achieving word-to-world and world-to-word fit: pronouncing marriage, adjourning meetings, declaring war, firing employees. This typology rests on felicity conditions specifying the preparatory, sincerity, and essential conditions required for successful performance of each act type.

Indirect speech acts complicate the mapping between sentence type and illocutionary force. Interrogative syntax conventionally signals questions, imperative syntax signals commands, declarative syntax signals assertions. Yet "Can you pass the salt?" conventionally functions as a request despite interrogative form, "I would appreciate it if you closed the door" functions as a directive despite declarative form, and "You will submit the report by Friday" functions as a command despite superficial future tense assertion. Searle analyzed indirect speech acts as performing two illocutionary acts simultaneously: a primary act (the intended request) and a secondary literal act (the surface question about ability). The hearer recognizes the primary act through reasoning about felicity conditions. Asking whether the hearer has the ability to pass salt is pointless unless that ability is somehow in question; the mutual knowledge that salt-passing ability is unproblematic triggers the inference that the speaker must be indirectly requesting the action.

This inferential mechanism connects to Grice's theory of conversational implicature. Grice proposed that rational conversation is governed by a Cooperative Principle: "Make your conversational contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange in which you are engaged." This principle unfolds into four maxims. The maxim of Quantity enjoins speakers to make contributions as informative as required but not more informative than required. The maxim of Quality demands that speakers assert only what they believe to be true and for which they have adequate evidence. The maxim of Relation stipulates relevance. The maxim of Manner prescribes perspicuity: avoid obscurity and ambiguity, be brief and orderly.

Conversational implicatures arise when a speaker flouting a maxim triggers the hearer to infer that the speaker is nevertheless maintaining the Cooperative Principle at a higher level. Consider the exchange: A: "How is John doing in his new job?" B: "Well, he hasn't been arrested yet." B's response appears to violate the Quantity maxim by providing less information than requested and the Relation maxim by failing to directly address job performance. The hearer reasons that B must be cooperative, therefore B intends to convey something beyond the literal content. The implicated meaning is that John's job performance is poor or problematic, communicated indirectly through mentioning a minimal threshold (avoiding arrest) that should be irrelevant to normal job evaluation. Gricean implicature thus provides a model for how hearers systematically recover non-literal meanings through abductive reasoning about speaker intentions and cooperative norms.

Grice distinguished conversational implicatures from conventional implicatures and presuppositions. Conversational implicatures are defeasible (cancellable without contradiction), calculable from maxims and context, non-detachable (cannot be avoided by paraphrasing while preserving literal content), and not part of conventional meaning. Conventional implicatures, by contrast, are encoded in lexical items like "but," "even," "therefore," which carry implications beyond truth-conditional content. The sentence "She is poor but honest" implicates a contrast between poverty and honesty, yet this contrast is conventional rather than derived through maxim-based reasoning. Presuppositions, such as the existence implication of definite descriptions ("The King of France is bald" presupposes France has a king), project through embeddings and survive negation, distinguishing them from both types of implicature.

Relevance theory, developed by Dan Sperber and Deirdre Wilson, reformulates pragmatic inference as a cognitive rather than social phenomenon. The theory rests on two principles. The Cognitive Principle of Relevance states that human cognition is geared toward maximizing relevance, where relevance is defined as a function of cognitive effects (strengthening, contradicting, or combining with existing assumptions to yield contextual implications) weighted against processing effort required to derive those effects. The Communicative Principle of Relevance states that every ostensive communicative act carries a presumption of optimal relevance: the act is relevant enough to be worth the hearer's processing effort, and it is the most relevant one compatible with the speaker's abilities and preferences.

Under relevance theory, utterance interpretation proceeds through a comprehension heuristic: follow a path of least effort in deriving cognitive effects (constructing explicatures through disambiguation, reference resolution, enrichment, and ad hoc concept construction; deriving implicatures through forward inference); stop when expectations of relevance are satisfied. This mechanism eliminates Grice's maxims, deriving similar predictions about implicature without invoking cooperative principles. Consider "A: Is John a good sailor? B: He's English." Relevance theory predicts that the hearer will access the most accessible contextual assumptions that yield adequate cognitive effects, such as the stereotype that English people are good sailors. The hearer stops processing when this implicature achieves sufficient relevance, without explicitly calculating Quantity or Relation maxim violations.

Deixis constitutes the class of linguistic expressions whose interpretation is inherently context-dependent, requiring identification of features of the utterance situation. Personal deixis includes pronouns differentiating speaker, addressee, and others, with languages varying in whether they grammatically mark inclusive versus exclusive first-person plural, levels of formality (T/V distinction in European languages), and gender. Spatial deixis centers on demonstratives and locative adverbs, distinguishing proximal ("this," "here") from distal ("that," "there") relative to the speaker's location, with some languages encoding finer-grained distinctions (proximal to speaker, proximal to addressee, distal from both). Temporal deixis manifests in tense systems and temporal adverbs, calibrating event time relative to speech time. Discourse deixis includes anaphoric pronouns and expressions like "the aforementioned," "as follows," which point to elements of the co-text rather than physical context.

Deictic expressions require identifying a deictic center, the origin point for indexical anchoring. In unmarked face-to-face conversation, the deictic center aligns with the speaker's spatiotemporal location: "I" refers to the speaker, "you" to the addressee, "here" to the speaker's location, "now" to speech time. However, narratives and reported speech permit center shifts. Free indirect discourse in literary fiction blends narrator and character perspective, allowing "now" and "here" to reflect the character's viewpoint while maintaining third-person reference. Epistolary deixis adjusts "here" and "now" to the writer's context even though the reader occupies a different location and time. These shifts demonstrate that deictic interpretation is not mechanically tied to physical speech situations but operates through a cognitive model of the communicative context, which may be actual or projected.

Information structure partitions the propositional content of an utterance according to its discourse status. The topic-focus articulation distinguishes what the utterance is about (topic) from what is predicated about the topic (focus). Topics typically denote discourse-old referents already established in the common ground, while focus marks the informationally salient portion of the utterance, answering an implicit or explicit question under discussion. Topic-prominent languages like Japanese and Korean possess syntactic particles marking topics (wa in Japanese), permitting topic-comment structures independent of grammatical subject-predicate organization. Subject-prominent languages like English rely more on word order and prosody, though constructions like left-dislocation ("As for John, he already left") permit explicit topic marking.

The given-new distinction crosscuts topic-focus, tracking whether referents are already in the discourse model (given) or newly introduced (new). Given information tends to appear early in the utterance, realized as unstressed pronouns or definite descriptions, while new information appears later and receives prosodic prominence. The interaction between topic-focus and given-new generates a four-way typology: given topic (continuing the same topic without adding new predication), new topic (shifting to a new topic), given focus (contrastive focus on a previously mentioned element), new focus (introducing a new focal referent). Languages deploy prosody, word order, morphology, and specialized constructions to encode these distinctions.

Coherence relations structure the semantic and pragmatic connections between discourse units. Mann and Thompson's Rhetorical Structure Theory identifies a taxonomy of relations including elaboration (one unit provides additional detail about another), cause (one unit presents a cause for another), result (one unit presents a consequence), contrast (units present contrasting situations), condition (one unit presents a condition for another), concession (one unit acknowledges a potential objection), and purpose (one unit presents a goal). These relations are not explicitly marked in every case but must be inferred by the hearer to construct a coherent discourse representation.

Discourse markers and connectives conventionally signal specific coherence relations, though they often underdetermine the precise relation. "Because" indicates causation but leaves open whether the causation is semantic (real-world cause-effect) or epistemic (evidential warrant for an inference). "However" signals contrast but does not specify the dimension of contrast. "So" marks inference but could indicate either result or conclusion. The interpretation of discourse markers requires integrating their conventional meaning with contextual reasoning about discourse structure, paralleling the pragmatic enrichment required for lexical interpretation more generally.

Conversational analysis investigates the sequential organization of talk-in-interaction through fine-grained transcription and analysis of naturally occurring conversation. The fundamental unit is the turn-constructional unit (TCU), a segment of speech that could constitute a complete turn, identified through syntactic, prosodic, and pragmatic completion. Transition-relevance places (TRPs) occur at TCU boundaries, where speaker change becomes appropriate without overlap constituting an interruption. Turn-taking is governed by a local management system: at a TRP, the current speaker may select the next speaker (through address terms, gaze, or questions); if no speaker is selected, any participant may self-select; if neither occurs, the current speaker may continue.

Adjacency pairs constitute the minimal unit of sequence organization: two utterances by different speakers, where the first part (question, greeting, invitation, request) creates a normative expectation for an appropriate second part (answer, greeting, acceptance/refusal, compliance/refusal). Failure to produce a second pair part is noticeably absent, requiring interactional work (repair, accounts, meta-comments). Dispreferred responses (refusals, rejections, disagreements) are systematically marked by delay, hedges, accounts, and mitigated formulations, while preferred responses occur immediately and directly. This preference structure shapes the temporal and linguistic form of turns independent of the individual speaker's personal preferences, demonstrating the social normativity of conversational organization.

Repair mechanisms handle interactional troubles, including mishearings, misunderstandings, word searches, and referential ambiguities. Self-initiated self-repair occurs when the current speaker stops and corrects or clarifies their own talk, often mid-TCU ("I saw Mary— I mean Jane"). Other-initiated self-repair occurs when an interlocutor signals trouble, prompting the original speaker to clarify ("A: I saw her. B: Who? A: Mary"). Self-initiated other-repair and other-initiated other-repair are progressively dispreferred, reflecting the interactional pressure for speakers to maintain responsibility for managing their own talk. The organization of repair demonstrates that conversational participants orient to intelligibility and intersubjectivity as locally managed achievements rather than pre-given conditions.

Politeness theory integrates face concerns with speech act theory to explain indirectness and mitigation strategies. Brown and Levinson identify four super-strategies for performing face-threatening acts (FTAs). Bald on-record strategy performs the FTA directly without mitigation ("Give me that"), appropriate when efficiency outweighs face concerns or when the FTA benefits the hearer. Positive politeness attends to the hearer's positive face through solidarity markers, compliments, agreement, and in-group identity tokens ("Hey buddy, could you help me out?"). Negative politeness attends to the hearer's negative face through apology, hedging, pessimism, and deference ("I'm terribly sorry to bother you, but I was wondering if you might possibly be able to…"). Off-record strategy performs the FTA through implicature, allowing the speaker plausible deniability ("It's cold in here" as an indirect request to close a window).

The choice among strategies is predicted by the weightiness of the FTA, calculated from three sociological variables: the power differential between speaker and hearer (P), the social distance between them (D), and the ranking of imposition in the culture (Rx). The formula Wx = D(S,H) + P(H,S) + Rx predicts that higher values correspond to more polite strategies. Cross-linguistic comparison reveals that languages provide varied morphosyntactic resources for encoding politeness, including honorific systems (Japanese, Korean), grammaticalized T/V pronouns (European languages), interrogative syntax for directives (English), and elaborate indirect request formulas, but the underlying face concerns and strategic logic appear universal.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Pragmatic competence in practice demands integrating multiple inference systems operating under processing constraints. Real-time utterance interpretation requires simultaneously resolving reference, computing implicatures, tracking discourse structure, identifying speech acts, and updating common ground, all within the few hundred milliseconds available before the next utterance arrives. The optimization problem facing both language users and computational models is how to allocate processing resources to achieve adequate comprehension without exhausting cognitive capacity.

Relevance-theoretic comprehension predicts that interpretation follows a path of least effort, accessing contextual assumptions in order of accessibility and stopping when expectations of relevance are satisfied. This heuristic optimizes the trade-off between processing effort and cognitive effects, but it also generates failure modes when accessible assumptions yield insufficient effects or when processing constraints prevent accessing less accessible but more relevant assumptions. Garden-path pragmatic effects occur when an initially plausible but ultimately incorrect implicature is derived, requiring reanalysis. Consider overhearing "The hostess was very generous with the wine" in a context where wine quality rather than quantity is salient. The accessible assumption might be that generous refers to providing expensive wine, but if the conversation continues "Everyone got completely drunk," reanalysis reveals that generous referred to serving large quantities. The initial interpretation satisfied local relevance but proved globally incoherent.

Information structure mismatches between speaker and hearer models of common ground produce communicative failure. When a speaker treats a referent as given (using a pronoun or reduced description) but the hearer's discourse model does not include that referent as salient, reference resolution fails. Conversely, when a speaker treats information as new (using prosodic prominence and indefinite description) while the hearer already has that information active, the utterance strikes the hearer as redundant or patronizing. Optimal information packaging requires accurate modeling of the interlocutor's knowledge state, which is itself an inference problem subject to uncertainty. Over-accommodation (treating more as given than actually is) produces unintelligibility; under-accommodation (treating as new what is already given) produces redundancy. The challenge is compounded in multilateral conversations where participants have differential access to prior discourse and background knowledge.

Politeness strategy selection optimizes face management but creates efficiency costs. Negative politeness strategies involving hedging, indirectness, and subordination increase syntactic and pragmatic complexity, demanding greater processing effort for both production and comprehension. "I was wondering if you might possibly be able to consider perhaps helping me with this" requires decoding multiple layers of mitigation to extract the core request, whereas "Help me with this" is immediately transparent. Languages and cultures that routinely employ high-politeness strategies thus impose systematic processing overhead in exchange for smoother social interaction. The optimization problem is not merely linguistic but social: what trade-off between communicative efficiency and face preservation best serves the current interaction?

Cross-cultural pragmatic transfer constitutes a major failure mode in second-language communication and intercultural interaction. Transfer of politeness norms from the first language to second-language contexts generates mismatches between intended and perceived face-work. English speakers learning Japanese often under-use honorifics, producing utterances that strike native speakers as brusque or presumptuous, despite the speaker intending neutral politeness. Conversely, Japanese speakers learning English often over-use hedging and negative politeness, producing utterances that strike native speakers as excessively tentative or uncertain. These failures occur not at the level of grammatical competence but at the level of pragmatic calibration of form to social context.

Implicature comprehension failures arise when hearers lack access to the contextual assumptions required to derive intended meanings. Metaphor and indirect criticism particularly depend on shared cultural knowledge and evaluative schemas. "John's a real Einstein" as sarcastic criticism implicates that John is unintelligent, but only for hearers who recognize that the utterance violates the Quantity maxim (if John were genuinely brilliant, simply stating that would suffice) and who infer that the speaker intends ironic meaning. Second-language learners and individuals on the autism spectrum often exhibit difficulty with implicature recovery, particularly for complex or culturally-specific implicatures, while performing at native levels on literal comprehension and explicit communication.

Deixis resolution demands continuous maintenance of the deictic center and participant structure. Failure modes include deictic center confusion in complex narratives with free indirect discourse, where readers may lose track of whose perspective "now" and "here" reflect. Pronoun resolution fails when multiple potential antecedents match morphosyntactic features, requiring pragmatic reasoning about topicality and coherence relations to disambiguate. Demonstrative choice in languages with distance-encoded systems requires accurate spatial modeling: Japanese "kore/sore/are" (proximal/medial/distal) selection depends on joint attention and physical arrangement of speaker, hearer, and referent, and mis-selection strikes native speakers as pragmatically anomalous even when reference succeeds.

Discourse coherence breaks down when hearers cannot identify plausible coherence relations linking utterances. Non-sequitur responses to questions ("A: What time is it? B: The moon is made of cheese") force the hearer to expend massive processing effort searching for a coherent interpretation, typically failing and concluding that the speaker is uncooperative, confused, or intoxicated. Subtle coherence failures occur when the intended relation is recoverable but requires significant inference. Academic writing that omits explicit discourse markers between paragraphs forces readers to reconstruct argument structure through content analysis alone, imposing processing costs that explicit connectives ("Therefore," "However," "Furthermore") would mitigate.

Turn-taking failures in conversation produce overlaps and gaps that require repair. Projecting TCU completion to time turn-entry appropriately demands real-time parsing of syntax, prosody, and pragmatic action completion. Mis-projection leads to interruptions (turn-entry before TRP) or gaps (delayed turn-entry after TRP). Overlaps are not random but cluster at TRPs, indicating that turn-entry is timed by projection of completion. Repair mechanisms exist precisely because the projection system is probabilistic rather than deterministic, and interactional synchrony requires local correction mechanisms when projection fails.

Computational modeling of pragmatic phenomena confronts the frame problem: pragmatic inference requires selecting relevant contextual assumptions from an unbounded set of world knowledge. Gricean maxims provide high-level constraints but underdetermine inference without a model of relevance. Relevance theory provides a processing heuristic but requires operationalizing accessibility and cognitive effects, which remain computationally intractable for open-domain interpretation. Current natural language processing systems handle conventional implicatures and routine indirect speech acts through pattern recognition, but novel implicatures requiring abductive reasoning over world knowledge remain largely unsolved. The success of large language models at pragmatic tasks likely derives from statistical learning over massive pragmatic regularities in training data rather than explicit implementation of pragmatic theory.

Experimental pragmatics employs psycholinguistic methods to test processing predictions of pragmatic theories. Eye-tracking during reading reveals that implicature computation incurs processing cost: readers slow down at points where implicatures must be derived, and this cost increases with the inferential distance between literal meaning and implicated meaning. Event-related potentials (ERPs) show distinct neural signatures for different types of pragmatic violations: N400 components for semantic anomalies, P600 components for syntactic violations, and late sustained negativities for pragmatic infelicities. These findings indicate that pragmatic processing is neurally distinct from semantic and syntactic processing, supporting modular theories of pragmatic competence.

Developmental pragmatics tracks the acquisition of speech act understanding, implicature comprehension, and conversational competence. Children acquire the ability to perform basic speech acts (requesting, asserting) early, around age two, but mastery of indirect speech acts and complex implicature emerges later, continuing into middle childhood. Theory of mind capacity—the ability to represent others' mental states—appears to be a prerequisite for pragmatic inference, since deriving implicatures requires modeling speaker intentions and knowledge states. Children with autism spectrum disorders often show delayed or impaired pragmatic development despite intact syntactic and semantic abilities, supporting the hypothesis that pragmatic competence depends on distinct social-cognitive mechanisms.

Cross-linguistic variation in pragmatic encoding creates optimization trade-offs for language design. Languages that grammaticalize evidentiality (marking whether information is witnessed, inferred, or reported) impose a production cost on speakers (who must encode evidential distinctions in every utterance) but reduce the interpretive burden on hearers (who need not infer epistemic stance). Languages without grammatical evidentiality place greater demand on pragmatic inference but allow greater encoding flexibility. Similarly, languages with elaborate honorific systems impose morphological complexity but reduce pragmatic ambiguity in face-work. The evolutionary stability of both types suggests that different optimization strategies can achieve comparable communicative success in different cultural-linguistic ecosystems.

Pragmatic enrichment of logical operators reveals that linguistic meaning systematically underdetermines truth-conditional content. The conjunction "and" encodes logical conjunction but pragmatically enriches to temporal sequence ("She got married and had children" implies temporal order, unlike "She had children and got married"). Disjunction "or" encodes inclusive disjunction but pragmatically strengthens to exclusive ("Tea or coffee?" implicates not both). Conditional "if" encodes material conditional but pragmatically restricts to relevant conditionals with causal or evidential connections. These enrichments are defeasible but default, suggesting that pragmatic processing routinely adjusts logical form to derive contextually appropriate interpretations.

Scalar implicature constitutes a test case for theories of pragmatic architecture. "Some" logically means "at least one, possibly all," but pragmatically implicates "not all" through Quantity reasoning: if the speaker knew that all held, they would have said "all," so saying "some" implicates ignorance or falsity of "all." Experimental evidence shows that scalar implicature computation is resource-demanding, more so for children than adults, and subject to individual differences in working memory and cognitive control. The debate centers on whether implicature is computed through default semantic rules or through pragmatic inference. Default theories predict implicature computation is automatic and cost-free; pragmatic theories predict it is optional and effortful. Empirical evidence favors the pragmatic view: implicature computation shows processing cost, is context-dependent, and is sometimes suspended under processing load.

The interaction between semantics and pragmatics defines a central architectural question: where does linguistic meaning end and pragmatic inference begin? Minimalist theories restrict semantic content to truth-conditional content recoverable from grammar and lexicon alone, relegating all context-dependent enrichment to pragmatics. Contextualist theories allow pragmatic processes to penetrate deeply into semantic composition, deriving what-is-said through pragmatic modulation of lexical meaning, free enrichment, and ad hoc concept construction. The debate has consequences for linguistic methodology: minimalists can study semantics independent of pragmatic context, while contextualists insist that semantic theory must account for context-sensitivity as a core feature of linguistic meaning.

Optimizing pragmatic instruction for second-language learners requires explicit instruction in implicature reasoning, politeness strategies, and discourse structure that native speakers acquire implicitly through social interaction. Textbooks that focus exclusively on grammatical form and literal meaning leave learners pragmatically incompetent, capable of producing grammatical but socially inappropriate utterances. Effective pragmatic pedagogy includes exposure to authentic discourse, explicit teaching of speech act formulas and politeness conventions, metapragmatic discussion of face management, and extensive practice with implicature recovery. Cross-cultural pragmatic awareness—understanding that pragmatic norms vary systematically—is essential for avoiding pragmatic failure in intercultural communication.

The applied domains of pragmatics span clinical linguistics (assessing pragmatic impairments in neurological and developmental disorders), forensic linguistics (analyzing deception, threat assessment, and speaker identification through pragmatic markers), human-computer interaction (designing dialogue systems with appropriate pragmatic competence), and intercultural communication training (developing cross-cultural pragmatic awareness). In each domain, the challenge is translating theoretical models of pragmatic competence into practical assessment instruments, training protocols, or computational implementations that operate robustly in real-world contexts.
