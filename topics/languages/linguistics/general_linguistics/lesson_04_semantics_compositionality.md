# Lesson 4: Semantics, Compositionality, Formal Models

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Semantics occupies the interface between linguistic form and the world it describes. Where phonology governs sound and syntax governs structure, semantics asks the fundamental question: what does an utterance mean, and how do we compute that meaning from the parts assembled by syntactic machinery? The core insight animating modern formal semantics is that meaning is compositional—the meaning of a complex expression derives systematically from the meanings of its constituents and the syntactic rules combining them. This principle, often attributed to Gottlob Frege and formalized in the twentieth century by Richard Montague, Alfred Tarski, and Barbara Partee, transforms semantics from impressionistic description into a rigorous mathematical enterprise.

Truth-conditional semantics anchors meaning in correspondence to the world. A declarative sentence has meaning insofar as it possesses truth conditions: the configuration of reality under which the sentence would be true. The sentence "Snow is white" means what it means because we can specify the state of affairs—snow possessing the property of whiteness—that would render it true. This approach sidesteps psychological states, speaker intentions, and cultural context, isolating the core denotational relationship between linguistic expressions and their referents. The power of truth-conditional semantics lies in its predictive precision: once we establish compositional rules mapping syntactic structures onto logical forms, we can mechanically derive the truth conditions of arbitrarily complex sentences we have never encountered before.

Compositionality demands a formal calculus. Natural language syntax generates hierarchical structures—trees where terminal nodes host lexical items and non-terminal nodes encode phrasal projections. Semantics must traverse this tree bottom-up, combining meanings at each node until we reach the root and obtain a propositional interpretation. The lambda calculus, borrowed from mathematical logic and computer science, provides the machinery. Lambda abstraction allows us to represent functions that await arguments: the expression λx.LOVES(x, Mary) denotes a function mapping any individual x to the proposition that x loves Mary. Function application combines meanings: when the verb phrase denoted by λx.LOVES(x, Mary) encounters the subject "John," we apply the function to the argument, yielding LOVES(John, Mary). This elegant mechanism handles predicate-argument structure, adjectival modification, relative clauses, and higher-order operators with uniform formal precision.

Quantification introduces indeterminacy and scope ambiguity. Sentences like "Every student read a book" do not refer to specific individuals but quantify over sets. The universal quantifier "every" asserts a relation between two sets—students and individuals who read a book—claiming the first is a subset of the second. The existential quantifier "a" merely claims the set is non-empty. Crucially, quantifiers exhibit scope: they bind variables across syntactic domains. The sentence "Every student read a book" admits two readings depending on whether the universal or existential quantifier takes wide scope. Under the surface scope reading, for every student, there exists some book they read (the books may differ). Under the inverse scope reading, there exists a particular book such that every student read it. Scope ambiguity arises from the interaction of multiple quantifiers, negation, modals, and intensional operators, and formal semantics must predict which scope configurations a given syntactic structure licenses.

Presupposition and entailment distinguish varieties of semantic inference. Entailment is monotonic: if sentence A entails sentence B, then whenever A is true, B must be true. "John killed the intruder" entails "The intruder died." Presupposition, by contrast, projects through negation and questions. "John stopped smoking" presupposes "John smoked previously," and this presupposition survives in "John didn't stop smoking" and "Did John stop smoking?" Presuppositions arise from definite descriptions, factive verbs, aspectual predicates, and cleft constructions. They constrain the common ground—the shared assumptions speakers rely upon—and their projection behavior through complex embeddings (under negation, conditionals, modals, attitude verbs) reveals deep architectural properties of semantic composition. The presupposition projection problem asks which presuppositions of embedded clauses percolate to the matrix clause and under what conditions they can be blocked or suspended.

Model-theoretic interpretation grounds semantics in set theory. A model consists of a domain of individuals, a set of possible worlds (for intensional operators), and an interpretation function mapping linguistic expressions onto entities in the model. Proper names denote individuals; common nouns denote sets of individuals; intransitive verbs denote sets of individuals; transitive verbs denote sets of ordered pairs. Logical connectives (negation, conjunction, disjunction) receive their classical Boolean interpretations. Quantifiers are interpreted as relations between sets: the universal quantifier ∀ maps two sets A and B to true if and only if A ⊆ B; the existential quantifier ∃ maps them to true if and only if A ∩ B ≠ ∅. Model theory allows us to define entailment formally: sentence A entails sentence B if and only if in every model where A is true, B is also true. This framework, developed by Tarski and extended by Montague, converts semantic questions into rigorous mathematical problems admitting proof-theoretic solutions.

Lexical semantics dissects word meaning into structured representations. Verbs decompose into event structures featuring participants, thematic roles, and aspectual properties. The verb "kill" denotes a causative event: an agent performs an action causing a patient to become dead. The aktionsart (lexical aspect) distinguishes states (know, love), activities (run, swim), accomplishments (build a house, draw a circle), and achievements (arrive, recognize). Accomplishments are telic—they have inherent endpoints—and durative, while achievements are telic and punctual. These distinctions predict grammatical behavior: accomplishments and achievements resist the progressive when focused on the culmination point ("*John is arriving right now" is marked), whereas activities combine freely with durational adverbials. Event semantics, pioneered by Donald Davidson and elaborated by Terence Parsons and James Higginbotham, posits an implicit event argument in the logical form of verbs, allowing adverbials and modifiers to predicate over events rather than multiplying verb arity arbitrarily.

The invariants governing compositional semantics form a tight constraint lattice. Function-argument application must respect type compatibility: a function of type ⟨e, t⟩ (mapping individuals to truth values) can only apply to an argument of type e. Type mismatches signal ungrammaticality or require type-shifting operations—coercion mechanisms that adjust the semantic type of an expression to satisfy combinatorial requirements. Montague's intensional logic introduces types s (possible worlds) and i (times), allowing predicates to vary in truth value across worlds and times, essential for handling modals, tense, and attitude verbs. The principle of compositionality itself acts as a metatheoretical constraint: any viable semantic theory must demonstrate how complex meanings arise from simpler constituents via explicit combinatorial rules. Violations of compositionality—idioms, metaphors, lexicalized collocations—mark the boundary between productive grammatical computation and memorized lexical entries.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The semantic interpretation pipeline begins with syntactic structure and terminates in a logical form suitable for truth-conditional evaluation. Syntactic trees generated by phrase structure rules or Merge operations provide the scaffolding; semantic interpretation rules decorate each node with a denotation. The fundamental architecture embodies a homomorphism between syntactic composition and semantic composition: every syntactic operation corresponds to a semantic operation, preserving structural relationships.

At the lexical level, each morpheme receives a denotation of a specific semantic type. Proper names like "Aristotle" denote individuals of type e. Common nouns like "philosopher" denote characteristic functions of type ⟨e, t⟩, mapping each individual to true if that individual is a philosopher, false otherwise. Intransitive verbs share this type: "snores" maps individuals to truth values. Transitive verbs like "admires" have type ⟨e, ⟨e, t⟩⟩, a function taking an individual (the object) and returning a function that takes another individual (the subject) and returns a truth value. This currying strategy decomposes polyadic relations into iterated unary functions, facilitating compositional combination.

Function application proceeds by recursive tree traversal. Consider the simple sentence "Aristotle snores." The syntactic structure combines the subject NP "Aristotle" with the VP "snores." The NP denotes the individual Aristotle (type e); the VP denotes a function from individuals to truth values (type ⟨e, t⟩). Semantic interpretation applies the function to its argument, yielding the proposition SNORES(Aristotle). For transitive constructions, consider "Plato admires Aristotle." The verb "admires" combines first with its object "Aristotle," yielding λx.ADMIRES(x, Aristotle), a function of type ⟨e, t⟩. This derived VP then combines with the subject "Plato," producing ADMIRES(Plato, Aristotle).

Lambda abstraction handles traces left by movement operations. In wh-questions like "Who does Plato admire?" syntactic movement displaces "who" from object position, leaving a coindexed trace. The semantic interpretation of the VP "admires t₁" (where t₁ is the trace) employs lambda abstraction over the trace variable: λx.ADMIRES(Plato, x). The wh-operator binds this abstracted function, yielding the question denotation: the set of individuals x such that Plato admires x. This mechanism extends to relative clauses, topicalization, and quantifier raising, unifying diverse syntactic displacement phenomena under a single semantic operation.

Quantificational NPs require type-shifting to compose with predicates. A quantified NP like "every student" does not denote an individual but a generalized quantifier—a function from predicates to truth values, type ⟨⟨e, t⟩, t⟩. The denotation is λP.∀x[STUDENT(x) → P(x)]: given any property P, the expression asserts that every individual who is a student has property P. When "every student" combines with the VP "snores," we apply the generalized quantifier to the VP denotation: ∀x[STUDENT(x) → SNORES(x)]. Existential quantifiers receive analogous treatment: "a student" denotes λP.∃x[STUDENT(x) ∧ P(x)].

Scope ambiguity emerges when multiple quantifiers interact. The sentence "Every professor read a book" generates two logical forms depending on quantifier raising operations:

    Surface scope (∃ > ∀): ∃y[BOOK(y) ∧ ∀x[PROFESSOR(x) → READ(x, y)]]
    Inverse scope (∀ > ∃): ∀x[PROFESSOR(x) → ∃y[BOOK(y) ∧ READ(x, y)]]

The first formula asserts there exists a particular book every professor read; the second asserts each professor read some book, possibly different. Syntactically, quantifier raising adjoins the quantified NP to a clausal node, leaving a trace and creating a lambda abstraction. The relative scope of raised quantifiers determines the interpretation. Constraints on quantifier scope—such as the tendency for surface c-command relations to predict scope preferences—constitute an active research frontier where syntactic structure, prosody, and discourse pragmatics interact.

Intensionality introduces possible-worlds semantics. Modal operators like "necessarily" and "possibly," attitude verbs like "believe" and "want," and tense operators require meanings that vary across possible worlds and times. An intensional verb like "seek" exhibits failure of substitutivity: "John seeks the fountain of youth" does not entail "John seeks the non-existent entity," even if "the fountain of youth" refers to nothing. Intensions are functions from possible worlds (and times) to extensions. A common noun like "president" has an extension at each world-time pair: in the actual world in 2024, the extension includes one individual, but in other worlds or times, the extension differs. Intensional operators manipulate intensions rather than extensions, preventing unwanted inferences.

The model-theoretic interpretation function [[·]] maps expressions to denotations relative to a model M and assignment function g (assigning values to free variables). For a proper name α, [[α]]^{M,g} = the individual in the domain D that α denotes. For a variable x_i, [[x_i]]^{M,g} = g(i). For a predicate P, [[P]]^{M,g} ⊆ D. Complex expressions receive denotations compositionally:

    Function Application: If α is of type ⟨σ, τ⟩ and β is of type σ, then [[[α β]]]^{M,g} = [[α]]^{M,g}([[β]]^{M,g}).
    Lambda Abstraction: If φ is a formula and x a variable, then [[λx.φ]]^{M,g} is the function h such that h(a) = [[φ]]^{M,g[x→a]} for each a ∈ D.
    Truth Conditions: A sentence φ is true in M under g if and only if [[φ]]^{M,g} = 1 (where 1 represents truth).

Presupposition projection demands multi-dimensional semantics. A sentence carries both an assertion and presuppositions. The assertion constitutes the at-issue content subject to truth-conditional evaluation; presuppositions form background conditions that must hold for the assertion to be evaluable. Definite descriptions like "the King of France" presuppose existence and uniqueness: "The King of France is bald" presupposes there exists exactly one King of France and asserts he is bald. In classical bivalent logic, the sentence would be false if the presupposition fails, but trivalent approaches (Kleene, Strawson) assign a third value (undefined or indeterminate) when presuppositions fail. Dynamic semantics models presuppositions as constraints on the context: a sentence is felicitous only if its presuppositions are entailed by the common ground, and successful assertion updates the common ground with the asserted content.

Entailment and inference rules enable automatic deduction. If "John killed Mary" is true, we can infer "Mary died," "Mary is not alive," "Something happened," and "John did something to Mary." These inferences follow from the lexical semantics of "kill" and general rules of logic. Hyponymy relations generate entailments: "x is a sparrow" entails "x is a bird," which entails "x is an animal." Formal semantics captures these patterns through meaning postulates—axioms constraining the interpretation function. For example, a meaning postulate might stipulate ∀x[KILL(e, x, y) → BECOME(e, ¬ALIVE(y))], encoding causative result states.

Event semantics decomposes verb meanings into structured event representations. Davidson proposed that sentences like "John buttered the toast in the kitchen with a knife" contain an implicit existential quantification over events: ∃e[BUTTER(e) ∧ AGENT(e, John) ∧ THEME(e, the-toast) ∧ LOCATION(e, the-kitchen) ∧ INSTRUMENT(e, the-knife)]. Each adverbial modifier predicates over the event variable rather than increasing verb arity. This architecture explains adverbial adjunction freedom and inferential patterns: if "John buttered the toast in the kitchen" is true, then "John buttered the toast" is true, because the latter follows by existential generalization dropping one conjunct.

Aspectual composition involves coercion and telicity calculation. Accomplishments like "draw a circle" have culmination points; activities like "run" do not. When a bare plural or mass noun replaces a quantified incremental theme, the predicate shifts from telic to atelic: "John ate apples" (activity) versus "John ate an apple" (accomplishment). The Vendler-Kenny-Dowty event typology interacts with grammatical aspect: the progressive is felicitous with activities and accomplishments in progress ("John is running," "John is drawing a circle") but resists achievements ("*John is recognizing Mary") and states ("*John is knowing the answer"). Type-shifting operators mediate mismatches: iterative or habitual interpretations coerce achievements into activities ("John is arriving every five minutes" is acceptable).

Noun phrase interpretation mobilizes multiple type-shifting strategies. Bare plurals like "dogs" can function as generics ("Dogs bark"), existentials ("Dogs are in the yard"), or kind-referring terms ("Dogs are mammals"). Predicative nominals in copular sentences ("John is a doctor") denote properties applied to individuals, while equative nominals ("Hesperus is Phosphorus") assert identity. Partee's triangle relates entity type e, predicate type ⟨e, t⟩, and generalized quantifier type ⟨⟨e, t⟩, t⟩, with systematic type-shifting operations (lifting, lowering, ident) connecting them. These shifts explain how a common noun can occupy argument positions ("Water is wet," where "water" shifts from predicate to kind) and why semantic flexibility emerges from rigid type-theoretic constraints.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Efficient semantic processing in computational implementations and psycholinguistic modeling demands strategic optimization of the compositional architecture. Naive bottom-up interpretation generates exponential complexity when scope ambiguity produces multiple logical forms for a single syntactic structure. A sentence with n quantifiers admits up to n! scope permutations, and brute-force enumeration becomes intractable. Incremental interpretation algorithms prune the search space by exploiting locality: quantifiers preferentially scope over their local syntactic domain, and only when semantic or pragmatic factors intervene do they take wider scope. Implementing a default scope assignment heuristic—respecting surface c-command unless coercion is necessary—reduces computational load and aligns with psycholinguistic evidence that surface scope readings incur lower processing cost.

Underspecification formalisms defer scope resolution until necessary. Quasi-logical forms or underspecified discourse representation structures represent scope-ambiguous sentences with a partial order over quantifiers, leaving the total ordering unspecified. Disambiguation occurs only when subsequent discourse or contextual information forces a particular reading. This lazy evaluation strategy mirrors human sentence processing: listeners often tolerate scope ambiguity until downstream material or real-world knowledge eliminates one reading. Computational semantic parsers employing underspecification achieve faster processing times and more graceful handling of ambiguity than systems requiring immediate full disambiguation.

Presupposition accommodation introduces context-dependence requiring dynamic update mechanisms. Static model-theoretic semantics assigns truth values in a fixed model, but presuppositions necessitate tracking the common ground—the set of propositions mutually accepted by discourse participants. Dynamic semantics frameworks (Dynamic Predicate Logic, File Change Semantics, Discourse Representation Theory) model meaning as context-change potential: the semantic value of a sentence is a function updating the context. A sentence with presupposition p uttered in context c succeeds only if c entails p; if not, accommodation may update c to include p before assertion. Efficient implementations maintain a discourse context stack, checking presuppositions against the current state and invoking accommodation rules when mismatches occur. Failure to accommodate yields discourse infelicity: "The King of France is bald" in a context lacking a salient King of France either triggers accommodation (creating a discourse referent) or fails, signaling a presupposition failure.

Type-shifting proliferation poses architectural risks. Unrestricted type-shifting allows any expression to coerce into any type, obliterating predictive power and generating spurious ambiguities. Constrained type-shifting—permitting only principled operations like Partee's triangle shifts or aspectual coercion licensed by event structure mismatches—maintains empirical coverage while preventing overgeneration. Computational grammars implementing semantic composition must explicitly enumerate permitted shifts and specify triggering conditions, balancing flexibility with restrictiveness. Overuse of coercion weakens falsifiability; a theory that explains any pattern by positing a new coercion operator loses explanatory force.

Quantifier scope freezing in certain syntactic configurations reveals processing bottlenecks. Inverse scope interpretations where an embedded quantifier scopes over a higher one generally require overt syntactic movement (Quantifier Raising) crossing a syntactic island boundary. Island constraints—restrictions on extraction from relative clauses, adjuncts, coordinate structures, and subjects—limit scope-taking ability. A quantifier trapped inside a relative clause island cannot scope over a matrix quantifier: "A professor who read every book arrived" strongly prefers the surface scope reading (∀ > ∃) even though semantically the inverse scope (∃ > ∀) is coherent. Semantic parsers must respect syntactic islands, pruning scope configurations that would require illicit movement. Failure to encode island constraints yields overgeneration, predicting scope readings that native speakers consistently reject.

Presupposition projection through embeddings exhibits complex filtering and inheritance patterns. Presuppositions of a complement clause typically project to the matrix clause: "John knows that Mary stopped smoking" presupposes Mary smoked previously. However, certain embeddings block projection. Conditional antecedents filter presuppositions: "If Mary stopped smoking, John will be pleased" does not presuppose Mary smoked, because the conditional already hypothesizes the truth of the antecedent. Semantic theories must specify projection algorithms predicting which presuppositions survive embedding under negation, modals, questions, and attitude verbs. The family-of-sentences test isolates projection behavior: construct a presupposition-carrying sentence, then embed it under various operators and observe which presuppositions persist. Failure to account for filtering contexts produces false predictions of infelicity or conversely fails to detect actual presupposition failures.

Lexical ambiguity and polysemy challenge compositional semantics. A word like "bank" exhibits homonymy—unrelated meanings (financial institution vs. river edge) requiring separate lexical entries. Polysemy, by contrast, involves systematic meaning extensions: "book" denotes a physical object ("The book is heavy") or informational content ("The book is insightful"). Copredication tests reveal polysemy: "The book is heavy and insightful" felicitously combines physical and informational predicates, impossible with homonymy ("*The bank is steep and corrupt" is incoherent). Formal semantics must represent polysemous meanings with shared structure, perhaps via dot types or qualia structure in generative lexicon theory, allowing context to select the appropriate facet. Parsing systems encountering ambiguous lexical items must maintain parallel interpretations or employ probabilistic ranking to prioritize likely readings, trading exhaustive enumeration for efficiency.

Intensional contexts generate opacity and referential indeterminacy. Substituting co-referential terms in extensional contexts preserves truth: if "Hesperus is bright" is true and Hesperus = Phosphorus, then "Phosphorus is bright" is true. Intensional contexts block this substitution: "John believes Hesperus is bright" does not entail "John believes Phosphorus is bright," because belief operates over intensions, not extensions. Semantic composition in intensional contexts requires intensional interpretation: expressions denote functions from possible worlds to extensions, and intensional operators manipulate these functions. Computational implementations must track intensional types and apply intension operators (typically denoted by ^) to arguments of attitude verbs and modals, preventing invalid inferences. Failure to distinguish intension from extension collapses modal and epistemic distinctions, rendering the system unable to model belief, desire, necessity, and possibility.

Quantificational determiners beyond "every" and "some" introduce monotonicity properties affecting entailment patterns. Upward monotone determiners (∀, many, several) license inferences from subsets to supersets: if "Every student passed" is true and students ⊂ people, then "Every person passed" is not entailed, but replacing "student" with a subset preserves truth. Downward monotone determiners (no, few) license inferences from supersets to subsets: "No professor failed" entails "No full professor failed" (since full professors ⊂ professors). Non-monotone determiners (exactly three, most) fail both directions. These monotonicity properties govern the licensing of negative polarity items ("any," "ever") and the distribution of scalar implicatures. Semantic parsers encoding determiners as generalized quantifiers must compute monotonicity profiles to predict inferential behavior and grammaticality patterns. Ignoring monotonicity yields incorrect predictions about NPI licensing and entailment.

Tense and temporal reference require interval semantics and reference-time manipulation. Reichenbachian tense logic distinguishes speech time (S), event time (E), and reference time (R). The simple past locates E before S with R coinciding with E; the past perfect locates E before R, which is before S. Temporal adverbials shift reference time: "John left at 3pm" anchors R to 3pm, asserting E occurs at R. Aspect further refines temporal structure: the perfective views an event as a complete whole, while the imperfective views internal structure. The English progressive imposes an imperfective viewpoint, requiring the event time to overlap rather than coincide with reference time. Formal implementations employ interval algebra and precedence relations, calculating entailment and consistency among temporally specified clauses. Misaligning tense, aspect, and adverbial anchors produces temporal contradictions detectable through automated consistency checking.

Event structure decomposition extends to causation, agency, and thematic role hierarchies. Proto-role theories (Dowty) derive thematic roles from entailments: a proto-agent is characterized by volition, causation, and sentience; a proto-patient by undergoing change, being causally affected, and stativity. Linking rules map proto-roles onto grammatical functions: the argument with the most proto-agent properties becomes the subject; the argument with the most proto-patient properties becomes the object. This probabilistic approach handles intermediate cases where clear-cut thematic labels fail, such as experiencer subjects ("John likes cake") combining agent-like subjecthood with patient-like semantic properties. Parsers implementing proto-role linking must compute entailment profiles for each argument and rank them, predicting grammatical realization without stipulating rigid thematic role inventories.

Failure modes in compositional semantics often stem from mismatches between syntactic structure and intended interpretation. Garden-path sentences temporarily mislead the parser: "The horse raced past the barn fell" initially parses "raced" as the main verb, forcing costly reanalysis when "fell" appears. Semantic anomaly detection identifies type mismatches and selectional restriction violations: "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" is syntactically well-formed but semantically incoherent due to contradictory modification and nonsensical predication. Robust parsers employ expectation-driven processing, using lexical and contextual cues to anticipate upcoming structures, and maintain ranked interpretations to recover from misanalyses. Probabilistic semantic models assign graded acceptability rather than binary judgments, accommodating the continuum between perfect coherence and outright anomaly.

Scalability challenges arise when extending compositional semantics to discourse and pragmatics. Anaphora resolution, ellipsis, and conversational implicature require global context beyond sentence-level semantics. Pronouns like "he" demand antecedent retrieval from discourse representation structures, obeying syntactic constraints (binding theory) and pragmatic salience hierarchies. Ellipsis reconstructs elided material through parallelism: "John left, and Mary did too" recovers "left" for the elided VP. Scalar implicatures ("Some students passed") generate the inference that not all students passed, arising from Gricean maxims of quantity rather than truth-conditional semantics. Bridging compositional sentence semantics with discourse pragmatics requires hybrid architectures where sentence interpretation feeds into discourse models, and pragmatic reasoning post-processes truth-conditional content to derive speaker meaning. Clean separation of semantic and pragmatic modules maintains theoretical clarity while enabling empirical coverage of language in use.
