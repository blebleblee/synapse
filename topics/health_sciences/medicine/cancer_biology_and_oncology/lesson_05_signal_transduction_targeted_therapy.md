# Lesson 5: Signal Transduction Pathways in Cancer

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Cancer's hallmark capabilities—sustained proliferation, evasion of growth suppressors, resistance to cell death, replicative immortality, angiogenesis induction, and metastatic invasion—emerge not from random chaos but from systematic hijacking of ancient, highly conserved signal transduction pathways that normally govern cellular homeostasis, differentiation, and survival. These pathways, refined across hundreds of millions of years of eukaryotic evolution, translate extracellular signals such as growth factors, morphogens, and mechanical cues into precise intracellular responses through cascades of protein phosphorylation, conformational changes, and transcriptional programs. When genetic or epigenetic alterations corrupt these signaling networks, the cell loses its capacity to read its positional identity within tissue architecture, ignore growth factor withdrawal, or execute programmed death in response to DNA damage. The result is a cell that behaves as though it exists in a perpetual state of embryonic expansion, with growth signals locked in the "on" position and death signals permanently disabled.

The fundamental invariant governing all oncogenic signal transduction is the principle of gain-of-function in pro-growth pathways and loss-of-function in growth-suppressive or pro-apoptotic pathways. Unlike germline genetic diseases where loss of a single housekeeping enzyme might cripple metabolism, cancer-associated mutations tend to cluster in regulatory nodes that control signal amplification, pathway crosstalk, and negative feedback loops. A single point mutation in RAS, for instance, does not destroy the protein's GTPase domain entirely but rather impairs its intrinsic GTP hydrolysis rate just enough to shift the equilibrium from predominantly GDP-bound (inactive) to GTP-bound (active), creating a constitutively firing signal that floods downstream effector cascades. Similarly, deletions or inactivating mutations in tumor suppressors such as PTEN do not eliminate phosphatidylinositol metabolism but selectively remove the brake on PI3K signaling, permitting unopposed accumulation of PIP₃ at the plasma membrane and sustained recruitment of AKT.

This lesson dissects five master signaling pathways whose dysregulation accounts for the majority of human cancers: the receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) to RAS to mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade, the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) to AKT to mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) axis, the Wnt to β-catenin pathway controlling stem cell self-renewal, the Notch pathway governing binary cell fate decisions, and the Hedgehog pathway that patterns tissue boundaries during development. For each pathway, we establish the normal physiological signal flow, identify the specific molecular lesions that convert these circuits into oncogenic drivers, examine the rational drug targeting strategies that exploit pathway-specific vulnerabilities, and confront the resistance mechanisms that routinely emerge under therapeutic pressure. The clinical imperative is to understand not merely that a pathway is activated, but precisely which nodes are mutated, which feedback loops are intact, and which escape routes the tumor can access when the primary target is inhibited.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The RTK/RAS/MAPK pathway exemplifies the canonical three-tiered kinase cascade architecture. At the plasma membrane, receptor tyrosine kinases such as EGFR, HER2, PDGFR, and VEGFR exist as monomeric inactive units until ligand binding induces dimerization and reciprocal trans-autophosphorylation of cytoplasmic tyrosine residues. These phosphotyrosines serve as docking sites for adaptor proteins bearing SH2 or PTB domains, most critically GRB2, which recruits the guanine nucleotide exchange factor SOS to the membrane. SOS catalyzes the exchange of GDP for GTP on RAS proteins (HRAS, KRAS, NRAS), converting them from their inactive to active conformational state. GTP-loaded RAS then binds and activates RAF kinases (ARAF, BRAF, CRAF), which phosphorylate and activate MEK1/2, which in turn phosphorylate and activate ERK1/2. Active ERK translocates to the nucleus where it phosphorylates transcription factors such as ELK1, MYC, and FOS, driving expression of genes promoting cell cycle entry, survival, and motility.

Oncogenic corruption of this pathway occurs at multiple nodes. EGFR amplification and activating mutations (particularly the exon 19 deletion and L858R point mutation) are endemic in non-small cell lung cancer, while HER2 amplification defines approximately twenty percent of breast cancers. RAS mutations, particularly KRAS G12C, G12D, and G12V, are among the most frequent oncogenic drivers across all human cancers, appearing in over ninety percent of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas, forty percent of colorectal cancers, and thirty percent of lung adenocarcinomas. The BRAF V600E mutation, which constitutively activates the kinase domain independent of RAS, defines the majority of melanomas and a subset of colorectal cancers. Each of these lesions locks the pathway in a state of tonic activation, rendering the cell independent of extracellular growth factor availability.

The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway operates in parallel to the MAPK cascade, receiving input from the same RTK activation events but channeling signals toward metabolic reprogramming, protein synthesis, and survival rather than proliferation alone. Upon RTK activation, the catalytic subunit of class IA PI3K (p110α, p110β, or p110δ) is recruited to the membrane where it phosphorylates phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP₂) to generate phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate (PIP₃). PIP₃ recruits AKT (also known as protein kinase B) and its activating kinase PDK1 to the membrane via their pleckstrin homology domains. PDK1 phosphorylates AKT at threonine 308, and full activation requires a second phosphorylation at serine 473 by mTORC2. Activated AKT phosphorylates over one hundred substrates, including MDM2 (inhibiting p53), BAD (blocking apoptosis), FOXO transcription factors (preventing their nuclear entry and pro-apoptotic gene expression), and TSC2. Phosphorylation of TSC2 inactivates the TSC1/TSC2 GAP complex, which normally suppresses the small GTPase RHEB. When RHEB is GTP-loaded, it directly activates mTORC1, a master regulator of anabolic metabolism that phosphorylates S6K and 4E-BP1 to promote ribosomal biogenesis and cap-dependent translation.

Oncogenic activation of this pathway most commonly occurs through PIK3CA mutations encoding the p110α catalytic subunit (particularly the helical domain mutation E545K and the kinase domain mutation H1047R), loss-of-function mutations or deletions of the lipid phosphatase PTEN (which normally dephosphorylates PIP₃ back to PIP₂), and activating mutations in AKT1 (E17K). These lesions collectively drive the pathway toward sustained mTORC1 activity, creating a cell that is hypersensitive to nutrient availability, resistant to metabolic stress, and primed for rapid growth.

The Wnt/β-catenin pathway governs stem cell maintenance and tissue polarity, and its dysregulation is nearly universal in colorectal cancer. In the absence of Wnt ligands, cytoplasmic β-catenin is captured by a destruction complex consisting of the scaffold protein AXIN, the tumor suppressor APC, and the kinases CK1α and GSK3β. CK1α phosphorylates β-catenin at serine 45, priming subsequent phosphorylation by GSK3β at serines 33, 37, and threonine 41. These phosphorylations create a recognition motif for the E3 ubiquitin ligase β-TrCP, which polyubiquitinates β-catenin and targets it for proteasomal degradation. When Wnt ligands bind to Frizzled receptors and LRP5/6 co-receptors, Dishevelled is recruited and inhibits the destruction complex, allowing β-catenin to accumulate in the cytoplasm, translocate to the nucleus, and bind TCF/LEF transcription factors to activate target genes such as MYC, CCND1, and AXIN2. Loss-of-function mutations in APC (occurring in over eighty percent of colorectal cancers) or gain-of-function mutations in β-catenin itself (particularly deletions or point mutations affecting the GSK3β phosphorylation sites) stabilize β-catenin constitutively, driving continuous transcription of proliferative genes even in the absence of Wnt ligands.

The Notch pathway mediates binary cell fate decisions through direct cell-cell contact. Notch receptors (Notch1-4 in mammals) are single-pass transmembrane proteins that, upon binding to ligands such as Delta or Jagged on adjacent cells, undergo two sequential proteolytic cleavages. The first cleavage by ADAM metalloproteases releases the extracellular domain, and the second cleavage by the γ-secretase complex liberates the Notch intracellular domain (NICD). NICD translocates to the nucleus where it binds the transcription factor CSL (also called RBP-Jκ), displacing co-repressor complexes and recruiting co-activators such as Mastermind-like proteins to activate target genes including HES and HEY family transcriptional repressors. Oncogenic Notch activation occurs through chromosomal translocations (particularly in T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia), activating mutations in the extracellular heterodimerization domain, or loss-of-function mutations in negative regulators such as FBXW7, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that normally promotes NICD degradation.

The Hedgehog pathway patterns tissue boundaries during embryonic development and maintains stem cell niches in adult tissues. In the absence of Hedgehog ligands, the receptor Patched (PTCH1) inhibits the seven-pass transmembrane protein Smoothened (SMO), preventing it from transducing signals. The transcription factors GLI2 and GLI3 are processed into repressor forms through phosphorylation by PKA, GSK3β, and CK1, followed by partial proteasomal degradation. When Hedgehog ligands (Sonic, Indian, or Desert) bind to PTCH1, the inhibition of SMO is released, allowing SMO to accumulate in the primary cilium and activate a signaling cascade that prevents GLI processing and promotes full-length GLI2 and GLI3 activation. These transcription factors then activate target genes including PTCH1, GLI1, and cyclin D1. Oncogenic activation of this pathway is particularly prominent in basal cell carcinoma and medulloblastoma, driven by loss-of-function mutations in PTCH1 or gain-of-function mutations in SMO.

Rational drug targeting strategies exploit specific vulnerabilities within each pathway. EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as erlotinib, gefitinib, and osimertinib bind competitively to the ATP-binding pocket of the kinase domain, selectively inhibiting EGFR-mutant lung cancers. Monoclonal antibodies such as trastuzumab (targeting HER2) and cetuximab (targeting EGFR) block ligand binding and induce receptor internalization. Direct RAS inhibitors remained elusive for decades due to the lack of deep binding pockets on RAS proteins, but the covalent KRAS G12C inhibitor sotorasib represents a breakthrough, binding irreversibly to the cysteine residue in the mutant switch II pocket and locking KRAS in its inactive GDP-bound state. BRAF V600E inhibitors such as vemurafenib and dabrafenib demonstrate dramatic response rates in melanoma but paradoxically activate MAPK signaling in KRAS-mutant cells through RAF dimerization, necessitating combination with MEK inhibitors such as trametinib.

PI3K pathway inhibitors include isoform-selective PI3K inhibitors (alpelisib targeting p110α), pan-AKT inhibitors (capivasertib), and mTOR inhibitors. First-generation mTOR inhibitors such as everolimus and temsirolimus are rapalogs that bind FKBP12 and inhibit mTORC1 but not mTORC2, and their efficacy is limited by feedback loops that reactivate AKT. Second-generation ATP-competitive mTOR kinase inhibitors and dual PI3K/mTOR inhibitors aim to suppress both complexes but carry significant metabolic toxicity. Wnt pathway inhibitors targeting the porcupine O-acyltransferase that palmitoylates Wnt ligands (such as ETC-159) or tankyrase enzymes that stabilize AXIN are in clinical development. Notch pathway inhibition via γ-secretase inhibitors showed initial promise but induces severe gastrointestinal toxicity due to on-target inhibition of Notch in intestinal stem cells. Hedgehog pathway inhibitors such as vismodegib and sonidegib target SMO and achieve high response rates in basal cell carcinoma but encounter resistance through SMO mutations or pathway-independent GLI2 activation.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

The clinical optimization of pathway-targeted therapies requires integration of genomic profiling, pharmacodynamic biomarker monitoring, and adaptive resistance surveillance. Initial patient stratification relies on next-generation sequencing panels or whole-exome sequencing to identify actionable driver mutations, copy number alterations, and gene fusions. For EGFR-mutant lung cancer, detection of the T790M gating mutation via liquid biopsy using circulating tumor DNA guides the switch from first- to third-generation inhibitors. For KRAS-mutant colorectal cancer, co-mutation profiling reveals that STK11 or KEAP1 loss predicts resistance to immune checkpoint blockade, while concurrent PIK3CA mutations suggest potential benefit from PI3K inhibitor combinations.

Pharmacodynamic monitoring tracks on-target pathway suppression and off-target toxicity through serial biopsies or surrogate biomarkers. Skin biopsies following EGFR inhibitor initiation demonstrate suppression of phospho-ERK and phospho-AKT within the first cycle, while rising serum insulin and glucose levels signal metabolic derangements from PI3K inhibition. Serial circulating tumor DNA monitoring detects emergent resistance mutations months before radiographic progression, enabling preemptive treatment modification. For BRAF-mutant melanoma, detection of NRAS mutations or MEK mutations in liquid biopsy at progression prompts escalation to MEK inhibitor combinations or switch to immune checkpoint blockade.

Resistance mechanisms fall into three major categories: on-target alterations, bypass pathway activation, and phenotypic plasticity. On-target resistance includes secondary kinase domain mutations that sterically hinder inhibitor binding (EGFR T790M, C797S), gene amplification that overwhelms inhibitor concentration (MET amplification in EGFR-mutant lung cancer), and alternative splicing that deletes the inhibitor-binding domain. Bypass pathway activation involves upregulation of parallel RTKs (HER3, MET, AXL), activation of downstream effectors independent of the inhibited node (KRAS mutations emerging under EGFR inhibition), or loss of negative regulators (PTEN deletion under PI3K inhibition). Phenotypic plasticity encompasses epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), neuroendocrine differentiation in prostate and lung cancers, and cancer stem cell enrichment, all of which reduce dependency on the originally inhibited pathway.

Rational combination strategies aim to forestall resistance by simultaneously targeting the primary driver and predicted escape routes. BRAF plus MEK inhibition in melanoma prevents paradoxical MAPK activation and prolongs progression-free survival compared to BRAF inhibition alone. PI3K plus CDK4/6 inhibition exploits synthetic lethality in hormone receptor-positive breast cancer by blocking both mitogenic signaling and cell cycle progression. EGFR inhibition plus MET inhibition or EGFR inhibition plus chemotherapy delays emergence of bypass resistance in lung cancer. However, combination regimens amplify toxicity, requiring careful dose optimization and rigorous patient selection based on molecular predictors of benefit.

Emerging strategies exploit synthetic lethality relationships and metabolic vulnerabilities. KRAS-mutant cancers exhibit heightened dependency on autophagy, glutamine metabolism, and reactive oxygen species scavenging pathways, making them vulnerable to autophagy inhibitors, glutaminase inhibitors, and ferroptosis inducers. Loss of tumor suppressors such as PTEN or LKB1 creates selective vulnerabilities to mTORC1/2 inhibition or metabolic stress. Cancers with activated Wnt signaling demonstrate exceptional sensitivity to disruption of one-carbon metabolism and nucleotide synthesis due to their high proliferative burden. Integration of functional genomics screens with patient-derived organoid models accelerates identification of context-specific vulnerabilities tailored to individual tumor genotypes.

Long-term disease control increasingly relies on adaptive therapy paradigms that modulate selection pressure rather than pursuing maximum cytotoxic effect. Intermittent dosing schedules that permit partial tumor regrowth during drug holidays maintain a population of drug-sensitive cells that competitively suppress resistant clones. Dose titration guided by circulating tumor DNA levels sustains pathway inhibition at the minimum effective dose, reducing toxicity and delaying resistance. Sequential monotherapy switching at predefined thresholds prevents fixation of any single resistance mechanism. These strategies, informed by evolutionary game theory and mathematical modeling of clonal dynamics, represent a fundamental shift from the doctrine of maximum tolerated dose toward ecologically informed cancer control.
