# Lesson 1: Foundations of Classical Linear Regression

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The classical linear regression model stands as the foundational architecture of empirical policy analysis, embodying a profound epistemological claim: that we can decompose observed economic phenomena into systematic patterns and irreducible randomness. When a central bank economist estimates the transmission mechanism from policy interest rates to inflation, when a fiscal analyst quantifies the employment multiplier of infrastructure spending, or when a development economist measures the returns to education investment, they invoke this framework—often implicitly—as the inferential engine underlying causal claims about policy interventions.

At its conceptual core, the linear regression model posits that an outcome variable of interest, which we denote $y_i$ for observation $i$, can be expressed as a linear function of observable explanatory variables $x_{i1}, x_{i2}, \ldots, x_{ik}$, plus an unobserved disturbance term $\epsilon_i$ that captures all other influences. The structural equation takes the canonical form:

$$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \cdots + \beta_k x_{ik} + \epsilon_i$$

The parameters $\beta_0, \beta_1, \ldots, \beta_k$ constitute the objects of inferential interest. They encode the marginal effect of each explanatory variable on the outcome, holding all other variables constant—the ceteris paribus interpretation that makes regression analysis the workhorse of policy evaluation. The intercept $\beta_0$ captures the baseline value of $y$ when all regressors equal zero, though its economic interpretation often lacks substantive meaning when regressors are not naturally centered.

The disturbance term $\epsilon_i$ absorbs measurement error, omitted variables, aggregation effects, and fundamental randomness in individual behavior. Its properties determine whether our estimators possess desirable statistical characteristics. The classical Gauss-Markov theorem establishes that under five core assumptions, the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimator—which minimizes the sum of squared residuals—yields the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE) of the parameter vector. This optimality result provides the axiomatic foundation for regression analysis in policy research.

The first Gauss-Markov assumption demands linearity in parameters: the data-generating process must conform to the linear specification above. This does not prohibit nonlinear relationships in variables—we can include polynomial terms, logarithmic transformations, or interaction effects—but the equation must be linear in the coefficients $\beta$. The second assumption requires that observations are drawn from a random sample, such that $(y_i, x_{i1}, \ldots, x_{ik})$ are independent and identically distributed across $i$. This rules out time-series autocorrelation and cross-sectional clustering, violations that plague macroeconomic policy analysis when observations exhibit temporal or spatial dependence.

The third assumption, strict exogeneity, imposes the orthogonality condition $E[\epsilon_i | x_{i1}, \ldots, x_{ik}] = 0$ for all observations. The error term must have conditional mean zero given the regressors, implying that no systematic relationship exists between explanatory variables and the disturbance. This assumption encodes the causal interpretation of regression coefficients: if education attainment $x$ is endogenous to unobserved ability $\epsilon$, then $\beta$ conflates the true causal effect with selection bias. Violations of strict exogeneity constitute the central identification challenge in policy evaluation, motivating instrumental variables, difference-in-differences, and regression discontinuity designs as alternative estimators.

The fourth assumption states that no perfect collinearity exists among regressors. The design matrix $X$, formed by stacking observation vectors $[1, x_{i1}, \ldots, x_{ik}]$ row-wise, must have full column rank. Perfect collinearity—when one regressor is an exact linear combination of others—renders the OLS estimator undefined, as the normal equations $X'X\beta = X'y$ cannot be inverted. Near-collinearity, while not technically violating the assumption, inflates standard errors and destabilizes coefficient estimates, a practical concern when including multiple proxies for the same underlying construct.

The fifth assumption, homoskedasticity, requires constant variance in the error term: $\text{Var}(\epsilon_i | x_{i1}, \ldots, x_{ik}) = \sigma^2$ for all $i$. Heteroskedasticity—when error variance depends on regressor values—arises frequently in cross-sectional policy data. High-income households exhibit greater consumption volatility than low-income households; large firms show more variable employment responses to demand shocks than small firms. While heteroskedasticity does not bias OLS coefficient estimates, it invalidates conventional standard error formulas, requiring robust variance estimators like the Eicker-Huber-White sandwich estimator to restore valid inference.

These five assumptions collectively define the classical linear regression model and deliver the Gauss-Markov theorem's optimality result. The theorem states that among all linear unbiased estimators—those that can be written as linear functions of $y$ and have expected value equal to the true parameter—OLS achieves minimum variance. No other linear unbiased estimator produces tighter confidence intervals or more powerful hypothesis tests. This efficiency property makes OLS the default estimator for policy analysis, though violations of Gauss-Markov assumptions motivate alternative estimators that sacrifice unbiasedness or linearity to achieve lower mean squared error in finite samples.

The econometric project for policy analysts consists of estimating the unknown parameter vector $\beta$ from observed data $(y_i, x_{i1}, \ldots, x_{ik})$ for $i = 1, \ldots, n$, conducting inference about parameter values, and evaluating model adequacy. The OLS estimator constructs fitted values $\hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_{i1} + \cdots + \hat{\beta}_k x_{ik}$ that approximate observed outcomes, with residuals $\hat{\epsilon}_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$ capturing unexplained variation. The sum of squared residuals, $\text{SSR} = \sum_{i=1}^n \hat{\epsilon}_i^2$, quantifies model fit, while the coefficient of determination $R^2 = 1 - \text{SSR}/\text{SST}$ measures the fraction of outcome variance explained by regressors, where $\text{SST} = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2$ denotes total sum of squares.

Sampling theory provides the probabilistic framework for inference. Since the OLS estimator $\hat{\beta}$ depends on the random sample $(y_i, x_i)$, it constitutes a random variable with its own distribution. Under the Gauss-Markov assumptions, $\hat{\beta}$ is unbiased, meaning $E[\hat{\beta}] = \beta$, and possesses a variance-covariance matrix $\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}) = \sigma^2 (X'X)^{-1}$. The diagonal elements of this matrix, scaled by an estimate of $\sigma^2$, yield the standard errors reported in regression output. These standard errors quantify the uncertainty in coefficient estimates arising from sampling variation—the fact that a different random sample would produce different point estimates.

The addition of a normality assumption on the error distribution, $\epsilon_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$ independently, enables exact finite-sample inference via $t$-statistics and $F$-statistics. The $t$-statistic for testing the null hypothesis $H_0: \beta_j = 0$ takes the form:

$$t_j = \frac{\hat{\beta}_j}{\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j)}$$

where $\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j)$ denotes the estimated standard error of $\hat{\beta}_j$. Under the null hypothesis, $t_j$ follows a $t$-distribution with $n - k - 1$ degrees of freedom, allowing construction of confidence intervals and $p$-values. The $F$-statistic tests joint hypotheses about multiple coefficients simultaneously, comparing the fit of restricted and unrestricted models. These hypothesis testing frameworks underpin policy evaluation: testing whether a fiscal stimulus affects GDP growth, whether monetary tightening reduces inflation, or whether a regulatory intervention alters firm behavior.

Without the normality assumption, asymptotic theory rescues inference in large samples. As the sample size $n$ grows, the central limit theorem ensures that the OLS estimator converges in distribution to a normal random variable, even when errors are non-normal. This asymptotic normality justifies using standard $t$-tests and $F$-tests as large-sample approximations, though finite-sample performance may degrade with severe non-normality, particularly in the tails of the distribution. Robust inference methods, including bootstrapped standard errors and randomization inference, provide alternatives when neither exact nor asymptotic normality holds.

The regression framework extends naturally to accommodate richer specifications. Multiple regression models include numerous regressors simultaneously, controlling for confounding variables that might otherwise bias estimates of the treatment effect of interest. Fixed effects models absorb time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity by including indicator variables for individuals, firms, or countries, exploiting within-unit variation to identify causal effects. Instrumental variables estimation addresses endogeneity by using excluded exogenous variation to isolate the causal component of regressor variation. These extensions inherit the foundational logic of the classical linear regression model while relaxing specific Gauss-Markov assumptions to handle identification challenges endemic to policy research.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The ordinary least squares estimator emerges from a pure optimization principle: choose parameter values that minimize the sum of squared vertical distances between observed outcomes and fitted values. Let the residual sum of squares function be defined as:

$$S(\beta_0, \beta_1, \ldots, \beta_k) = \sum_{i=1}^n \epsilon_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_{i1} - \cdots - \beta_k x_{ik})^2$$

The OLS estimator $\hat{\beta}$ solves the minimization problem $\min_{\beta} S(\beta)$. Taking the first-order conditions with respect to each parameter and setting them equal to zero yields the system of normal equations. In matrix notation, let $y$ denote the $n \times 1$ vector of outcomes, $X$ the $n \times (k+1)$ design matrix whose first column consists entirely of ones (for the intercept) and subsequent columns contain the regressors, and $\beta$ the $(k+1) \times 1$ parameter vector. The residual sum of squares can be written compactly as:

$$S(\beta) = (y - X\beta)'(y - X\beta) = y'y - 2\beta'X'y + \beta'X'X\beta$$

Differentiating with respect to $\beta$ and setting the derivative equal to zero produces the normal equations:

$$X'X\beta = X'y$$

Under the no-perfect-collinearity assumption, $X'X$ is invertible, yielding the closed-form OLS estimator:

$$\hat{\beta} = (X'X)^{-1}X'y$$

This formula encodes the computational architecture of regression estimation. The matrix $X'X$, known as the Gram matrix or moment matrix, accumulates cross-products of regressors across observations. Its diagonal elements equal the sum of squares of each regressor (after centering, if the intercept is included), while off-diagonal elements capture covariances between regressors. The vector $X'y$ accumulates cross-products between each regressor and the outcome. The inversion $(X'X)^{-1}$ transforms these raw moment conditions into coefficient estimates, weighting regressors by their variance and correlations.

To develop intuition, consider the bivariate regression case with a single regressor $x_i$ and an intercept. The OLS slope estimator takes the explicit form:

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{\text{Cov}(x, y)}{\text{Var}(x)}$$

This formula reveals that the OLS slope equals the sample covariance between $x$ and $y$ divided by the sample variance of $x$. The estimator decomposes the outcome into a component predicted by the regressor and an orthogonal residual component. The intercept estimator satisfies $\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}$, ensuring that the fitted regression line passes through the point of means $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$. This property guarantees that residuals sum to zero, $\sum_i \hat{\epsilon}_i = 0$, and that residuals are orthogonal to regressors, $\sum_i x_{ij} \hat{\epsilon}_i = 0$ for all $j$.

The fitted values $\hat{y} = X\hat{\beta} = X(X'X)^{-1}X'y$ define the orthogonal projection of $y$ onto the column space of $X$. The projection matrix $P = X(X'X)^{-1}X'$ satisfies $P'P = P$, the defining property of an orthogonal projector. Geometrically, OLS decomposes the outcome vector into a component $\hat{y}$ lying in the span of the regressors and a residual component $\hat{\epsilon} = y - \hat{y} = (I - P)y$ orthogonal to that span. This geometric interpretation clarifies that OLS performs optimally only when the true data-generating process lies within the linear span of included regressors—a restrictive assumption when functional forms are unknown.

The variance-covariance matrix of the OLS estimator, conditional on $X$, takes the form:

$$\text{Var}(\hat{\beta} | X) = \sigma^2 (X'X)^{-1}$$

where $\sigma^2 = E[\epsilon_i^2]$ denotes the error variance. This formula reveals that coefficient variance depends inversely on the variability of regressors and the sample size. Regressors with low variance—those exhibiting little variation across observations—yield imprecise estimates. Collinearity among regressors inflates $(X'X)^{-1}$, magnifying standard errors. These mechanical relationships explain why policy analysts prioritize rich variation in treatment assignment and seek exogenous shifters that move regressors independently.

Since $\sigma^2$ is unknown, we replace it with the consistent estimator:

$$\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \hat{\epsilon}_i^2}{n - k - 1} = \frac{\text{SSR}}{n - k - 1}$$

The denominator $n - k - 1$ adjusts for degrees of freedom consumed by estimating $k + 1$ parameters. The estimated variance-covariance matrix $\hat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\beta}) = \hat{\sigma}^2 (X'X)^{-1}$ produces the standard errors reported in regression tables. Under homoskedasticity, these conventional standard errors are consistent and asymptotically efficient. Under heteroskedasticity, they are inconsistent, motivating the heteroskedasticity-robust variance estimator:

$$\hat{\text{Var}}_{\text{HC}}(\hat{\beta}) = (X'X)^{-1} \left( \sum_{i=1}^n \hat{\epsilon}_i^2 x_i x_i' \right) (X'X)^{-1}$$

This sandwich estimator, proposed independently by Eicker, Huber, and White, replaces the homoskedastic variance assumption with observation-specific residual-based variance estimates. It remains consistent under arbitrary heteroskedasticity, though it may exhibit downward bias in small samples, prompting finite-sample corrections like HC2 and HC3 adjustments.

The hypothesis testing framework for policy analysis relies on the sampling distribution of $\hat{\beta}$. To test the null hypothesis $H_0: \beta_j = \beta_j^0$ against the two-sided alternative $H_1: \beta_j \neq \beta_j^0$, we compute the $t$-statistic:

$$t_j = \frac{\hat{\beta}_j - \beta_j^0}{\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j)}$$

Under the null hypothesis and the normality assumption, $t_j$ follows a $t$-distribution with $n - k - 1$ degrees of freedom. We reject $H_0$ at significance level $\alpha$ if $|t_j| > t_{\alpha/2, n-k-1}$, the critical value from the $t$-distribution. The corresponding $p$-value, $p = 2 \cdot P(T > |t_j|)$ where $T \sim t_{n-k-1}$, quantifies the probability of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as $t_j$ under the null hypothesis. Conventional practice deems effects statistically significant when $p < 0.05$, though this threshold is arbitrary and context-dependent.

For testing joint hypotheses involving multiple coefficients, the $F$-statistic compares the fit of a restricted model imposing the null hypothesis constraints to an unrestricted model. Consider testing $H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \cdots = \beta_k = 0$, the null that all slope coefficients equal zero simultaneously. The $F$-statistic takes the form:

$$F = \frac{(\text{SSR}_{\text{restricted}} - \text{SSR}_{\text{unrestricted}})/q}{\text{SSR}_{\text{unrestricted}}/(n - k - 1)}$$

where $q$ denotes the number of restrictions imposed. Under the null hypothesis and normality, $F$ follows an $F$-distribution with $q$ and $n - k - 1$ degrees of freedom. The $F$-test dominates individual $t$-tests when evaluating joint significance, as it controls the overall Type I error rate and accounts for correlations among coefficient estimates.

Confidence intervals provide an alternative representation of sampling uncertainty. A $(1 - \alpha)$ confidence interval for $\beta_j$ is constructed as:

$$\hat{\beta}_j \pm t_{\alpha/2, n-k-1} \cdot \text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j)$$

This interval contains the true parameter value in $(1 - \alpha)$ of repeated samples, assuming the model is correctly specified and Gauss-Markov assumptions hold. Policy analysts often report 95% confidence intervals, corresponding to $\alpha = 0.05$, to communicate the precision of effect estimates and the range of economically plausible values.

The computational workflow for estimating a linear regression model in policy research follows a systematic sequence. First, load the dataset and inspect variable distributions, checking for outliers, missing values, and coding errors. Second, specify the regression equation, selecting outcome and explanatory variables based on theoretical priors and identification assumptions. Third, estimate the model via OLS, extracting coefficient estimates, standard errors, $t$-statistics, $p$-values, $R^2$, and the residual standard error. Fourth, conduct diagnostic checks: plot residuals against fitted values to detect heteroskedasticity and nonlinearity; compute variance inflation factors (VIFs) to diagnose collinearity; test for normality using quantile-quantile plots or formal tests like Jarque-Bera. Fifth, report results in a regression table, including coefficient estimates, robust standard errors in parentheses, significance stars, sample size, and $R^2$.

Statistical software packages implement OLS estimation via numerical linear algebra routines optimized for computational efficiency. The QR decomposition factorizes the design matrix $X = QR$, where $Q$ is orthogonal and $R$ is upper triangular, transforming the normal equations into $R\beta = Q'y$, which is solved by back-substitution. This approach is numerically stable and avoids explicitly forming $X'X$, which can exacerbate rounding errors when regressors exhibit high collinearity. Sparse matrix techniques exploit structure when the design matrix contains many zeros, reducing memory consumption and accelerating computation in high-dimensional settings.

The regression model's predictive performance is evaluated using in-sample and out-of-sample metrics. The in-sample $R^2$ measures the proportion of variance explained within the estimation sample, but it mechanically increases with the number of regressors, even when additional variables lack predictive power. The adjusted $R^2$, defined as $\bar{R}^2 = 1 - (1 - R^2)(n - 1)/(n - k - 1)$, penalizes model complexity, declining when added regressors fail to improve fit sufficiently. Out-of-sample validation, where a held-out test set evaluates predictions from a model trained on a separate training set, provides a more rigorous assessment of generalization performance, guarding against overfitting.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

The practical execution of regression analysis for policy evaluation demands attention to computational efficiency, numerical stability, and robustness to specification errors. Large-scale policy datasets—longitudinal microdata tracking millions of individuals, high-frequency macroeconomic time series, or granular administrative records—challenge standard estimation algorithms, requiring optimized implementations and careful algorithmic choices to achieve tractable runtimes.

When the number of observations $n$ grows large but the number of regressors $k$ remains modest, the computational bottleneck lies in forming the $k \times k$ Gram matrix $X'X$ and the $k \times 1$ vector $X'y$. This operation scales as $O(nk^2)$, dominated by the cost of computing cross-products. Streaming algorithms reduce memory overhead by accumulating $X'X$ and $X'y$ incrementally, processing one observation at a time rather than loading the entire dataset into memory. This approach enables estimation on datasets exceeding available RAM, a common constraint when analyzing census microdata or satellite-derived economic indicators.

For panel data with fixed effects—regression models including indicator variables for each individual, firm, or geographic unit—the design matrix dimension explodes, rendering direct inversion of $X'X$ computationally infeasible. The within transformation exploits the block structure of fixed effects, demeaning variables within each group to eliminate fixed effects algebraically before applying OLS to transformed data. Let $\tilde{y}_{it} = y_{it} - \bar{y}_i$ and $\tilde{x}_{it} = x_{it} - \bar{x}_i$ denote demeaned variables, where $\bar{y}_i$ is the mean of $y_{it}$ over time periods $t$ for individual $i$. Regressing $\tilde{y}_{it}$ on $\tilde{x}_{it}$ yields coefficient estimates identical to the full fixed effects specification, but avoids inverting a matrix of dimension equal to the number of individuals. This transformation reduces computational complexity from $O(n^3)$ to $O(nT)$, where $T$ denotes the number of time periods, enabling estimation on panels with millions of units.

Collinearity diagnostics detect near-linear dependencies among regressors that inflate standard errors and destabilize coefficient estimates. The variance inflation factor (VIF) for regressor $j$, defined as $\text{VIF}_j = 1/(1 - R_j^2)$, where $R_j^2$ is the $R^2$ from regressing $x_j$ on all other regressors, quantifies how much the variance of $\hat{\beta}_j$ is inflated due to collinearity. A VIF exceeding 10 signals problematic collinearity, prompting remedial action: dropping redundant regressors, combining correlated variables into indices, or collecting additional data to increase regressor variability. Ridge regression, which adds an $L_2$ penalty $\lambda \sum_j \beta_j^2$ to the residual sum of squares, shrinks coefficient estimates toward zero, trading bias for reduced variance in the presence of collinearity. While ridge regression sacrifices unbiasedness, it can achieve lower mean squared error when collinearity is severe.

Heteroskedasticity diagnostics assess whether error variance depends on regressor values, violating the homoskedasticity assumption. The Breusch-Pagan test regresses squared residuals $\hat{\epsilon}_i^2$ on regressors $x_i$, testing whether the $R^2$ of this auxiliary regression significantly exceeds zero. The White test generalizes this approach, including cross-products and squared regressors to detect more flexible heteroskedasticity patterns. Graphical diagnostics, plotting residuals against fitted values or individual regressors, often reveal heteroskedasticity more intuitively than formal tests. When heteroskedasticity is detected, report heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, use weighted least squares (WLS) if the variance function is known, or transform variables to stabilize variance.

Outliers and influential observations disproportionately affect OLS coefficient estimates, as the squared residual criterion heavily penalizes large deviations. Leverage, measured by the diagonal elements of the projection matrix $h_{ii} = P_{ii}$, quantifies how much observation $i$ influences fitted values. High-leverage points have regressor values far from the sample mean, pulling the fitted regression line toward themselves. Cook's distance combines leverage and residuals to identify influential observations:

$$D_i = \frac{\hat{\epsilon}_i^2}{(k+1)\hat{\sigma}^2} \cdot \frac{h_{ii}}{(1 - h_{ii})^2}$$

Observations with $D_i > 1$ merit scrutiny, as their removal would substantially alter coefficient estimates. Robust regression methods, such as iteratively reweighted least squares or $M$-estimators, downweight outliers automatically, reducing sensitivity to extreme observations. However, outliers may convey genuine information about tail behavior or policy effects in unusual contexts, warranting careful substantive interpretation rather than mechanical removal.

Specification testing evaluates whether the linear functional form adequately captures the true data-generating process. Ramsey's RESET test augments the regression with powers of fitted values $\hat{y}_i^2, \hat{y}_i^3$, testing whether these terms have explanatory power. Rejection indicates functional form misspecification, prompting inclusion of polynomial terms, interactions, or nonlinear transformations. Link specification tests, such as the Box-Cox transformation, estimate the optimal transformation parameter $\lambda$ simultaneously with regression coefficients, nesting linear, logarithmic, and power transformations in a unified framework.

Omitted variable bias arises when relevant regressors are excluded from the model, violating the strict exogeneity assumption. Let the true model be $y = X_1\beta_1 + X_2\beta_2 + \epsilon$, but suppose we estimate the short regression $y = X_1\beta_1 + u$ by OLS, omitting $X_2$. The omitted variable bias formula shows that:

$$E[\hat{\beta}_1] = \beta_1 + (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_2\beta_2$$

The bias equals the effect of omitted variables $\beta_2$ multiplied by the regression coefficients from regressing $X_2$ on $X_1$. Bias is zero only if omitted variables are orthogonal to included regressors ($X_1'X_2 = 0$) or have no effect on the outcome ($\beta_2 = 0$). Policy analysts mitigate omitted variable bias by including rich control variables, exploiting panel data to difference out time-invariant confounders, or using instrumental variables to isolate exogenous variation.

Measurement error in regressors attenuates coefficient estimates toward zero, a phenomenon known as attenuation bias. Suppose the true regressor $x_i^*$ is unobserved, and we observe $x_i = x_i^* + v_i$, where $v_i$ is classical measurement error independent of $x_i^*$ and $\epsilon_i$. The probability limit of the OLS slope estimator in the bivariate case is:

$$\text{plim} \, \hat{\beta}_1 = \beta_1 \cdot \frac{\text{Var}(x^*)}{\text{Var}(x^*) + \text{Var}(v)} = \beta_1 \cdot \lambda$$

where $\lambda < 1$ is the reliability ratio, the signal-to-noise ratio of the observed regressor. Measurement error biases estimates toward zero, understating true effect magnitudes. Instrumental variables or structural equation models that explicitly model measurement error can correct this bias, though these methods require valid instruments or auxiliary data on measurement quality.

Serial correlation in time-series regressions violates the independence assumption, inducing correlation between residuals at different time periods. The Durbin-Watson statistic tests for first-order autocorrelation, comparing $\text{DW} = \sum_{t=2}^n (\hat{\epsilon}_t - \hat{\epsilon}_{t-1})^2 / \sum_{t=1}^n \hat{\epsilon}_t^2$ to critical values. Values near 2 indicate no autocorrelation, values near 0 indicate positive autocorrelation, and values near 4 indicate negative autocorrelation. Newey-West heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) standard errors correct for both heteroskedasticity and serial correlation, weighting lagged autocovariances by a kernel function to ensure positive definiteness. Dynamic models incorporating lagged dependent variables or ARMA error structures explicitly model serial dependence, improving efficiency and forecast accuracy.

Model selection criteria balance goodness-of-fit against parsimony, penalizing complexity to avoid overfitting. The Akaike information criterion (AIC), defined as $\text{AIC} = 2k - 2\ln(L)$, where $L$ is the maximized likelihood, trades off likelihood for the number of parameters. The Bayesian information criterion (BIC), $\text{BIC} = k\ln(n) - 2\ln(L)$, imposes a stronger penalty for large $n$, favoring more parsimonious models. These criteria guide variable selection and functional form choice, though they do not replace substantive economic reasoning about which regressors causally affect the outcome.

Cross-validation partitions data into training and test sets, repeatedly estimating the model on training data and evaluating prediction error on held-out test data. $K$-fold cross-validation divides data into $K$ subsets, using each subset once as a test set while training on the remaining $K-1$ folds. The average test-set prediction error, often measured by mean squared error or mean absolute error, provides an unbiased estimate of out-of-sample performance. Cross-validation guards against overfitting by penalizing models that memorize training data rather than learning generalizable patterns, a critical concern when estimating flexible models with many regressors.

Bootstrap inference resamples data with replacement to construct empirical sampling distributions of estimators, circumventing reliance on asymptotic normality. The percentile bootstrap confidence interval for $\beta_j$ is formed by taking the $\alpha/2$ and $1 - \alpha/2$ quantiles of bootstrap estimates $\hat{\beta}_j^{(b)}$ for $b = 1, \ldots, B$, where $B$ denotes the number of bootstrap replications. Bootstrap methods accommodate non-normal errors, heteroskedasticity, and clustered data, offering robust inference when classical asymptotic approximations fail. However, bootstrap validity requires that the resampling scheme mirrors the dependence structure of the data-generating process, necessitating block bootstraps for time series or cluster bootstraps for panel data.

The workflow for robust policy inference synthesizes these diagnostic and corrective techniques into a coherent estimation strategy. Begin with exploratory data analysis to identify outliers, distributional features, and potential nonlinearities. Estimate the baseline OLS specification, reporting both conventional and robust standard errors. Conduct specification tests for functional form, omitted variables, and heteroskedasticity. If diagnostics reveal violations, implement corrective measures: robust standard errors, transformed variables, additional controls, or alternative estimators. Validate the final specification using out-of-sample prediction or sensitivity analysis, varying specification choices to assess robustness. Report results transparently, documenting estimation choices, diagnostic test results, and sensitivity checks to facilitate replication and critical evaluation.
