# Lesson 3: Model Specification, Heteroskedasticity, Autocorrelation

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The classical linear regression model rests on a foundation of assumptions so fundamental that their violation transforms policy inference from science into speculation. When a central bank economist estimates the impact of interest rate changes on unemployment, or when a fiscal policy advisor quantifies the multiplier effect of infrastructure spending, the validity of their conclusions hinges entirely on whether the underlying econometric model has been correctly specified and whether the error structure behaves as theory demands. This lesson confronts the three most consequential departures from classical assumptions that plague applied policy work: omitted variable bias, heteroskedasticity, and autocorrelation.

Consider the fundamental regression equation $y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \beta_2 x_{2i} + \cdots + \beta_k x_{ki} + \varepsilon_i$, where the disturbance term $\varepsilon_i$ is assumed to be independently and identically distributed with mean zero and constant variance $\sigma^2$. The Gauss-Markov theorem guarantees that ordinary least squares (OLS) produces the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE) under these conditions. But policy environments rarely cooperate with these idealized assumptions. Time-series data from national accounts exhibit persistence and momentum. Cross-sectional household surveys display variance that explodes with income levels. Panel data on regional economic performance carry both dimensions of violation simultaneously.

Omitted variable bias emerges when a regressor correlated with both the dependent variable and included explanatory variables is excluded from the specification. The bias is not a statistical accident but a mathematical certainty. If the true data-generating process includes $z_i$ but the analyst estimates a model without it, the OLS estimator for the included variable absorbs the effect of the omitted variable through the correlation structure. The magnitude and direction of the bias depend on two distinct channels: the correlation between the omitted variable and the dependent variable, and the correlation between the omitted variable and the included regressors. A central bank estimating the output gap without accounting for structural supply shocks will systematically attribute demand-side effects to monetary policy, leading to misguided interest rate adjustments.

The algebra is instructive. Suppose the true model is $y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \gamma z_i + u_i$, but we estimate $y_i = \tilde{\beta}_0 + \tilde{\beta}_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i$. The OLS estimator $\tilde{\beta}_1$ converges in probability to $\beta_1 + \gamma \frac{\text{Cov}(x_i, z_i)}{\text{Var}(x_i)}$. The bias term $\gamma \frac{\text{Cov}(x_i, z_i)}{\text{Var}(x_i)}$ is the product of the true coefficient on the omitted variable and the regression coefficient from projecting the omitted variable onto the included regressor. This decomposition reveals that omitted variable bias vanishes if either the omitted variable is uncorrelated with the dependent variable (making $\gamma = 0$) or if the omitted variable is uncorrelated with the included regressor (making the covariance term zero). In policy contexts, neither condition typically holds. Institutional quality affects both economic growth and the effectiveness of fiscal stimulus. Labor force participation rates influence both GDP growth and the measured unemployment rate. The bias is structural, not sampling noise, and it does not disappear with larger datasets.

Functional form misspecification constitutes a related but distinct threat. Even when all relevant variables are included, imposing linearity on inherently nonlinear relationships generates biased estimates and invalid inference. The production function relating capital and labor to output exhibits diminishing returns. The Phillips curve mapping unemployment to inflation is convex, not linear. Tax revenue as a function of tax rates traces the Laffer curve, rising initially but eventually declining. When the true relationship is $y_i = f(x_i) + \varepsilon_i$ but the analyst estimates $y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + u_i$, the residuals $u_i$ contain systematic patterns that violate the zero conditional mean assumption. The consequences extend beyond bias in point estimates to invalidate hypothesis tests and confidence intervals.

Ramsey's regression specification error test (RESET) provides a diagnostic tool for detecting functional form misspecification. The test augments the original regression with powers of the fitted values and tests whether these additional terms have zero coefficients. If the true relationship is nonlinear, the fitted values from the misspecified linear model contain information about the nonlinearity, and their powers will enter significantly. The RESET statistic follows an F-distribution under the null hypothesis of correct specification. Rejection signals that the linear functional form is inadequate, though it does not prescribe the correct alternative. The analyst must then deploy economic theory, visualization of partial regression plots, and Box-Cox transformations to identify the appropriate nonlinear specification.

Heteroskedasticity, the condition where $\text{Var}(\varepsilon_i | x_i) = \sigma_i^2$ varies across observations, does not bias OLS coefficient estimates but destroys their efficiency and invalidates standard error calculations. The policy consequences are severe. A regression estimating the impact of education spending on test scores using district-level data will exhibit larger residual variance in large urban districts than in small rural districts simply due to population heterogeneity. If standard errors are computed under the false assumption of homoskedasticity, hypothesis tests will have incorrect size, confidence intervals will have incorrect coverage, and policy recommendations will rest on statistically invalid foundations.

The White test for heteroskedasticity exploits the insight that if conditional variance depends on regressors, an auxiliary regression of squared residuals on regressors and their cross-products will exhibit explanatory power. The test statistic is $n \times R^2$ from this auxiliary regression, asymptotically distributed as $\chi^2$ with degrees of freedom equal to the number of regressors in the auxiliary equation. The Breusch-Pagan test offers a parametric alternative, regressing squared residuals on the original regressors and testing for joint significance. Both tests detect heteroskedasticity but do not specify its functional form, which may be multiplicative, exponential, or follow some other pattern determined by the economic data-generating process.

Autocorrelation, or serial correlation, arises when disturbances exhibit temporal or spatial dependence: $\text{Cov}(\varepsilon_i, \varepsilon_j | X) \neq 0$ for $i \neq j$. In time-series policy data, autocorrelation is the rule rather than the exception. GDP growth exhibits momentum. Inflation displays persistence. Unemployment rates follow autoregressive patterns. The Durbin-Watson statistic tests for first-order autocorrelation by computing $d = \frac{\sum_{t=2}^n (\hat{\varepsilon}_t - \hat{\varepsilon}_{t-1})^2}{\sum_{t=1}^n \hat{\varepsilon}_t^2}$, which ranges from zero (perfect positive autocorrelation) to four (perfect negative autocorrelation), with two indicating no autocorrelation. Critical values depend on sample size and the number of regressors, with inconclusive regions near the critical thresholds.

The Breusch-Godfrey test generalizes Durbin-Watson to higher-order autocorrelation and models with lagged dependent variables. The test regresses OLS residuals on the original regressors and $p$ lags of the residuals, then computes $(n - p) \times R^2$, which follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with $p$ degrees of freedom under the null of no autocorrelation. Unlike Durbin-Watson, the Breusch-Godfrey test remains valid when the regressor matrix includes lagged dependent variables, a common feature in dynamic policy models where current outcomes depend on past outcomes.

The econometric challenge is not merely to detect these violations but to correct for them while preserving the economic interpretation of coefficients. Heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, feasible generalized least squares (FGLS), and Newey-West autocorrelation-consistent covariance estimators provide solutions, but each imposes trade-offs between efficiency, robustness, and computational complexity. The policy analyst must navigate this landscape with both statistical rigor and economic intuition, recognizing that econometric technique serves economic understanding, not the reverse.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The machinery of modern econometric correction begins with the recognition that OLS remains consistent under heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation, but its variance estimator becomes biased. The classical variance formula $\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}) = \sigma^2 (X'X)^{-1}$ assumes $\text{Var}(\varepsilon | X) = \sigma^2 I_n$. When this assumption fails, the correct variance is $\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}) = (X'X)^{-1} X' \Omega X (X'X)^{-1}$, where $\Omega$ is the true variance-covariance matrix of the disturbances. The sandwich form of this estimator—with $(X'X)^{-1}$ on both sides and $X' \Omega X$ in the middle—gives rise to the term "sandwich estimator" for robust variance calculations.

White's heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC) covariance estimator replaces the unknown $\Omega$ with a diagonal matrix containing squared OLS residuals: $\hat{\Omega} = \text{diag}(\hat{\varepsilon}_1^2, \hat{\varepsilon}_2^2, \ldots, \hat{\varepsilon}_n^2)$. The resulting variance estimator $\hat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\beta}) = (X'X)^{-1} X' \hat{\Omega} X (X'X)^{-1}$ produces standard errors that remain consistent even when the conditional variance is heteroskedastic. The HC0 variant uses raw squared residuals, while HC1 applies a degrees-of-freedom correction $\frac{n}{n-k}$, HC2 adjusts for leverage by dividing each squared residual by $1 - h_i$ where $h_i$ is the $i$-th diagonal element of the hat matrix $H = X(X'X)^{-1}X'$, and HC3 uses $(1 - h_i)^{-2}$ for stronger correction in the presence of high-leverage observations.

The practical implementation in statistical software typically involves a single argument change. In R's `lmtest` package, the `coeftest()` function accepts a `vcov` argument specifying the variance-covariance estimator: `coeftest(model, vcov = vcovHC(model, type = "HC3"))`. In Stata, the `robust` option after regression commands automatically computes HC1 standard errors. Python's `statsmodels` library provides the `get_robustcov_results()` method with a `cov_type` parameter accepting values like `'HC0'`, `'HC1'`, `'HC2'`, or `'HC3'`. The coefficient estimates remain identical to OLS; only the standard errors, t-statistics, and p-values change.

The computational workflow for detecting and correcting heteroskedasticity follows a diagnostic-then-correct pattern. First, estimate the model via OLS and extract residuals. Second, conduct a White test by regressing squared residuals on regressors, their squares, and cross-products, then compute the test statistic. Third, if heteroskedasticity is detected, re-estimate standard errors using a heteroskedasticity-consistent covariance estimator. Fourth, compare inference under classical and robust standard errors to assess the magnitude of the correction. If robust standard errors differ substantially from classical standard errors, heteroskedasticity is not merely a theoretical concern but an empirical reality that would have led to incorrect inference.

For autocorrelation, the architecture shifts from sandwich estimators to generalized least squares (GLS) and dynamic error structures. The first-order autoregressive process AR(1) models the error term as $\varepsilon_t = \rho \varepsilon_{t-1} + u_t$, where $u_t$ is white noise and $|\rho| < 1$ ensures stationarity. The Cochrane-Orcutt procedure estimates $\rho$ from OLS residuals, transforms the data by quasi-differencing $y_t^* = y_t - \rho y_{t-1}$ and $x_t^* = x_t - \rho x_{t-1}$, then re-estimates the model on transformed data. The transformation eliminates autocorrelation by construction, producing efficient estimates. The procedure iterates until $\rho$ converges, typically requiring three to five iterations.

The Prais-Winsten modification preserves the first observation by applying the transformation $y_1^* = \sqrt{1 - \rho^2} \, y_1$ and $x_1^* = \sqrt{1 - \rho^2} \, x_1$, which maintains efficiency without discarding data. Both Cochrane-Orcutt and Prais-Winsten assume a known autocorrelation structure, but feasible GLS (FGLS) extends the approach to unknown autocorrelation by estimating $\rho$ from residuals. The FGLS estimator is consistent and asymptotically efficient but not finite-sample efficient, and its standard errors require adjustment for the estimation of $\rho$.

Newey-West heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) standard errors provide a robust alternative that does not require specifying or estimating the autocorrelation structure. The Newey-West estimator constructs the variance-covariance matrix as $\hat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\beta}) = (X'X)^{-1} \left( \sum_{j=-L}^L w_j \sum_{t=|j|+1}^n x_t x_{t-|j|}' \hat{\varepsilon}_t \hat{\varepsilon}_{t-|j|} \right) (X'X)^{-1}$, where $L$ is the lag truncation parameter and $w_j$ are kernel weights. The Bartlett kernel uses $w_j = 1 - \frac{|j|}{L+1}$, declining linearly to zero at lag $L$. The choice of $L$ balances bias and variance: too small and autocorrelation at longer lags is ignored, too large and variance inflates. A common rule of thumb sets $L = \lfloor 4 (n/100)^{2/9} \rfloor$ for annual data and $L = \lfloor 4 (n/100)^{1/4} \rfloor$ for quarterly data.

The distinction between FGLS and HAC standard errors is subtle but consequential. FGLS transforms the data to eliminate autocorrelation, producing efficient coefficient estimates but requiring correct specification of the autocorrelation structure. HAC standard errors leave the coefficient estimates unchanged and correct only the variance estimator, remaining robust to misspecification but sacrificing efficiency. In policy applications where the autocorrelation structure is uncertain or time-varying, HAC standard errors provide insurance against misspecification at the cost of larger standard errors.

The implementation mechanics differ across software platforms. In R, the `sandwich` package provides `vcovHAC()` with arguments for kernel type and bandwidth selection: `coeftest(model, vcov = vcovHAC(model, kernel = "Bartlett", bandwidth = 4))`. Stata's `newey` command computes Newey-West standard errors directly: `newey y x1 x2, lag(4)`. Python's `statsmodels` offers `cov_type='HAC'` in regression methods with a `cov_kwds` dictionary specifying the maxlag parameter. The computational cost increases with lag length, as each lag adds matrix multiplications proportional to the number of observations and regressors.

The workflow for autocorrelation correction parallels the heteroskedasticity protocol. First, estimate via OLS and conduct a Durbin-Watson or Breusch-Godfrey test. Second, if autocorrelation is detected, decide between FGLS for efficiency or HAC standard errors for robustness. Third, if choosing FGLS, determine the autocorrelation order via information criteria or residual correlograms, then apply Cochrane-Orcutt or Prais-Winsten. Fourth, validate the correction by testing residuals from the transformed model for remaining autocorrelation. If autocorrelation persists, increase the order or switch to HAC standard errors.

Panel data introduces both cross-sectional heteroskedasticity and serial correlation simultaneously. The error structure decomposes into $\varepsilon_{it} = \alpha_i + u_{it}$, where $\alpha_i$ is a time-invariant individual effect and $u_{it}$ is an idiosyncratic shock. If $\alpha_i$ is correlated with regressors, fixed effects estimation is required; if uncorrelated, random effects estimation is efficient. The Hausman test compares fixed and random effects estimates, with rejection indicating correlation between individual effects and regressors. Cluster-robust standard errors account for correlation within clusters (individuals or groups) while allowing arbitrary correlation patterns. The implementation adds a `cluster` argument: in R, `vcovCL(model, cluster = ~ id)`; in Stata, `reg y x, vce(cluster id)`.

The general estimation strategy for complex error structures involves a hierarchy of corrections. Start with OLS and classical standard errors. Diagnose heteroskedasticity via White or Breusch-Pagan tests, and autocorrelation via Durbin-Watson or Breusch-Godfrey. If only heteroskedasticity is present, use HC3 standard errors. If only autocorrelation is present, choose between FGLS and Newey-West depending on confidence in the autocorrelation structure. If both are present, use Newey-West HAC or cluster-robust standard errors. If panel structure exists, apply fixed or random effects with cluster-robust variance estimation. At each stage, compare inference across estimators to assess robustness. If conclusions change dramatically, the specification is fragile and requires deeper investigation or instrumental variables to address omitted variable bias.

The architecture of model specification testing centers on the information-theoretic trade-off between fit and parsimony. The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) computes $-2 \log L + 2k$, penalizing models with more parameters. The Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) uses $-2 \log L + k \log n$, imposing a stronger penalty for large samples. Lower values indicate better models. Cross-validation divides the sample into training and test sets, estimates the model on the training set, and evaluates prediction error on the test set. The procedure repeats over multiple random splits, averaging prediction errors to assess out-of-sample performance. Models that overfit training data exhibit large test errors.

For nested models, likelihood ratio tests provide classical inference. If model A is nested within model B, the test statistic $2(\log L_B - \log L_A)$ follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the number of additional parameters in model B. Rejection indicates that the additional parameters significantly improve fit. For non-nested models, the Davidson-MacKinnon J-test regresses the dependent variable on regressors from model A plus the fitted values from model B. If the coefficient on the fitted values is significant, model A is rejected. The procedure is asymmetric; reversing the roles of A and B may yield different conclusions, indicating that neither model dominates.

The Box-Cox transformation offers a data-driven approach to functional form. The transformation $y^{(\lambda)} = \frac{y^\lambda - 1}{\lambda}$ for $\lambda \neq 0$ and $\log y$ for $\lambda = 0$ nests common specifications: $\lambda = 1$ is linear, $\lambda = 0$ is log-linear, $\lambda = 0.5$ is square-root. Maximum likelihood estimation jointly estimates $\lambda$ and regression coefficients, selecting the transformation that maximizes the likelihood. The profile likelihood over $\lambda$ reveals the sensitivity of fit to functional form. If the profile is flat, functional form is not critical; if sharply peaked, the optimal transformation is empirically determined and deviations degrade performance.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

The transition from diagnostic testing to operational policy analysis demands a workflow that balances statistical rigor with computational efficiency. Large administrative datasets from tax authorities or social security systems contain millions of observations. Panel data on municipalities or firms span decades with annual or quarterly frequency. Estimation speed, memory management, and numerical stability become constraints, not mere conveniences.

Heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors scale quadratically with the number of observations in naive implementations, as the $X' \hat{\Omega} X$ matrix multiplication requires $O(n k^2)$ operations. Sparse matrix techniques exploit the diagonal structure of $\hat{\Omega}$ to reduce complexity. Block matrix algorithms partition large datasets into chunks that fit in cache, minimizing memory access latency. Multi-threaded computation parallelizes the calculation across cores, particularly effective when cluster-robust standard errors require computing within-cluster covariances independently.

The numerical stability of sandwich estimators depends on the condition number of $X'X$, the ratio of largest to smallest eigenvalues. Multicollinearity inflates the condition number, magnifying rounding errors in matrix inversion. Centering and scaling regressors reduces the condition number by stabilizing the magnitude of matrix elements. Ridge regression adds a small constant to the diagonal of $X'X$ before inversion, trading a small bias for substantial variance reduction and numerical stability. In policy contexts where predictions matter more than unbiased parameter estimates, this trade-off is often favorable.

FGLS estimation introduces additional failure modes. If the autocorrelation parameter $\rho$ is estimated near the boundary $|\hat{\rho}| \approx 1$, the transformation collapses to a first difference, and the model loses the level information entirely. The Cochrane-Orcutt iteration may fail to converge if residuals exhibit complex autocorrelation patterns not well approximated by AR(1). Divergence signals fundamental misspecification, not computational error. The remedy is to test for higher-order autocorrelation or structural breaks that fragment the sample into regimes with different dynamics.

Newey-West standard errors exhibit sensitivity to bandwidth selection. Automatic bandwidth rules based on asymptotic theory perform poorly in finite samples with strong autocorrelation. The Andrews rule minimizes asymptotic mean squared error but requires estimating spectral density, introducing estimation error. A robust approach computes standard errors over a range of bandwidths and reports results if they stabilize, or flags the model as unreliable if they vary wildly. Policy conclusions should not hinge on arbitrary bandwidth choices.

Cluster-robust standard errors assume clusters are independent, an assumption often violated in spatial or network settings. Regional economic shocks spill over to neighboring regions. Financial institutions are interconnected through interbank lending. Ignoring these dependencies underestimates standard errors, inflating significance. Spatial econometric models incorporate spatial correlation explicitly via spatial lag or spatial error specifications. Network robust standard errors adjust for correlation induced by network structure, though implementation remains cutting-edge and requires specialized software.

Panel data fixed effects with large numbers of groups strain memory and computation time. The within transformation subtracts group means, eliminating fixed effects but requiring storage of group-demeaned data. For panels with millions of individuals, this becomes infeasible. The Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem allows projection onto the orthogonal complement of fixed effects without explicitly constructing demeaned data. Modern algorithms like the method of alternating projections (MAP) or the LSMR iterative solver exploit this structure, scaling to arbitrary panel dimensions.

The most insidious failure mode is false confidence from robust standard errors applied to misspecified models. Heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors correct for efficiency loss but do not address omitted variable bias or functional form misspecification. A regression with severe omitted variable bias yields precisely estimated but deeply biased coefficients, and robust standard errors merely tighten confidence intervals around the wrong parameter. The analyst must distinguish between violations of classical assumptions that invalidate inference (heteroskedasticity, autocorrelation) and violations that invalidate identification (omitted variables, simultaneity). Robust standard errors solve the former, not the latter.

Pre-testing for heteroskedasticity or autocorrelation and then conditionally applying corrections introduces pre-test bias. The decision to use robust standard errors depends on a test statistic with its own sampling distribution, and conditioning inference on this preliminary test distorts coverage probabilities. A conservative approach uses robust standard errors by default, accepting modest efficiency loss under homoskedasticity in exchange for robustness. This aligns with the principle that policy inference should be robust to specification uncertainty, not optimized for a single idealized model.

Computational workflow optimization involves caching intermediate results. The $X'X$ and $X'y$ matrices need computation only once, reused across different variance estimators. The QR decomposition of $X$ provides both coefficient estimates and the foundation for robust variance calculations, avoiding redundant matrix operations. Profiling code execution identifies bottlenecks, often revealing that 90% of computation time concentrates in 10% of the code, typically matrix inversions or eigenvalue decompositions. Replacing dense linear algebra with sparse routines or switching from eigenvalue to Cholesky decomposition can yield order-of-magnitude speedups.

Model specification testing requires multiple comparisons, raising familywise error rates. Testing ten nested specifications at the 5% significance level yields a 40% probability of at least one false rejection under the global null. Bonferroni correction divides the significance level by the number of tests, controlling familywise error rate but sacrificing power. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure controls the false discovery rate, allowing more rejections while maintaining a specified proportion of false positives. In exploratory policy analysis, false discovery rate control balances discovery of genuine effects against spurious findings.

The reproducibility of econometric analysis demands version control of data, code, and software environments. Regression results depend on software versions, random number generators for bootstraps, and floating-point precision. A policy recommendation based on a regression estimated in Stata 15 may differ from the same model in Stata 18 due to numerical algorithm updates. Containerization via Docker or virtual environments in Python ensures reproducibility by freezing the entire software stack. Literate programming tools like R Markdown or Jupyter Notebooks integrate code, output, and narrative, making analysis transparent and auditable.

Validation against external data sources provides a reality check on econometric estimates. A fiscal multiplier estimated from historical data should align with estimates from natural experiments or structural models. A labor supply elasticity from survey data should cohere with evidence from tax reform quasi-experiments. Triangulation across datasets, identification strategies, and estimation methods builds confidence in the robustness of findings. Discrepancies signal either misspecification in one approach or genuine heterogeneity requiring explanation.

The ultimate optimization is clarity of communication. A policy memo presenting regression results must convey the economic substance, not drown readers in t-statistics and diagnostic tests. Report coefficient magnitudes in economically meaningful units: a one percentage point increase in the unemployment rate associates with a $X billion decrease in tax revenue, not an abstract coefficient of $-0.037$. State standard errors and confidence intervals but relegate diagnostic tests to an appendix. Explain functional form choices in economic terms: logs because elasticity is constant, squares because marginal effects diminish. Acknowledge limitations: robust standard errors correct for heteroskedasticity, but omitted variables remain a threat. Policy inference demands not just technical correctness but communicative precision.
