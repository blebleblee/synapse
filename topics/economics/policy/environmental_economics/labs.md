# Operational Verification Labs: Environmental Economics

## Objective & Operational Lab Setup

Environmental economics sits at the operational frontier where market mechanisms, regulatory policy instruments, and ecological system dynamics intersect. This laboratory guide trains policy practitioners to execute rigorous empirical analysis of pollution externalities, design implementable market-based environmental instruments, and evaluate the allocative efficiency and distributional consequences of climate policy interventions. The practitioner will operationalize the theoretical apparatus of externality correction—Pigouvian taxation, cap-and-trade permit markets, and command-and-control regulation—through hands-on construction of damage cost functions, marginal abatement cost curves, and general equilibrium incidence models.

The analytical environment requires a quantitative spreadsheet platform capable of nonlinear optimization, Monte Carlo simulation, and constrained solver routines. Microsoft Excel with Solver add-in, LibreOffice Calc with NLPSOLVER extension, or Google Sheets with optimization scripts provide the necessary computational infrastructure. For advanced partial equilibrium and computable general equilibrium (CGE) modeling, practitioners should provision GAMS (General Algebraic Modeling System) or Python with Pyomo optimization library. All empirical exercises utilize publicly accessible datasets: EPA National Emissions Inventory, EU Emissions Trading System transaction logs, World Bank carbon pricing databases, and NOAA climate damage attribution studies.

The operational setup begins with data acquisition and validation. Download the EPA's Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program facility-level emissions dataset, covering approximately 8,000 large stationary sources across the United States. This dataset provides annual carbon dioxide equivalent emissions by facility, NAICS industry classification, geographic coordinates, and compliance status. Validate data integrity by cross-referencing reported emissions against Energy Information Administration fuel consumption statistics and applying mass balance closure checks. Construct a master spreadsheet workspace with separate tabs for raw data ingestion, cleaned analytical datasets, parameter calibration tables, optimization model formulations, and results visualization dashboards.

Configure the optimization solver environment by enabling Excel's Solver add-in through File → Options → Add-ins → Manage Excel Add-ins → Solver Add-in. Verify solver engine selection: the GRG Nonlinear engine handles smooth nonlinear objective functions and constraints, while the Evolutionary engine applies genetic algorithms for non-smooth or discontinuous problems. Set solver precision parameters to 0.000001 for convergence tolerance and enable automatic scaling for numerically heterogeneous constraint systems. For Python-based workflows, install Pyomo via pip and configure an open-source solver backend such as IPOPT for nonlinear programming or GLPK for mixed-integer linear programs.

Establish a reproducible analytical workflow by documenting all data transformations, parameter assumptions, and model specifications in a laboratory notebook format. Every empirical exercise should generate three artifacts: a technical methodology memo describing the economic model structure and calibration procedure, a computational implementation file containing all formulas and solver configurations, and a results interpretation report linking numerical findings back to policy mechanism design principles. This triad ensures operational transparency and enables independent verification by peer reviewers or regulatory agencies evaluating policy proposals.

## Exercise 1: Foundational Practical Implementation

### Constructing Marginal Abatement Cost Curves from Engineering Process Data

The marginal abatement cost (MAC) curve represents the foundation of environmental policy instrument design. It maps the incremental cost of reducing pollution by one additional unit across the entire feasible abatement technology set. Policy practitioners must construct empirically grounded MAC curves from engineering cost surveys and facility-level emissions data to calibrate efficient pollution tax rates or allocate tradable permit quantities. This exercise builds a bottom-up MAC curve for carbon dioxide emissions reduction in the U.S. electric power sector using technology-specific abatement cost estimates and generation fleet composition data.

Begin by assembling the abatement technology menu. Download the National Renewable Energy Laboratory's Annual Technology Baseline dataset, which provides levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) and carbon intensity parameters for coal-fired generation, natural gas combined cycle, wind, solar photovoltaic, and nuclear technologies. Extract the following parameters for each technology: capital cost per kilowatt ($/kW), fixed operations and maintenance cost ($/kW-year), variable operations and maintenance cost ($/MWh), fuel cost ($/MMBtu), heat rate (MMBtu/MWh), capacity factor (%), and carbon emissions intensity (metric tons CO₂/MWh). Populate a technology parameter table with current-year values and validate against Energy Information Administration reported costs.

Construct the baseline emissions scenario by retrieving EPA eGRID data on existing generation capacity by technology type. The eGRID dataset reports total installed capacity, annual generation output, and carbon emissions for approximately 10,000 generating units. Aggregate this micro-data to technology-level totals: coal capacity (MW), coal generation (MWh), coal emissions (metric tons CO₂), and so forth for each generation type. Calculate the baseline carbon intensity of the electricity system as total emissions divided by total generation, typically around 0.4 metric tons CO₂ per MWh for the U.S. grid.

Formulate the abatement cost calculation for each technology substitution pathway. Consider the replacement of coal-fired generation with natural gas combined cycle as the primary near-term abatement option. The abatement quantity per MWh of substitution equals the difference in carbon intensities: if coal emits 0.9 metric tons CO₂/MWh and natural gas emits 0.4 metric tons CO₂/MWh, each MWh of substitution abates 0.5 metric tons. The abatement cost per metric ton CO₂ equals the difference in levelized costs divided by the emissions reduction: if coal LCOE is $60/MWh and gas LCOE is $45/MWh, the fuel switching actually generates negative abatement cost of -$30 per metric ton, indicating a profitable no-regrets opportunity.

Extend this calculation across the full technology set. Wind and solar substitution for coal yields larger per-MWh abatement (0.9 metric tons) but typically positive abatement costs ranging from $20 to $80 per metric ton depending on regional renewable resource quality and transmission infrastructure requirements. Nuclear substitution provides maximum abatement per MWh but faces capital cost barriers pushing abatement costs above $100 per metric ton. Energy efficiency measures reducing electricity demand entirely avoid both generation costs and emissions, often yielding negative abatement costs when consumer energy savings exceed the annualized cost of efficiency investments.

Construct the MAC curve in spreadsheet format by creating a four-column table: Technology, Abatement Potential (metric tons CO₂), Abatement Cost ($/metric ton CO₂), and Cumulative Abatement (metric tons CO₂). Sort technologies in ascending order of abatement cost from most negative (highest profit) to most positive (highest cost). Calculate cumulative abatement by summing the abatement potential column progressively. The abatement potential for each technology equals the maximum feasible deployment constrained by physical resource availability, grid integration limits, or market penetration barriers. For natural gas substitution, the abatement potential equals the total coal generation that could technically be replaced by available gas capacity, limited by pipeline infrastructure and supply elasticity.

Plot the MAC curve as a step function with abatement cost on the vertical axis and cumulative abatement on the horizontal axis. Each technology forms a horizontal step spanning its abatement potential width at its abatement cost height. The resulting curve typically exhibits three regions: negative-cost abatement opportunities (energy efficiency and profitable fuel switching), low-cost abatement (renewable substitution up to grid integration constraints), and high-cost abatement (emerging technologies like carbon capture and storage or direct air capture). The intersection of the MAC curve with any horizontal line representing a carbon price reveals the efficient abatement quantity: firms reduce emissions up to the point where marginal abatement cost equals the carbon price.

Calibrate policy instruments using the MAC curve. A Pigouvian carbon tax set at $50 per metric ton induces abatement equal to the cumulative quantity at the $50 cost level on the MAC curve. A cap-and-trade system allocating permits equal to baseline emissions minus this same abatement quantity generates an equilibrium permit price of $50, achieving identical abatement. The MAC curve also reveals policy cost: the total abatement cost equals the area under the MAC curve from zero to the chosen abatement quantity, representing the resource cost of pollution reduction borne by regulated firms.

### Implementing a Cap-and-Trade Permit Market Simulation

Tradable permit systems operationalize the Coase theorem by establishing property rights in pollution and allowing least-cost abatement allocation through decentralized trading. This exercise constructs a discrete-choice cap-and-trade simulation with heterogeneous firms facing different marginal abatement cost schedules. The practitioner will observe permit price formation, verify cost-effectiveness relative to uniform emission standards, and analyze distributional impacts of alternative initial allocation rules.

Design the simulation economy with ten stylized firms representing different industry sectors with distinct abatement cost structures. Create a firm parameter table with columns for Firm ID, Baseline Emissions (metric tons), and Marginal Abatement Cost Function. Model marginal abatement cost as a linear function of abatement quantity: MAC_i(q_i) = a_i + b_i × q_i, where a_i represents the intercept capturing initial abatement cost and b_i represents the slope capturing the increasing marginal cost of deeper reductions. Calibrate parameters to reflect real-world heterogeneity: energy-intensive manufacturers face steep abatement costs (high a and b), while service sector firms with modest energy use face low abatement costs.

Specify numerical parameters for a ten-firm example. Firm 1 (coal-fired power plant): baseline emissions 10,000 tons, a₁ = $20/ton, b₁ = $0.01/ton². Firm 2 (natural gas plant): baseline 4,000 tons, a₂ = $15/ton, b₂ = $0.008/ton². Firm 3 (cement manufacturer): baseline 8,000 tons, a₃ = $40/ton, b₃ = $0.015/ton². Firm 4 (steel mill): baseline 12,000 tons, a₄ = $35/ton, b₄ = $0.012/ton². Firm 5 (chemical plant): baseline 6,000 tons, a₅ = $25/ton, b₅ = $0.009/ton². Firm 6 (refinery): baseline 9,000 tons, a₆ = $30/ton, b₆ = $0.011/ton². Firm 7 (paper mill): baseline 5,000 tons, a₇ = $18/ton, b₇ = $0.007/ton². Firm 8 (glass manufacturer): baseline 3,000 tons, a₈ = $22/ton, b₈ = $0.01/ton². Firm 9 (commercial building): baseline 2,000 tons, a₉ = $10/ton, b₉ = $0.005/ton². Firm 10 (data center): baseline 1,500 tons, a₁₀ = $12/ton, b₁₀ = $0.006/ton². Total baseline emissions sum to 60,500 tons.

Establish the cap-and-trade regulatory framework. The environmental authority sets an aggregate emissions cap 20% below baseline: 48,400 tons, requiring total abatement of 12,100 tons. The authority must allocate 48,400 permits among the ten firms using one of three initial allocation rules: grandfathering (allocation proportional to baseline emissions), auction (firms bid for permits), or output-based allocation (permits per unit of production). Begin with grandfathering: each firm receives permits equal to 80% of baseline emissions.

Calculate the competitive equilibrium permit price and abatement allocation using the marginal abatement cost equalization condition. In equilibrium, all firms face the same permit price p*, and each firm chooses abatement quantity q_i* such that MAC_i(q_i*) = p*. The firm's optimization problem is to minimize total cost: TC_i = ∫₀^q_i MAC_i(x)dx + p* × (E_i - q_i - A_i), where E_i is baseline emissions, q_i is abatement, and A_i is the firm's permit allocation. Taking the first-order condition yields MAC_i(q_i*) = p*.

For the linear MAC specification, MAC_i(q_i) = a_i + b_i × q_i, setting this equal to p* and solving for abatement gives q_i* = (p* - a_i) / b_i. The market clearing condition requires that total abatement equals the mandated reduction: Σq_i* = 12,100 tons. Substitute the firm-specific abatement functions into this condition: Σ[(p* - a_i) / b_i] = 12,100. Solve for the equilibrium permit price: p* = [12,100 + Σ(a_i / b_i)] / Σ(1 / b_i).

Implement this calculation in spreadsheet format. Create a column for 1/b_i and compute the sum: Σ(1/b_i) = 100 + 125 + 66.67 + 83.33 + 111.11 + 90.91 + 142.86 + 100 + 200 + 166.67 = 1,186.55. Create a column for a_i/b_i and compute the sum: Σ(a_i/b_i) = 2,000 + 1,875 + 2,666.67 + 2,916.67 + 2,777.78 + 2,727.27 + 2,571.43 + 2,200 + 2,000 + 2,000 = 23,735.51. Calculate equilibrium permit price: p* = (12,100 + 23,735.51) / 1,186.55 = $30.18 per ton.

Calculate each firm's equilibrium abatement using q_i* = (p* - a_i) / b_i. Firm 1: (30.18 - 20) / 0.01 = 1,018 tons. Firm 2: (30.18 - 15) / 0.008 = 1,897.5 tons. Firm 3: (30.18 - 40) / 0.015 = -655.2 tons (negative indicates firm is a permit buyer, not abater beyond its allocation). Recalculate recognizing corner solutions: if calculated abatement is negative, firm abates zero and buys permits; if calculated abatement exceeds baseline emissions, firm abates entire baseline and sells all permits. Recompute with correct bounds and iterate until market clearing holds.

The equilibrium allocation reveals the cost-effectiveness property: firms with low marginal abatement costs (low a and b parameters) undertake proportionally more abatement, while firms with high abatement costs purchase permits and abate less. Compute total abatement cost as the sum of each firm's abatement cost integral: TC_i = ∫₀^q_i* (a_i + b_i × x)dx = a_i × q_i* + 0.5 × b_i × (q_i*)². Sum across all firms to obtain total system abatement cost.

Compare this cap-and-trade outcome against a uniform emission rate standard requiring each firm to reduce emissions by 20%. Calculate each firm's abatement cost under this command-and-control approach by setting q_i = 0.2 × E_i and computing the abatement cost integral. Sum across firms to obtain total cost under uniform regulation. The cost differential quantifies the efficiency gain from allowing trading: typically, tradable permits achieve the same environmental outcome at 30-50% lower total cost than uniform standards due to heterogeneous abatement cost exploitation.

Analyze distributional impacts by calculating each firm's net compliance cost: NC_i = abatement cost + permit purchases - permit sales revenue. Under grandfathered allocation, firms with low abatement costs become net permit sellers, earning revenue that partially or fully offsets their abatement costs. Firms with high abatement costs become net permit buyers, facing compliance costs equal to their abatement expenditure plus permit purchase costs. Contrast this with auction allocation where firms receive no free permits: net compliance cost equals abatement cost plus the full cost of permits needed to cover residual emissions. The auction generates government revenue equal to p* × cap, which can be recycled through lump-sum transfers or used to reduce distortionary taxes elsewhere in the economy.

## Exercise 2: Parameter Variation & Empirical Analysis

### Climate Damage Function Estimation and Optimal Carbon Tax Calibration

The optimal Pigouvian tax equals the marginal social damage of pollution: the present discounted value of climate and ecological harm caused by one additional metric ton of carbon emissions. This exercise estimates an empirical climate damage function using integrated assessment model outputs and observable climate-economy relationships. The practitioner will calibrate a damage function linking global mean temperature increase to GDP losses, discount future damages using alternative social discount rates, and compute the optimal carbon tax trajectory consistent with dynamic efficiency.

Download climate damage estimates from the Climate Impact Lab's Data-Driven Spatial Climate Impact Model or retrieve damage function parameters from meta-analyses of integrated assessment models (IAMs) such as DICE (Dynamic Integrated Climate-Economy), FUND (Framework for Uncertainty, Negotiation and Distribution), and PAGE (Policy Analysis of the Greenhouse Effect). The canonical damage function takes the quadratic form: D(T) = α × T + β × T², where T represents global mean surface temperature increase above pre-industrial baseline in degrees Celsius, and parameters α and β capture linear and convex damage escalation.

Calibrate damage function parameters using observed GDP-temperature relationships from econometric panel studies. Burke et al. (2015) estimate that a one-degree Celsius warming reduces global GDP by approximately 1.2% at the median, with damage accelerating nonlinearly at higher temperatures. Translate this into functional form by setting α = 0 (no linear term) and β = 1.2, implying D(T) = 1.2 × T², yielding 1.2% GDP loss at T = 1°C, 4.8% loss at T = 2°C, and 10.8% loss at T = 3°C. This specification matches the convex damage profile observed in agricultural productivity losses, sea level rise infrastructure damage, and heat-related mortality increases.

Link emissions to temperature using the carbon cycle and climate sensitivity parameters. The IPCC AR6 reports transient climate response to cumulative CO₂ emissions (TCRE) of approximately 0.45°C per 1,000 gigatons of cumulative carbon emissions. Convert to per-ton marginal impact: 0.45°C / 1,000 Gt = 0.45 × 10⁻³ °C per gigaton = 0.45 × 10⁻¹² °C per metric ton. This infinitesimal per-ton impact requires aggregation over the global emissions trajectory to compute marginal damage.

Construct the social cost of carbon (SCC) calculation framework. The SCC equals the present value of all future damages caused by one additional metric ton of CO₂ emitted today: SCC_t = Σ_{s=t}^{∞} [∂D_s / ∂E_t] × Y_s × (1 + ρ)^{-(s-t)}, where E_t is emissions at time t, D_s is damage in period s, Y_s is GDP in period s, and ρ is the social discount rate. The term ∂D_s / ∂E_t captures how emissions today alter future temperature and thus future damage.

Operationalize this formula in discrete time using a 300-year projection horizon. Create a time-series projection table with columns for Year, Global GDP, Temperature Increase, Damage Percentage, Absolute Damage, and Discounted Damage. Initialize Year 0 as 2024 with global GDP of $100 trillion and current temperature increase of 1.2°C above pre-industrial. Project GDP growth at 2% annually: Y_t = Y_0 × (1.02)^t. Project temperature increase under a baseline emissions pathway: assume continued emissions of 40 gigatons CO₂ annually, increasing temperature by 0.45°C per 1,000 Gt or 0.018°C per year.

Calculate annual damage using the quadratic damage function. At Year 0, T = 1.2°C yields damage D = 1.2 × (1.2)² = 1.73% of GDP, or $1.73 trillion. At Year 50, T = 2.1°C yields damage D = 1.2 × (2.1)² = 5.29% of GDP. Apply this calculation for each projection year. Compute the marginal damage increment attributable to one additional metric ton of emissions by calculating the temperature increase caused by that ton: ΔT = 0.45 × 10⁻¹² °C per ton. The marginal damage in year s is then: ∂D_s / ∂E_0 = 2 × β × T_s × ΔT × Y_s.

Discount future damages using alternative social discount rate specifications. The Ramsey formula decomposes the discount rate as ρ = δ + η × g, where δ is the pure rate of time preference, η is the elasticity of marginal utility of consumption, and g is the per-capita consumption growth rate. The Stern Review uses δ = 0.1%, η = 1, and g = 1.3%, yielding ρ = 1.4%. Nordhaus's DICE model uses δ = 1.5%, η = 1.45, and g = 2%, yielding ρ = 4.4%. Compute discounted damages under both discount rate scenarios to illustrate the profound sensitivity of SCC estimates to discounting assumptions.

Implement the SCC calculation in spreadsheet format. For each projection year s from 0 to 300, calculate: marginal damage MD_s = 2 × 1.2 × T_s × (0.45 × 10⁻¹²) × Y_s, discounted marginal damage DMD_s = MD_s / (1 + ρ)^s, and cumulative SCC = Σ DMD_s. Using ρ = 1.4%, the SCC typically reaches $200-300 per metric ton CO₂. Using ρ = 4.4%, the SCC falls to $40-60 per ton, reflecting the heavy discounting of long-term damages.

Translate the SCC into an optimal carbon tax trajectory. Static efficiency requires the carbon tax to equal the current-period SCC: τ_t = SCC_t. Dynamic efficiency additionally requires the tax to grow at the social discount rate adjusted for mitigation technology progress. If abatement costs decline due to renewable energy learning curves and energy efficiency improvements, the optimal tax path grows more slowly than the discount rate. Construct a 50-year optimal tax trajectory starting from the calculated SCC and growing at ρ - λ, where λ represents the annual percentage reduction in abatement costs due to technological progress (typically 1-2%).

Conduct sensitivity analysis on key parameters: damage function convexity (β), climate sensitivity (TCRE), discount rate (ρ), and GDP growth (g). Create a two-way sensitivity table varying β from 0.8 to 1.6 and ρ from 1% to 5%, calculating the resulting SCC matrix. This analysis reveals that uncertainty in the damage function shape and intergenerational discounting dominates the numerical uncertainty, spanning an SCC range from $20 to $400 per ton. Document this parameter sensitivity in policy communication to convey the appropriate epistemic humility regarding optimal tax levels.

### Environmental Kuznets Curve Estimation and Policy Regime Transitions

The Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) hypothesis posits an inverted-U relationship between per-capita income and environmental degradation: pollution initially rises with industrialization, peaks at middle-income levels, then declines as economies shift toward services and invest in environmental quality. This exercise estimates EKC relationships using cross-country panel data on income and emissions, tests structural break hypotheses corresponding to policy regime changes, and projects emissions trajectories under alternative development pathways.

Assemble a cross-country panel dataset linking GDP per capita to carbon emissions intensity and local air pollution concentrations. Download World Bank World Development Indicators data on GDP per capita (constant 2015 USD), CO₂ emissions per capita (metric tons), and PM2.5 air pollution (annual mean exposure in micrograms per cubic meter) for all countries from 1990 to 2023. Supplement with OECD data on sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxide emissions for developed economies. Organize data in long panel format with columns for Country, Year, GDP per capita, CO₂ per capita, PM2.5, and Policy Regime (coded as 0 for pre-policy period, 1 for post-policy implementation).

Estimate the baseline EKC specification using quadratic regression: E_it = α + β₁ × Y_it + β₂ × Y_it² + γ × X_it + μ_i + ε_it, where E_it is emissions for country i in year t, Y_it is GDP per capita, X_it represents control variables (urbanization rate, industrial share of GDP, energy intensity), μ_i is a country fixed effect, and ε_it is the error term. The EKC hypothesis predicts β₁ > 0 and β₂ < 0, implying an inverted-U curve. The turning point occurs at Y* = -β₁ / (2β₂), the income level at which emissions peak before declining.

Implement the estimation in a spreadsheet regression framework or statistical software. For spreadsheet execution, create a column for Y² (GDP per capita squared), then use Excel's Data Analysis Toolpak → Regression function with E as the dependent variable and Y, Y², and control variables as independent variables. Include country dummy variables to capture fixed effects. For panel data with many countries, export to R or Python for fixed-effects regression using the plm package or statsmodels library.

Execute the baseline regression for CO₂ emissions per capita. Restrict the sample to countries with complete data from 1990-2023. The estimated coefficients typically yield β₁ ≈ 0.0003 and β₂ ≈ -0.000000015, implying a turning point at Y* = 10,000 USD per capita. Interpret this result: countries with per-capita income below $10,000 experience rising emissions with economic growth, while countries above this threshold see emissions decline with further income growth. The turning point corresponds roughly to the transition from industrial to post-industrial economies.

Test for structural breaks corresponding to major environmental policy interventions. Consider the EU Emissions Trading System launched in 2005 as a regime change for European countries. Create an interaction term: Policy × Y, and re-estimate the specification including this interaction. The interaction coefficient captures whether the income-emissions relationship shifted after policy implementation. A negative interaction coefficient indicates that post-policy, emissions decline more steeply with income growth due to regulatory pressure and clean technology incentives.

Estimate separate EKC curves for local air pollutants versus global climate pollutants. Regress PM2.5 concentration on GDP per capita using the same quadratic specification. Local air pollution typically exhibits a sharper inverted-U with turning point around $5,000-8,000 per capita, reflecting earlier policy intervention due to immediate public health impacts and shorter-range damage. CO₂ emissions show flatter or less conclusive EKC patterns because climate damages are delayed, diffuse, and global, generating weaker political economy incentives for mitigation at the national level.

Project future emissions trajectories under alternative development scenarios. Using the estimated EKC coefficients, simulate emissions paths for rapidly developing countries (India, Indonesia, Vietnam) as their per-capita incomes grow from current levels toward the OECD average. Under a business-as-usual scenario without accelerated policy intervention, these countries will traverse the rising segment of the EKC, with emissions peaking in 2040-2050 before declining. Under an aggressive policy scenario implementing carbon pricing and renewable energy mandates immediately, the effective EKC shifts downward, reducing the peak emission level and accelerating the transition to declining emissions.

Quantify the policy-induced EKC shift by comparing projected cumulative emissions under the two scenarios. Integrate the area under the baseline EKC curve from current income to OECD-level income, yielding cumulative emissions without policy intervention. Repeat for the policy-shifted curve where the quadratic coefficient β₂ becomes more negative (steeper decline) and the turning point Y* occurs at lower income due to earlier policy adoption. The difference in cumulative emissions represents the mitigation benefit of early policy action, typically 20-40% reduction in cumulative CO₂ for developing countries implementing carbon pricing at middle-income stages rather than waiting for high-income status.

Conduct robustness checks on the EKC specification. Estimate cubic and logarithmic functional forms: E = α + β₁ × ln(Y) + β₂ × [ln(Y)]² and E = α + β₁ × Y + β₂ × Y² + β₃ × Y³. The cubic specification allows for N-shaped curves where emissions initially rise, decline, then potentially rise again at very high incomes. Empirically, cubic specifications rarely show significant third-order terms for CO₂, supporting the inverted-U hypothesis. For local pollutants, some studies find N-shaped curves reflecting re-industrialization or regulatory erosion in mature economies.

## Exercise 3: Stress Testing, Boundary Failure & Recovery Protocol

### General Equilibrium Carbon Tax Incidence and Welfare Analysis

Partial equilibrium analysis of environmental taxes ignores critical general equilibrium feedback effects: changes in relative prices alter factor demands, shifting the distribution of tax burdens across households and industries. This exercise constructs a computable general equilibrium (CGE) model to trace the economy-wide incidence of a carbon tax, accounting for input-output linkages, factor market adjustments, and heterogeneous household impacts. The practitioner will identify regressive distributional consequences, design compensatory transfer mechanisms, and evaluate the double-dividend hypothesis that environmental taxes can simultaneously improve environmental quality and reduce distortionary tax burdens elsewhere.

Begin by assembling a social accounting matrix (SAM) representing the circular flow of income in a stylized economy. The SAM organizes all economic transactions among industries, factors of production (capital and labor), households, government, and the rest of the world into a square matrix where rows represent receipts and columns represent expenditures. Download a published SAM for the United States from the GTAP (Global Trade Analysis Project) database or construct a simplified SAM using input-output tables from the Bureau of Economic Analysis and household income data from the Current Population Survey.

Structure a minimal SAM with six accounts: Energy sector, Non-energy sectors, Capital, Labor, Households, and Government. The Energy sector produces fossil fuel-based energy with high carbon intensity. Non-energy sectors aggregate all other production activities. Capital and Labor are primary factors earning rental and wage income. Households supply factors, purchase goods, and pay taxes. Government collects taxes and provides public goods. Populate the SAM cells: Energy sector purchases $200 billion in intermediate inputs from Non-energy sectors and pays $100 billion in capital income and $50 billion in labor income, producing $350 billion in gross output. Non-energy sectors purchase $50 billion from Energy, $800 billion in intra-sectoral inputs, pay $600 billion to Capital and $500 billion to Labor, totaling $1,950 billion gross output.

Households receive $700 billion in capital income and $550 billion in labor income, for $1,250 billion total income. They spend $300 billion on Energy consumption and $800 billion on Non-energy consumption, pay $100 billion in taxes, and save $50 billion. Government collects $100 billion in taxes and purchases $50 billion in Energy and $50 billion in Non-energy goods. Verify SAM balance: each row sum must equal its corresponding column sum, ensuring accounting consistency.

Calibrate a CGE model using the SAM as the benchmark equilibrium. Specify production functions for each sector using nested constant elasticity of substitution (CES) structures. The Energy sector combines Capital and Labor in a Cobb-Douglas top-level nest, then combines this value-added composite with intermediate inputs in a Leontief fixed-proportions structure. The Non-energy sector similarly combines factors and intermediates. Define production function parameters: capital share in Energy sector θ_K^E = 0.67, labor share θ_L^E = 0.33, intermediate input requirement a_E = 0.57 (dollars of intermediates per dollar of gross output).

Specify household utility function as a Cobb-Douglas aggregation over Energy and Non-energy consumption: U = E^α × N^(1-α), where α = 0.24 based on household expenditure shares. Households maximize utility subject to their income constraint: p_E × E + p_N × N = I, where p_E and p_N are prices and I is household income from factor earnings.

Implement the CGE model in a spreadsheet-based solver framework. Define endogenous variables: sectoral output quantities (Q_E, Q_N), factor prices (wage w, rental rate r), commodity prices (p_E, p_N), household consumption (E, N), and government revenue (G). Define exogenous parameters: production function shares, intermediate input requirements, household income, and tax rates. The model comprises a system of simultaneous equations: production functions equating marginal products to factor prices, market clearing conditions equating supply and demand in each goods and factor market, household optimization first-order conditions, and government budget balance.

Solve the benchmark equilibrium by setting up a square system of equations in Excel. Normalize the Non-energy price to p_N = 1 as the numeraire. Guess initial values for all endogenous variables and use Solver to find values satisfying all equations simultaneously. The Solver objective cell can be set to minimize the sum of squared deviations from market clearing conditions. Verify the solution replicates the SAM benchmark: calculated quantities and prices should generate transaction values matching the SAM cells.

Introduce a carbon tax by adding an ad valorem tax τ = 0.20 (20%) on Energy sector output, representing a $50 per ton carbon tax applied to fossil fuels with average carbon intensity of 0.25 tons CO₂ per dollar of output. The tax raises the consumer price of energy to p_E^new = (1 + τ) × p_E^pre, where p_E^pre is the pre-tax equilibrium price. Re-solve the CGE model under the tax regime by adjusting the Energy sector pricing equation to include the tax wedge and adding government revenue from the tax to the government budget constraint.

The new equilibrium reveals the tax incidence across factors and households. Energy sector output contracts due to higher consumer prices, reducing demand for capital and labor in that sector. If energy production is capital-intensive relative to the economy average (as specified in the model), capital bears a disproportionate share of the tax burden: the rental rate r falls more than the wage rate w declines. This occurs because capital cannot easily substitute away from the shrinking energy sector, suffering a factor market general equilibrium adjustment beyond the direct tax payment.

Calculate household welfare changes using the equivalent variation measure: the income change at pre-tax prices that generates the same utility change as the tax-induced price and income shifts. Compute pre-tax utility U⁰ using benchmark consumption levels, and post-tax utility U¹ using new equilibrium consumption. The equivalent variation EV = e(p⁰, U¹) - e(p⁰, U⁰), where e(p, U) is the expenditure function: the minimum expenditure needed to achieve utility U at prices p. For Cobb-Douglas utility, this calculation simplifies to direct comparison of real income changes.

Disaggregate household welfare impacts by income quintile. Low-income households spend a larger share of income on energy (heating, transportation) than high-income households, making the carbon tax regressive. Calculate the tax burden as a percentage of income for each quintile by multiplying the household's energy consumption share by the energy price increase. If low-income households spend 15% of income on energy and high-income households spend 8%, a 20% energy price increase imposes a 3% income burden on low-income households versus 1.6% on high-income households, a regressive incidence pattern.

Design a revenue-recycling mechanism to address distributional concerns. The carbon tax generates government revenue equal to τ × p_E × Q_E, approximately $60 billion in the benchmark calibration. Implement three alternative recycling schemes: (1) lump-sum rebate distributing revenue equally per household, (2) wage tax reduction decreasing labor income tax rates proportionally, and (3) capital tax reduction decreasing capital income tax rates. Resolve the CGE model under each recycling scheme by adjusting the relevant tax rates to balance the government budget.

Compare welfare outcomes across recycling schemes. Lump-sum rebate produces progressive incidence: low-income households receive the same absolute transfer as high-income households, representing a larger percentage of income. Combining the regressive carbon tax with progressive lump-sum rebate can achieve net progressive incidence, with low-income households experiencing net welfare gains. Wage tax recycling generates employment and efficiency gains by reducing labor market distortions, potentially yielding a "double dividend" of environmental improvement and reduced excess burden from taxation. Capital tax recycling most benefits capital owners but generates smaller efficiency gains due to lower labor supply elasticity.

Quantify the double dividend by comparing aggregate welfare under the carbon tax with recycling versus the benchmark without environmental taxation but maintaining government revenue through higher income taxes. Calculate the efficiency gain from substituting the carbon tax for distortionary income taxes using the marginal excess burden formula: MEB = λ × t × ε, where λ is the marginal cost of public funds (typically 1.2-1.4), t is the tax rate, and ε is the compensated elasticity. A carbon tax on a negative externality has negative excess burden (a benefit), while income taxes on labor have positive excess burden (a cost). The net efficiency gain is the sum of reduced income tax excess burden plus environmental benefit minus any carbon tax administration costs.

Conduct boundary stress tests by varying critical elasticity parameters. Re-solve the CGE model with low elasticity of substitution between Energy and Non-energy in household consumption (σ = 0.3 instead of base case σ = 1.0). This low-elasticity scenario implies households cannot easily substitute away from energy, magnifying the welfare cost of the carbon tax. Conversely, implement a high-elasticity case (σ = 2.0) representing long-run adjustments with electric vehicle adoption, building retrofits, and industrial process substitution. The high-elasticity scenario shows lower welfare costs and smaller regressivity because all households can adjust consumption patterns to avoid the tax.

Test model sensitivity to trade openness by introducing a rest-of-world sector. Allow the domestic economy to import Energy at world prices and export Non-energy goods. Implement a carbon border adjustment mechanism: domestic firms pay the carbon tax, but imports face an equivalent border charge based on embedded carbon content. Re-solve the model with trade to assess carbon leakage risk: without border adjustment, domestic energy-intensive production shifts abroad to avoid the tax, reducing environmental effectiveness and causing domestic job losses. Border adjustment mitigates leakage but faces implementation challenges in measuring embedded carbon in complex supply chains.

### Climate Policy Failure Analysis: Carbon Lock-in and Stranded Assets

Environmental policy implementation confronts path-dependent physical and institutional infrastructure creating resistance to rapid decarbonization. This exercise quantifies carbon lock-in from existing fossil fuel capital stock, values stranded asset exposure facing premature retirement under aggressive climate policy, and models political economy constraints on policy ambition arising from concentrated transition costs. The practitioner will evaluate compensatory mechanisms to overcome lock-in barriers and design transition policies minimizing adjustment costs while maintaining environmental integrity.

Begin by inventorying the existing fossil fuel capital stock and its implied future emissions under business-as-usual operation. Download the Global Coal Plant Tracker and Global Gas Plant Tracker databases from Global Energy Monitor, providing facility-level data on operational power plants, construction pipelines, and planned retirements. Extract key attributes: plant capacity (MW), commissioning year, expected operational lifetime (typically 40 years for coal, 30 years for gas), average capacity factor, and emissions intensity (metric tons CO₂ per MWh).

Construct a capital stock age profile by aggregating plants into vintage cohorts. Create a table with columns for Vintage Year, Capacity (GW), Average Age, Remaining Lifetime, Annual Generation (TWh), and Cumulative Committed Emissions. A plant commissioned in 2000 with 40-year lifetime and currently aged 24 years has 16 remaining years of operation. Calculate annual generation as Capacity × 8,760 hours × capacity factor. Typical coal plant capacity factors range from 50-70%; gas plants operate at 30-50% depending on baseload versus peaker role.

Compute committed emissions for each vintage cohort as Annual Generation × Emissions Intensity × Remaining Lifetime. Sum across all vintages to obtain total carbon lock-in: the cumulative CO₂ emissions from operating existing plants through their expected retirement dates. Global coal and gas infrastructure represents approximately 300 GtCO₂ of committed emissions, consuming roughly 30% of the remaining carbon budget for a 1.5°C warming limit.

Model accelerated retirement scenarios reducing carbon lock-in. Implement a policy mandating early retirement of coal plants reaching 25 years of age (instead of 40) and gas plants at 20 years (instead of 30). Recalculate remaining lifetimes for each vintage cohort under the accelerated schedule and recompute cumulative committed emissions. The accelerated retirement scenario reduces lock-in by 100-150 GtCO₂ but creates a stranded asset problem: plant owners and investors lose expected future returns from premature closure.

Quantify stranded asset value using discounted cash flow analysis. For each plant facing early retirement, calculate the net present value of lost future revenues. Assume a coal plant generates $50 million annual revenue from electricity sales, incurs $30 million annual operating costs, yielding $20 million annual operating profit. If the plant faces early retirement foregoing 15 years of operation, the stranded asset value equals the NPV of 15 years of $20 million profit: NPV = Σ_{t=1}^{15} [20 / (1 + r)^t], where r is the discount rate (typically 8% for merchant power). At 8% discount, NPV = $170 million per plant.

Aggregate stranded asset exposure by applying this calculation to all plants subject to early retirement. If 500 coal plants face 15-year premature retirement, total stranded value reaches $85 billion for coal generation alone. Add stranded exploration and development assets: oil and gas reserves that cannot be developed under carbon budget constraints. The International Energy Agency estimates $1 trillion in upstream oil and gas assets at risk of stranding in a 1.5°C scenario. Stranded asset risk concentrates in high-cost production regions (Canadian oil sands, Arctic reserves, deepwater fields) that become economically unviable at lower fossil fuel prices resulting from climate policy.

Model political economy constraints arising from stranded asset distribution. Map plant locations to electoral constituencies and tabulate employment exposure. Coal-dependent regions with concentrated employment and limited economic diversification face severe transition costs, generating political resistance to aggressive climate policy. Create a constituency-level database with columns for Region, Coal Employment, Share of Local Economy, Stranded Asset Exposure, and Political Representation Strength (seats in legislative body).

Identify high-constraint regions where political resistance will be strongest: areas with >10% of employment in fossil fuel industries, stranded assets exceeding 20% of regional GDP, and high representation in national legislature relative to population. These regions form veto coalitions capable of blocking climate legislation unless compensatory transition assistance is provided. The political constraint tightens in political systems requiring supermajority approval or coalition government formation where regional parties hold swing votes.

Design a just transition compensation mechanism addressing stranded asset losses and worker displacement. Allocate transition funds proportional to stranded asset exposure and employment impacts. A region with 10,000 coal jobs facing elimination and $1 billion in stranded assets might receive $500 million in transition assistance, funding retraining programs, early retirement benefits, and economic diversification investments. Calculate total compensation cost across all affected regions and evaluate funding sources: dedicated carbon tax revenue, general budget appropriations, or international climate finance transfers.

Evaluate policy robustness to transition speed variations. Implement three policy trajectories: slow phase-out (40-year coal retirement schedule matching existing lifetime), medium acceleration (25-year schedule), and rapid phase-out (15-year schedule). The slow phase-out minimizes stranded assets and political resistance but exceeds carbon budget limits, locking in dangerous temperature increases. The rapid phase-out achieves environmental targets but generates $200 billion in stranded assets and provokes political backlash potentially derailing the policy. The medium trajectory represents an optimal compromise, retiring plants at 25 years (just beyond financing amortization periods), reducing stranded value while achieving substantial emissions reductions.

Model technology substitution dynamics determining lock-in persistence. If renewable energy and storage costs decline rapidly due to learning-by-doing, fossil fuel plants become economically obsolete before their physical lifetime, reducing stranded asset value. Conversely, if renewable cost declines stall, fossil assets remain profitable longer, increasing lock-in persistence and raising required carbon tax levels to induce retirement. Construct a sensitivity analysis varying renewable cost decline rates from 5% to 15% annually and observing impacts on fossil asset profitability and optimal retirement timing.

Test boundary failure scenarios where carbon lock-in prevents climate stabilization. If existing fossil infrastructure operates through its full lifetime and new investment continues unabated, cumulative emissions exceed 600 GtCO₂ by 2050, implying warming exceeding 2°C even with subsequent zero emissions. This trajectory triggers severe climate damages including multi-meter sea level rise, widespread ecosystem collapse, and agricultural productivity declines reducing global GDP by 10-20%. Calculate the climate damage externality from allowing lock-in to persist and compare to the stranded asset compensation cost: climate damages exceed stranded asset losses by 10-100x, justifying aggressive policy intervention despite transition costs.

Identify institutional barriers constraining policy effectiveness beyond physical capital lock-in. Regulatory path dependence, utility rate-base structures rewarding capital investment, and fossil fuel subsidy programs embedded in tax codes create institutional inertia resisting decarbonization. Document specific institutional lock-in mechanisms: guaranteed rate-of-return regulation allowing utilities to earn returns on coal plant undepreciated capital even after retirement creates incentives to delay closures. Propose regulatory reforms breaking institutional lock-in: shift from rate-of-return to performance-based regulation rewarding emissions reductions rather than capital stock, eliminate fossil fuel subsidies redirecting funds to clean energy investment, and reform permitting processes accelerating renewable deployment.

## Operational Reference Guide & Diagnostic Checklist

### Policy Instrument Selection Decision Tree

Environmental policy effectiveness depends on matching instrument choice to the underlying market failure structure, information environment, and political economy constraints. Apply this decision framework systematically to select efficient policy instruments for specific environmental problems.

For pollution externalities with well-characterized marginal damage functions and low measurement costs, Pigouvian taxation dominates alternative instruments. When marginal damages rise steeply with pollution levels (convex damage functions), certainty over pollution quantity through cap-and-trade or quantity regulation becomes preferable to price instruments. Weitzman's price-versus-quantity analysis establishes that under uncertain abatement costs, price instruments (taxes) dominate when marginal damage is relatively flat, while quantity instruments (caps) dominate when marginal damage is steep.

When transaction costs of establishing property rights and enabling trading are low, cap-and-trade systems harness decentralized information about abatement costs, achieving least-cost allocation. High transaction costs, thin markets, or concerns about permit price volatility favor command-and-control standards over market mechanisms. Hybrid policies combining price and quantity elements (price collars, banking and borrowing provisions) can address multiple objectives simultaneously.

Information asymmetries between regulators and firms regarding abatement technologies favor flexible performance standards over prescriptive technology mandates. Performance standards specify emissions rates or efficiency levels while allowing firms to choose compliance technologies exploiting private information. Technology mandates sacrifice efficiency but may be justified when learning spillovers or coordination failures prevent optimal technology adoption.

### Empirical Validation Protocol

Environmental policy analysis demands rigorous empirical validation distinguishing causal impacts from spurious correlations. Implement these diagnostic procedures to verify analytical credibility.

When estimating pollution-income relationships, conduct unit root tests on time-series data to avoid spurious regression from trending variables. Environmental and economic variables typically exhibit strong trends; differencing or cointegration analysis separates long-run relationships from short-run fluctuations. For panel data, implement fixed effects regression controlling for time-invariant country characteristics or first-difference estimation removing country-specific trends.

For policy evaluation studies estimating treatment effects, validate parallel trends assumptions underlying difference-in-differences designs. Plot outcome variable trends for treated and control groups in pre-policy periods; parallel evolution supports causal identification. Conduct placebo tests assigning fake treatment dates to pre-policy periods; significant effects in placebo tests indicate violation of parallel trends and invalidity of the research design.

When calibrating integrated assessment models linking emissions to damages, conduct out-of-sample forecasting validation. Estimate the model using data through 2010, project emissions and impacts through 2020, then compare projections against realized outcomes. Large forecast errors indicate model misspecification or omitted structural breaks. Update damage functions and climate sensitivity parameters as observational evidence accumulates rather than anchoring to outdated calibrations.

For CGE models, validate benchmark replication by verifying that the solved equilibrium exactly reproduces SAM transaction values. Calibration errors appear as market clearing violations or income-expenditure imbalances. Conduct sensitivity analysis on key elasticity parameters by varying substitution elasticities across plausible ranges and observing impacts on policy conclusions. Robustness to parameter variation strengthens confidence in qualitative findings even when precise numerical estimates remain uncertain.

### Common Implementation Pathologies and Diagnostic Remedies

Carbon pricing implementation frequently encounters political economy obstacles and design flaws undermining effectiveness. This diagnostic guide identifies common failures and operational corrections.

Pathology: Weak carbon tax rates. Many implemented carbon taxes start at $5-15 per ton, far below social cost of carbon estimates and insufficient to induce behavioral change. Diagnosis: compare enacted tax rate against empirically estimated MAC curves and social cost of carbon. Remedy: implement automatic tax escalation schedules written into enabling legislation, indexed to inflation or programmed to rise 5-10% annually, reaching efficient levels ($50-100 per ton) within 10-15 years. Automatic escalation reduces political costs of rate increases and provides investment certainty.

Pathology: Revenue recycling that exacerbates regressivity. Using carbon tax revenue for general deficit reduction or unrelated spending programs concentrates burdens on low-income households. Diagnosis: calculate tax incidence by income decile accounting for direct energy price impacts and indirect price changes for carbon-intensive consumption goods. Remedy: dedicate a substantial share (40-60%) of revenue to progressive lump-sum rebates or targeted transfers to low-income households. The Alaska Permanent Fund model distributes oil revenue as equal per-capita dividends; carbon dividend schemes replicate this structure for climate policy.

Pathology: Emissions cap-and-trade systems with excessive permit allocation or price floors creating non-binding constraints. The EU ETS phase I allocated permits exceeding emissions, collapsing permit prices to zero. Diagnosis: compare aggregate permit supply against business-as-usual emissions projections; identify surplus allocation. Remedy: implement dynamic supply adjustment mechanisms (market stability reserves) withdrawing permits when prices fall below thresholds. Set initial caps 15-20% below baseline emissions ensuring meaningful scarcity.

Pathology: Border carbon adjustment implementation failures. Measuring embedded carbon in imports across complex supply chains faces administrative feasibility constraints. Diagnosis: assess data availability on foreign production processes and carbon intensity; identify high-uncertainty sectors. Remedy: implement border adjustment initially for carbon-intensive homogeneous commodities (steel, cement, aluminum) where production processes and emissions factors are well-characterized. Phase in coverage for complex manufactured goods gradually as measurement methodologies and international data sharing improve.

Pathology: Climate policy myopia neglecting long-lived capital stock decisions. Short policy horizons fail to account for infrastructure lock-in effects. Diagnosis: calculate committed emissions from approved fossil fuel infrastructure projects; compare against remaining carbon budgets. Remedy: implement integrated permitting review requiring climate impact assessment for long-lived capital projects. Deny permits for new fossil fuel infrastructure inconsistent with long-term emissions pathways. Establish clear phase-out schedules providing investment certainty while preventing lock-in.

### Stakeholder Communication Framework

Environmental policy analysis must translate technical findings into accessible formats for policymakers, industry stakeholders, and affected communities. Structure communication around decision-relevant information rather than academic completeness.

For legislative audiences, lead with bottom-line policy recommendations and headline numerical impacts (emissions reductions, economic costs, employment effects), followed by one-page executive summary, then detailed technical appendices. Use clear comparison tables contrasting alternative policy designs along key dimensions: environmental effectiveness, economic efficiency, distributional impacts, and administrative feasibility.

For industry stakeholders facing compliance obligations, emphasize operational flexibility provisions, transition timelines, and competitiveness protection mechanisms. Provide facility-level cost calculators allowing firms to input their emissions profiles and estimate compliance costs under alternative policy scenarios. Highlight opportunities for profit from innovation and early adoption rather than framing policy purely as regulatory burden.

For environmental advocacy organizations, present cumulative emissions trajectories and temperature implications linking policy stringency to climate stabilization goals. Quantify co-benefits from air quality improvements, public health gains, and ecosystem protection that reinforce climate mitigation rationale. Address environmental justice concerns by documenting disproportionate pollution exposure faced by low-income and minority communities and designing policies prioritizing burden reduction in these populations.

For affected workers and communities facing transition costs, lead with concrete adjustment assistance mechanisms: retraining program specifications, income support levels, and economic diversification investment plans. Provide transparent transition timelines and eligibility criteria for assistance. Facilitate community engagement in designing local transition strategies rather than imposing top-down plans. Document case studies of successful economic transitions (e.g., Appalachian coal communities diversifying into outdoor recreation and technology sectors) providing tangible models.

Environmental economics operationalizes the theoretical apparatus of welfare economics and public finance to address the defining policy challenge of the 21st century. Mastery requires integrating rigorous empirical methods, computational modeling capabilities, and institutional design expertise. The practitioner must simultaneously inhabit the roles of econometrician estimating damage functions, computational economist solving general equilibrium models, and policy architect designing politically feasible instruments balancing efficiency and equity. This laboratory guide has equipped the environmental policy specialist with the operational toolkit to execute this multifaceted mission.
