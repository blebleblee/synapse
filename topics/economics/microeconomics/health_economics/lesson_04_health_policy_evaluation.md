# Lesson 4: Health Policy Evaluation

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Health policy evaluation sits at the intersection of microeconomic theory, empirical causal inference, and real-world resource constraints. At its core, the discipline grapples with a deceptively simple question: given finite budgets and competing health interventions, how do we allocate resources to maximize societal welfare? This question is deceptively simple because its answer demands we first resolve fundamental tensions between efficiency and equity, between aggregate health gains and distributional fairness, and between what we can measure and what we actually care about.

The microeconomic foundations of health policy evaluation rest on consumer theory extended into the health domain. Just as a consumer chooses bundles of goods to maximize utility subject to a budget constraint, a health authority must choose a portfolio of interventions to maximize population health subject to fiscal constraints. But health differs categorically from ordinary consumption goods: health is not purely private (epidemiological spillovers exist), not fully reversible (mortality is irreversible), and heavily intertwined with fundamental questions of equity and human dignity. This asymmetry forces us to build evaluation frameworks that extend beyond the classical utilitarian calculus.

The canonical invariant underlying health policy evaluation is the production function relationship between health inputs and health outputs. In its most elementary form, we express this as:

$$H_i = f(X_i, \epsilon_i)$$

where $H_i$ represents the health status of individual $i$, $X_i$ is a vector of health-relevant inputs (medical treatments, preventive services, behavioral factors, environmental conditions), and $\epsilon_i$ captures unobserved heterogeneity and stochastic shocks. The challenge is not merely writing this equation—it is identifying the causal parameters of $f(\cdot)$ from observational data where treatment assignment is typically non-random, where individuals self-select into interventions based on unobserved severity, and where counterfactual outcomes remain fundamentally unobservable.

The second foundational invariant concerns the measurement of health itself. Health is inherently multidimensional: survival, functional capacity, pain, psychological well-being, and social participation all constitute legitimate dimensions of health. Yet policy decisions require a scalar summary measure to enable comparison across interventions. This measurement problem gives rise to the quality-adjusted life year (QALY), the disability-adjusted life year (DALY), and related constructs. A QALY represents one year of life lived in perfect health, with years lived in states of reduced health weighted by quality weights ranging from 0 (death) to 1 (perfect health). An intervention extending life by one year in a health state valued at 0.7 generates 0.7 QALYs. This is not merely an accounting convenience—it encodes specific ethical assumptions about how we value different health states and whether we view health gains as additive across populations.

The third invariant is the willingness-to-pay (WTP) framework, which bridges health outcomes back to economic value. In health economics, WTP represents the maximum amount an individual (or society) is willing to pay to secure a health improvement. This concept connects directly to revealed preference theory: if an individual purchases a health intervention at price $p$, their willingness-to-pay must be at least $p$. For non-market health goods—like improved air quality or reduced disease incidence—we must infer WTP indirectly through stated preference methods, hedonic pricing models, or revealed preference from related markets. The willingness-to-pay threshold, often denoted $\lambda$, represents the policy ceiling: interventions with cost-effectiveness below the threshold (measured in dollars per QALY gained) are deemed cost-effective and candidates for funding, while those exceeding the threshold face rejection or restricted access. In the United Kingdom, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) employs a willingness-to-pay threshold of approximately £20,000–£30,000 per QALY, while in the United States, thresholds range from $50,000 to $200,000 per QALY depending on the decision-maker and context.

The fourth invariant addresses the equity-efficiency frontier. Standard cost-effectiveness analysis ranks interventions purely by their efficiency—the health gained per dollar spent—without explicit consideration of how health gains distribute across populations. But two interventions generating identical aggregate health at identical cost may distribute gains vastly differently: one might benefit the severely ill while the other benefits the already-healthy; one might narrow health disparities while the other widens them. This tension between aggregate efficiency (maximizing total health) and distributional equity (ensuring fair allocation across populations) constitutes the equity-efficiency tradeoff. Microeconomic theory provides tools for characterizing this tradeoff—through social welfare functions that weight health gains differently depending on the recipient, through constrained optimization that incorporates equity constraints alongside efficiency objectives, and through explicit modeling of societal preferences over distributional outcomes.

The fifth invariant concerns causal identification in observational health data. The gold standard for establishing causation is the randomized controlled trial (RCT), where random assignment of individuals to treatment and control groups ensures that treated and untreated populations are balanced on both observed and unobserved confounders. But RCTs are often infeasible due to cost, ethical constraints, or slow deployment timelines. Health economists therefore rely on quasi-experimental methods: instrumental variables that exploit natural variation in treatment exposure uncorrelated with confounders, regression discontinuity designs that leverage sharp eligibility thresholds, difference-in-differences estimators that compare treated and control groups before and after policy changes, and matching estimators that condition on observed confounders to create comparable treatment and control groups. Each method rests on distinct identifying assumptions; understanding these assumptions and their plausibility in context is central to credible policy evaluation.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The operational structure of health policy evaluation unfolds across three interconnected layers: measurement and data architecture, causal identification strategies, and decision-making optimization.

**The Measurement and Data Architecture Layer** begins with defining and operationalizing health outcomes. Consider a pragmatic example: evaluating a new antihypertensive therapy. The raw data inputs include baseline blood pressure measurements, medication adherence records (obtained through pharmacy refill data or patient surveys), clinical event data (myocardial infarction, stroke, hospitalization), mortality records, and quality-of-life survey responses. These heterogeneous data streams must be integrated into a coherent longitudinal dataset where each individual is tracked over time, linked across sources through encryption-preserved identifiers, and structured to enable both outcome measurement and confounder adjustment.

The construction of QALYs from raw health data exemplifies this architecture. Suppose we observe that a treatment prevents a stroke in an individual who would otherwise have lived 15 years post-stroke in a health state valued at 0.6. The treatment effect on QALYs is calculated as:

$$\Delta \text{QALY} = (1 \times 15) - (0.6 \times 15) = 0.4 \times 15 = 6 \text{ QALYs}$$

In practice, this requires: first, estimating survival curves for treated and untreated individuals; second, obtaining health state valuations (often from generic preference-based instruments like the EQ-5D or SF-6D, which map self-reported health descriptions to utility weights); third, integrating these components into lifetime QALY calculations. When follow-up data is incomplete due to censoring (individuals lost to follow-up, still alive at study end), we employ Kaplan-Meier estimation to construct non-parametric survival curves, or parametric survival models (Weibull, log-normal, generalised gamma) that allow extrapolation beyond observed data.

The cost data architecture parallels health outcome measurement. Direct medical costs include treatment acquisition, administration, monitoring, and management of adverse events. A comprehensive economic evaluation may also include direct non-medical costs (transportation, caregiver time) and productivity losses (foregone earnings due to disability or early mortality). The temporal dimension matters critically: a treatment preventing early mortality might incur prevention costs today but generate savings tomorrow through averted future medical spending. We discount future costs and benefits to present value using a discount rate $r$, typically 3% annually, yielding:

$$\text{Present Value} = \sum_{t=0}^{T} \frac{C_t + B_t}{(1+r)^t}$$

where $C_t$ is net cost in year $t$, $B_t$ is net benefit, and $T$ is the analysis horizon. Sensitivity analysis around the discount rate (often testing 1.5%, 3%, and 5%) is standard practice, as discount rate choice can substantially alter cost-effectiveness conclusions for interventions with long-term benefits.

**The Causal Identification Layer** operationalizes methods for estimating treatment effects from observational data. Consider a health policy evaluation comparing health outcomes between individuals who received an intensive chronic disease management intervention versus usual care. A naive comparison of average outcomes between the two groups would likely yield biased estimates because individuals who enrolled in intensive management may differ systematically from those who did not: they might have higher baseline disease severity (selection on observables), greater health consciousness (selection on unobservables), or access to enabling resources like transportation (confounding).

The regression adjustment approach mitigates observable confounding by estimating a treatment effect conditional on observed covariates:

$$Y_i = \alpha + \beta T_i + \gamma X_i + \epsilon_i$$

where $Y_i$ is the health outcome for individual $i$, $T_i$ is treatment assignment (0 or 1), $X_i$ is a vector of observed confounders, and $\beta$ estimates the average treatment effect conditional on $X_i$. This approach assumes that all confounding factors are measured and included in $X_i$—an assumption often unrealistic in health data where important determinants of both treatment receipt and outcomes (like baseline health motivation or access to informal care networks) remain unmeasured.

The instrumental variables approach addresses unmeasured confounding by leveraging variation in treatment exposure that is correlated with treatment but uncorrelated with unobserved confounders. A canonical health economics example involves policy eligibility thresholds: suppose a prevention program targets individuals over age 65, and age eligibility is a discontinuous policy feature. Age itself influences health outcomes directly (confounding), but the discontinuity at the age threshold creates exogenous variation in treatment exposure that is uncorrelated with health motivation or other unmeasured factors. The two-stage least squares (2SLS) estimator proceeds as:

**Stage 1:** Estimate the first-stage relationship between the instrument and treatment:
$$T_i = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 Z_i + \nu_i$$

**Stage 2:** Use fitted treatment values $\hat{T}_i$ to estimate the treatment effect:
$$Y_i = \alpha + \beta \hat{T}_i + \gamma X_i + \epsilon_i$$

The validity of this approach hinges critically on the instrumental variable satisfying two conditions: relevance (the instrument must predict treatment assignment strongly) and exogeneity (the instrument must be uncorrelated with unobserved determinants of outcomes). Testing relevance is straightforward—the first-stage F-statistic should exceed 10 to avoid weak instrument bias. Testing exogeneity is fundamentally challenging because it concerns unobserved factors; instead, we conduct sensitivity analyses exploring how conclusions change under alternative assumptions about the degree of unobserved confounding.

The regression discontinuity design formalizes the intuition that sharp policy eligibility thresholds create quasi-experimental variation. Suppose a transportation voucher program for outpatient visits covers individuals with household income below 200% of the federal poverty line. Individual income is continuous and influenced by unmeasured factors, but the threshold creates a discontinuity in treatment receipt at the eligibility cutoff. The regression discontinuity estimator compares outcomes for individuals just below and just above the threshold:

$$\text{Treatment Effect} = \lim_{x \to c^-} E[Y_i | X_i = x] - \lim_{x \to c^+} E[Y_i | X_i = x]$$

where $c$ is the eligibility threshold and $x$ is the running variable (income). Individuals just below and above the threshold differ minimally in their characteristics other than treatment exposure, making them a credible comparison. This approach requires large sample sizes near the threshold and careful attention to the functional form—should we use linear, polynomial, or nonparametric specifications for the relationship between outcomes and the running variable?

The difference-in-differences approach exploits temporal policy changes. Suppose a state implements a new prescription drug benefit for low-income adults in year 2015. We compare health outcomes for eligible individuals (those in states and years covered by the policy) versus control individuals (those in states and years not covered), before and after policy implementation:

$$Y_{ist} = \alpha + \beta \text{Treated}_{is} + \gamma \text{Post}_{st} + \delta (\text{Treated}_{is} \times \text{Post}_{st}) + X_{ist}\phi + \epsilon_{ist}$$

where $i$ indexes individuals, $s$ indexes state, $t$ indexes time period. The coefficient $\delta$ estimates the policy effect, capturing the differential change in outcomes for treated versus control groups after policy implementation. The identifying assumption is parallel trends: absent the policy, treated and control groups would have experienced similar outcome trajectories. This assumption is testable (though not definitively provable) by examining pre-policy trends; if treated and control groups diverged before the policy, parallel trends is questionable.

**The Decision Optimization Layer** synthesizes causal estimates and cost data into policy recommendations. The cost-effectiveness threshold represents the policy decision boundary. If an intervention generates health outcomes (measured in QALYs or other units) at a cost below the willingness-to-pay threshold, standard cost-effectiveness analysis recommends adoption. Formally, the net monetary benefit (NMB) framework reformulates the decision:

$$\text{NMB} = \lambda \times \Delta \text{Health} - \Delta \text{Cost}$$

where $\lambda$ is the willingness-to-pay threshold. Interventions with positive NMB are cost-effective candidates for funding. The NMB framework elegantly accommodates uncertainty: by constructing confidence intervals around NMB estimates, we can characterize the probability that an intervention is cost-effective given statistical uncertainty in the underlying parameters.

The cost-effectiveness acceptability curve (CEAC) visualizes this uncertainty across different willingness-to-pay thresholds. The CEAC plots, for each possible threshold value, the proportion of bootstrap replicates of the underlying analysis where the intervention achieves positive net monetary benefit. An intervention with a high CEAC at the policy's implicit willingness-to-pay threshold provides confidence that the recommendation is robust to parameter uncertainty.

When budgets are insufficient to fund all cost-effective interventions, budget-constrained optimization applies. The problem becomes:

$$\max_{q} \sum_j \Delta \text{Health}_j \times q_j$$

subject to:

$$\sum_j \Delta \text{Cost}_j \times q_j \leq B$$

where $q_j \in \{0, 1\}$ indicates whether intervention $j$ is funded (assuming binary funding decisions for simplicity), and $B$ is the budget constraint. This is a constrained optimization problem solvable through integer programming. The solution ranks interventions by their cost-effectiveness ratio and funds them in descending order of efficiency until the budget is exhausted. Interventions at the budget margin may be partially funded.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

The practical implementation of health policy evaluation confronts several critical failure modes that can corrupt analysis and mislead policy.

**Outcome Measurement Gaming and Metric Manipulation** represents the first class of failure. When policy decisions explicitly hinge on cost-effectiveness thresholds, incentives emerge to design studies that make interventions appear more favorable. Consider a pharmaceutical manufacturer evaluating a new treatment for hypertension. The manufacturer controls study design choices: the comparator (comparing against placebo looks better than comparing against existing therapy), the patient population (including sicker patients who experience larger absolute health gains), the outcome measurement horizon (longer follow-up periods generate larger cumulative benefits), and the valuation of health states (hiring preference-elicitation firms that generate high QALY weights for the relevant health states). Each choice is individually defensible but collectively biases the cost-effectiveness estimate toward favorable conclusions.

Mitigating this failure requires institutional structures: independent health technology assessment bodies (like NICE or Germany's Institut für Qualität und Wirtschaftlichkeit im Gesundheitswesen) that apply consistent methodology across evaluations, perform critical appraisals of studies submitted by manufacturers, and incorporate sensitivity analyses that test how conclusions change under alternative plausible assumptions. Transparency requirements—publishing analysis plans prospectively and registered analyses before results are known—reduce the degrees of freedom available for cherry-picking favorable specifications. Meta-analyses that synthesize evidence across multiple independently-conducted studies provide a reality check against individual study-level manipulation.

**Unobserved Confounding and Internal Validity Threats** constitute the second failure class. Even sophisticated causal identification strategies rest on assumptions about unobserved factors. An instrumental variables estimate of the treatment effect is valid only if the instrument satisfies exogeneity—a property that cannot be tested directly in finite samples. Consider a policy evaluation comparing health outcomes between individuals who live in counties with high versus low density of primary care clinics. The instrumental variable strategy might leverage historical variation in clinic siting (clinics were built decades ago based on population distributions that no longer persist). But suppose unobserved factors—like the health culture or social cohesion in the county—influence both clinic density and health outcomes independently of clinic access. Then the instrumental variable violates exogeneity, and estimates will be biased.

Addressing this failure requires transparent specification of assumptions and sensitivity analyses exploring how conclusions change under alternative confounding structures. Rotnitzky-VanderWeele bounds formalizes this by calculating how large unobserved confounding would need to be to reverse policy conclusions. If sensitivity analyses reveal that trivial amounts of unobserved confounding would alter conclusions, the estimate provides weak evidence for policy. Conversely, if conclusions are robust to substantial hypothetical confounding, confidence is warranted.

**Equity Blindness and Distributional Neglect** represents a third critical failure. Standard cost-effectiveness analysis ignores how health gains distribute across populations. An intervention might be highly cost-effective in aggregate but concentrate benefits among already-wealthy populations while imposing costs on the disadvantaged. Consider a preventive health program requiring internet access and health literacy: cost-effectiveness analysis might rank it highly because it prevents expensive downstream medical events, but the program's benefits accrue disproportionately to educated, digitally-connected populations while administrative costs are borne equally across all taxpayers.

Addressing equity requires extending the analysis framework to incorporate explicit distributional weights. The generalized cost-effectiveness approach weights health gains based on the recipient's baseline health and socioeconomic status:

$$\text{Social Welfare} = \sum_i w_i \times \Delta \text{QALY}_i - \Delta \text{Cost}_i$$

where $w_i$ represents the social weight assigned to individual $i$'s health gain. A uniform utilitarian approach sets $w_i = 1$ for all individuals; egalitarian approaches increase $w_i$ for disadvantaged populations, creating preference for interventions that narrow health disparities. The key operational challenge is specifying these weights transparently and obtaining democratic legitimacy for the distributional judgments they encode. Without explicit societal deliberation, analysts risk imposing distributional preferences without democratic authorization.

**Discount Rate Sensitivity and Long-Term Bias** constitutes the fourth failure mode. Standard practice discounts future health and costs at 3% annually, but this assumption dramatically favors interventions with immediate benefits over those with benefits concentrated in the distant future. Consider prevention programs targeting children: benefits accrue over 60+ year horizons, and at 3% annual discounting, benefits occurring 50 years in the future are worth only 23% of their nominal value. Small changes in the discount rate produce large changes in conclusions. At 5% discounting, that 50-year benefit is worth only 9% of nominal value. Conversely, interventions treating existing disease generate immediate outcomes that are unaffected by discounting assumptions.

Mitigating this failure requires sensitivity analyses across plausible discount rates (typically 1.5%, 3%, and 5% annually), transparent reporting of how conclusions change, and explicit societal deliberation about appropriate discount rates for health (distinct from financial discounting). Some health economists argue for lower discount rates for health than for monetary costs, reflecting uncertainty about future health preferences and concern for intergenerational equity. The United Kingdom's recent shift toward lower discount rates (1.5% for health outcomes versus 3% for costs) reflects this intuition. When conclusions are highly sensitive to discount rate assumptions, this uncertainty should inform policy rather than being suppressed.

**Extrapolation Beyond Observed Data and Structural Breaks** represents the fifth failure mode. Many health policy evaluations extrapolate benefits estimated over short study periods (2-5 years of follow-up) to lifetime horizons (60-80 years). This extrapolation demands strong assumptions about the persistence of treatment effects, the stability of cost structures, and the absence of structural breaks due to technology change or disease progression.
