# Lesson 3: Provider Behavior in Healthcare Supply

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Healthcare markets defy the classical competitive ideal in fundamental ways that trace back to information asymmetry, agency relationships, and institutional constraints on price discovery. When a patient consults a physician, they delegate not merely treatment execution but the very determination of need itself. The physician simultaneously diagnoses the condition and prescribes the remedy, holding both epistemic authority and supply control. This dual role creates an agency problem distinct from standard consumer markets: the agent who advises consumption also profits from its volume, and the principal lacks the specialized knowledge required to audit recommendations independently.

The neoclassical model of producer behavior assumes firms maximize profit subject to exogenous demand and transparent pricing. Healthcare providers operate under a different regime. Demand curves are not exogenous but partially endogenous to provider recommendations. Prices are often administered rather than market-clearing, determined by third-party insurers, government fee schedules, or negotiated contracts that obscure the marginal cost-price relationship patients would face in cash markets. Quality is multidimensional, imperfectly observable ex ante, and entangled with reputation mechanisms that evolve slowly relative to treatment cycles. These frictions transform provider behavior from passive price-taking to active demand shaping, quality signaling, and strategic technology adoption.

At the foundation lies the concept of **supplier-induced demand** (SID), a phenomenon where providers exploit their informational advantage to shift patient utilization beyond the welfare-maximizing level. The theoretical framework originates in McGuire's agency model, where physician utility depends on both income and some ethical constraint against over-treatment. Formally, let the physician maximize $U(I, \theta)$ where $I$ denotes income and $\theta$ represents a professional norm parameter penalizing deviation from clinically appropriate care. Income derives from the volume and intensity of services: $I = \sum_i p_i q_i$ where $p_i$ is the fee for service $i$ and $q_i$ is quantity provided. The physician faces a trade-off: increasing $q_i$ raises income but may violate $\theta$ if $q_i$ exceeds the patient's clinical optimum $q_i^*$.

The SID hypothesis predicts that when physician density increases in a market—holding patient morbidity constant—each physician faces a smaller patient pool and responds by recommending more services per patient to maintain target income. Empirical tests of SID examine whether per-capita utilization rises with provider density after controlling for demand-side factors. Early cross-sectional studies by Fuchs (1978) found positive correlations between surgeon density and surgery rates, but identification remained contentious because unobserved patient demand shifters might drive both physician entry and utilization. Subsequent natural experiments leveraging exogenous shocks to physician supply—military base closures, medical school openings, immigration policy changes—have produced mixed evidence. Gruber and Owings (1996) documented that obstetricians responded to the secular decline in fertility rates during the 1970s-1980s by increasing cesarean section rates, consistent with target income models. Conversely, Dranove and Wehner (1994) found that surgeons in markets with falling demand reduced quantity but did not fully offset revenue losses, suggesting ethical constraints bind but imperfectly.

The informational asymmetry underlying SID operates through several channels. **Diagnostic discretion** allows physicians to frame marginal cases as requiring intervention. A borderline cardiac stress test can be read as "equivocal, suggesting further evaluation" versus "normal with no action needed." **Treatment intensity variation** permits substitution toward higher-reimbursed modalities when multiple options hold comparable efficacy. **Referral networks** enable internists to steer patients toward specialists with whom they have cross-referral arrangements, sustaining utilization volumes across the network. These mechanisms are constrained by malpractice liability, peer review, and professional norms, but the constraint binds less tightly when fee-for-service payment rewards volume and when patients lack second-opinion access or medical literacy to challenge recommendations.

Reimbursement structure fundamentally alters the incentive landscape. **Fee-for-service** (FFS) payment compensates each discrete service act, creating linear returns to volume. Marginal revenue equals the fee schedule amount $p_i$, while marginal cost to the provider includes time, materials, and malpractice exposure. Providers maximize profit where $MR = p_i = MC_i$, but because patients face minimal out-of-pocket cost under insurance, demand is highly inelastic to the provider's cost. The physician can thus recommend services up to the point where their personal marginal disutility (time, ethical constraint) equals the fee, often exceeding the socially optimal level where marginal benefit to the patient equals social marginal cost. The RAND Health Insurance Experiment (1974-1982) demonstrated that patient cost-sharing significantly reduces utilization, confirming that moral hazard on both patient and provider sides inflates consumption under FFS regimes.

**Capitation** payment inverts the incentive by paying a fixed amount per patient per period regardless of service volume. Now the provider's profit function becomes $\pi = N \cdot C - \sum_i MC_i q_i$ where $N$ is the enrolled patient count, $C$ is the capitation rate, and $MC_i$ are the marginal costs of services actually delivered. Minimizing costs aligns with profit maximization, creating incentives to reduce utilization. The welfare implications depend critically on whether utilization cuts come from eliminating low-value care or denying beneficial treatments. Capitation combined with quality monitoring can achieve efficient rationing, but without oversight it risks under-provision. Empirical studies of Medicaid managed care transitions in the 1990s found that capitation reduced specialist referrals and hospitalizations but had ambiguous effects on health outcomes, varying with the stringency of quality safeguards and the baseline appropriateness of care.

Between these poles lies **bundled payment**, which compensates a fixed amount for an entire episode of care—say, a hip replacement including surgery, hospitalization, and 90-day post-acute services. Bundling transfers utilization risk to the provider but maintains volume incentives for attracting patients. Medicare's Bundled Payments for Care Improvement (BPCI) initiative launched in 2013 aimed to reduce spending on high-variation episodes. Early results showed modest savings driven primarily by post-acute care substitution—discharging patients to home health rather than skilled nursing facilities—rather than surgical efficiency gains. The key theoretical insight is that bundling internalizes complementarities and substitutes within the episode, encouraging providers to minimize total episode cost rather than maximizing individual service counts, but it does not eliminate incentives to attract marginal patients or select healthier enrollees.

Provider competition in healthcare markets diverges sharply from the Bertrand price competition model because patients rarely observe prices ex ante, and insurance shields them from marginal cost at the point of service. Instead, competition occurs along **quality dimensions**: outcomes, patient experience, amenities, and wait times. Reputation serves as the signaling mechanism, but reputation capital accumulates slowly and imperfectly tracks true quality due to small sample sizes at the individual physician level and the difficulty patients face in disentangling skill from case complexity. Hospitals compete for patients and, more strategically, for physicians who control patient flow through referrals and admitting privileges.

The structure of hospital competition depends on geographic market concentration and regulatory entry barriers. Certificate-of-need (CON) laws in many U.S. states require regulatory approval for facility expansion or new capital investments, ostensibly to prevent wasteful duplication but effectively protecting incumbents from entry. In unconcentrated markets without CON, competition may manifest as a **medical arms race** where hospitals over-invest in advanced technology and amenities to attract physicians and patients, even when the incremental clinical benefit does not justify the capital cost. Dranove, Simon, and White (2002) documented that hospitals in more competitive markets adopted MRI scanners earlier and at lower patient volumes than monopolistic hospitals, consistent with strategic preemption and quality signaling rather than efficient capacity planning.

However, hospital competition does not universally improve welfare. When hospitals compete for insured patients but cannot price discriminate, they may compete by offering excessive amenities that raise costs without commensurate health benefits. Gaynor, Ho, and Town (2015) synthesized the empirical literature and concluded that hospital competition improves quality in administered-price settings (e.g., Medicare's prospective payment system where prices are fixed) but can raise prices in negotiated-price environments where hospitals bargain with insurers. The key distinction is whether price or quality serves as the margin of competition: fixed prices force competition on quality, while negotiated prices allow hospitals with market power to extract higher reimbursement without quality improvement.

Physician agency extends beyond individual volume decisions to include **referral behavior** and **practice style diffusion**. Physicians embedded in group practices or accountable care organizations face peer monitoring and shared financial risk, which can constrain SID but also enables coordinated strategies to maximize collective revenue. The geographic variation in Medicare spending documented by the Dartmouth Atlas—where per-beneficiary spending varies two-fold across hospital referral regions after adjusting for age, sex, and race—reflects persistent differences in practice style intensity that cannot be explained by patient preferences or health outcomes. Molitor (2018) used physician moves across regions to decompose this variation into place effects (local practice norms) versus physician effects (individual practice patterns), finding that place effects dominate: physicians adopt the local practice style within several years of relocation, suggesting that peer influence and institutional context shape treatment decisions more than individual training or preferences.

Technology adoption in healthcare exhibits distinct dynamics driven by reimbursement policy, legal liability, and professional prestige. New medical technologies often diffuse rapidly despite weak evidence of cost-effectiveness because payment systems reward utilization of novel modalities before long-term efficacy data accumulate. **Procedure-based specialties** (cardiology, orthopedic surgery, gastroenterology) have particularly strong incentives to adopt technologies that justify higher-intensity billing. The proliferation of cardiac stents in the 2000s exemplifies this pattern: despite randomized trials showing that elective stenting for stable angina produced no mortality benefit over medical management, stent volumes surged because reimbursement for percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) far exceeded medical therapy payments. Chan et al. (2014) found that cardiologists with ownership stakes in ambulatory surgery centers performed twice as many stent procedures as hospital-employed peers, illustrating how financial integration amplifies volume incentives.

Defensive medicine—the provision of services primarily to reduce malpractice liability rather than improve patient outcomes—represents another supply-side distortion. Kessler and McClellan (1996) exploited state-level tort reform variation and found that malpractice liability reductions decreased hospital spending by 5-9 percent for heart attack and ischemic heart disease patients without adverse mortality effects, suggesting that a meaningful fraction of baseline spending served legal rather than clinical purposes. Imaging for low-back pain, a condition where early MRI does not improve outcomes but generates lucrative findings, increased sharply during periods when malpractice premiums rose, consistent with physicians ordering tests to document thoroughness rather than to guide treatment.

Quality competition among providers faces measurement and attribution challenges that limit its efficacy. Outcomes depend on patient case mix, which providers have incentives to manipulate through **patient selection**—preferentially treating healthier, more compliant patients whose outcomes will appear superior. Risk adjustment attempts to level the playing field by controlling for observable patient characteristics, but unobserved heterogeneity (patient motivation, social support, health literacy) remains, and providers can game the system by **upcoding**—documenting diagnoses that inflate risk scores without changing care. Medicare Advantage plans increased their risk scores 4.9 percentage points faster than fee-for-service Medicare beneficiaries after the program's 2004 expansion, with much of the growth attributed to diagnostic coding intensity rather than true health deterioration (Geruso and Layton 2020).

Hospital quality report cards aim to make outcomes transparent, enabling patients to select high-performers and creating competitive pressure for improvement. However, empirical evidence on their effectiveness is mixed. Dranove et al. (2003) found that New York's cardiac surgery report cards induced hospitals to decline high-risk patients, reducing access for the sickest without improving aggregate health outcomes—a manifestation of the multitasking problem where agents optimize the measured dimension at the expense of unmeasured dimensions. More recent studies leveraging Medicare's Hospital Compare public reporting find modest improvements in reported measures but little spillover to unreported dimensions, consistent with narrow teaching-to-the-test responses rather than broad quality investment.

The distinction between **horizontal integration** (mergers among hospitals) and **vertical integration** (hospitals acquiring physician practices) generates different competitive effects. Horizontal integration increases market concentration, enabling hospitals to negotiate higher prices with insurers without necessarily improving quality or efficiency. Meta-analyses by Gaynor and Town (2012) conclude that hospital mergers in concentrated markets raise prices by 20-40 percent on average, with minimal evidence of offsetting quality gains or cost efficiencies. Vertical integration, by contrast, aims to align incentives between hospitals and physicians, facilitating care coordination and enabling bundled payment models. Yet empirical studies find that physician-hospital integration often raises spending due to **site-of-service payment differentials**: insurers pay hospitals substantially more than freestanding practices for the same outpatient services, so acquired practices convert to hospital outpatient departments and bill at hospital rates (Neprash et al. 2015).

The organizational form of medical practice influences provider behavior through ownership incentives, scale economies, and monitoring capacity. **Solo practitioners** maximize personal income but face full residual claimancy on both profits and malpractice risk, creating sharp volume incentives tempered by reputational concerns. **Group practices** introduce profit-sharing and peer monitoring, which can reduce SID by socializing the costs of aggressive practice styles but can also enable collusion to maintain high utilization norms. **Hospital employment** converts physicians to salary or productivity-adjusted compensation, reducing direct volume incentives but introducing bureaucratic performance metrics that may emphasize throughput and revenue targets. The shift from solo to employed practice over the past two decades—60 percent of U.S. physicians are now employed rather than practice owners—reflects both market concentration by hospital systems and physician preferences for salaried employment with predictable hours and administrative support.

Nonprofit hospitals, which constitute 60 percent of U.S. community hospitals, face a nondistribution constraint prohibiting equity ownership and dividend payments, yet they operate in revenue-maximizing fashion similar to for-profits. The theoretical rationale for nonprofit dominance in healthcare, articulated by Arrow (1963) and Hansmann (1980), centers on contract failure and trust: patients unable to verify quality ex ante may trust nonprofits more because the nondistribution constraint reduces incentives to sacrifice quality for profit. Empirical tests of this hypothesis yield weak support. Duggan (2000) found that nonprofit hospitals responded to California's Medicaid payment increases in the early 1990s by admitting more Medicaid patients, just as for-profits did, and Cutler and Horwitz (2000) documented that nonprofits closed obstetric and emergency services in unprofitable markets as frequently as for-profits. The modern consensus is that nonprofit status is a governance form with tax advantages rather than a constraint that fundamentally alters behavior, because managers face weak residual claimancy and pursue growth, prestige, and market share objectives that mimic profit-seeking in many dimensions.

Technology adoption follows a **quality ladder** model where providers signal sophistication and attract referrals by offering the latest modalities, even when incremental benefits are marginal. Robotic surgery systems exemplify this dynamic: despite randomized trials showing no superiority over laparoscopic surgery for prostatectomy or hysterectomy, robotic platforms diffused rapidly after 2000 because hospitals marketed them as cutting-edge and surgeons faced competitive pressure to adopt or risk losing patient volume. The capital cost of a da Vinci robot exceeds two million dollars, plus per-procedure disposable costs, yet hospitals absorbed these expenses to maintain market position. Kolstad (2013) found that hospitals advertising superior heart attack outcomes experienced increased patient inflow, but the effect was limited to high-education zip codes where patients possessed the literacy to interpret quality signals, suggesting that quality competition is segmented by patient sophistication and that providers cater to the most informed segment.

The ethical dimension of physician agency—modeled as the parameter $\theta$ in the utility function—reflects professional norms instilled through medical education and reinforced by peer review, malpractice liability, and professional society guidelines. The Hippocratic tradition emphasizes fiduciary duty, enjoining physicians to prioritize patient welfare over personal gain. Yet the empirical evidence reveals substantial elasticity in treatment intensity with respect to financial incentives, indicating that ethical constraints bind imperfectly. McGuire (2000) synthesized the SID literature and concluded that physicians exhibit "imperfect agency": they internalize patient welfare but are also influenced by income considerations, with the relative weight depending on the strength of professional norms, the transparency of the decision, and the financial stakes. Strong ethical norms can suppress SID in domains where clinical appropriateness is clear-cut and peer monitoring is robust, but discretionary gray-zone decisions—borderline surgical indications, choice among therapeutically equivalent options—remain sensitive to payment incentives.

Payment reform initiatives aim to realign incentives by shifting from volume-based to value-based reimbursement. **Accountable care organizations** (ACOs) accept shared savings contracts where they retain a fraction of spending reductions achieved relative to historical benchmarks, provided quality metrics meet thresholds. The theoretical appeal is that shared savings internalize the social cost of utilization without imposing full capitation risk on providers, enabling a smoother transition from FFS. Early Medicare ACO evaluations found modest savings of 1-2 percent in the first contract periods, driven primarily by reduced post-acute care and imaging utilization rather than broad efficiency gains (McWilliams et al. 2016). The mechanisms appear to be formulary management, referral channeling to lower-cost settings, and utilization review rather than fundamental care redesign. Selection effects complicate interpretation: ACOs may attract providers already practicing efficiently or patients with lower baseline spending growth, artificially inflating measured savings.

**Pay-for-performance** (P4P) programs explicitly link payment to measured quality or efficiency metrics. The conceptual framework draws from multitasking principal-agent theory (Holmstrom and Milgrom 1991): when effort across multiple dimensions is costly and some dimensions are more easily measured, agents substitute toward measured tasks and away from unmeasured ones. P4P programs thus risk distorting priorities toward narrow targets at the expense of broader care quality. Britain's Quality and Outcomes Framework (QOF), introduced in 2004, tied 25 percent of general practitioner income to 146 quality indicators for chronic disease management. Early evaluations found substantial improvements in documented compliance with indicators—blood pressure control for diabetes, asthma management plans—but modest or no improvements in patient-reported outcomes or mortality, and some evidence of reduced attention to non-incentivized conditions (Roland and Campbell 2014). The incentive power was strong enough to change recorded behavior but not transformative enough to shift population health.

Hospital competition for physicians shapes much of provider behavior because physicians, particularly specialists, control patient flow through referrals and admitting privileges. Hospitals compete by offering attractive compensation packages, scheduling flexibility, and capital investments in technology that enhance physician productivity or prestige. This competition generates **physician-driven arms races** where hospitals over-invest in technology and facilities to recruit profitable specialists. Cardiac surgery and orthopedic surgery programs produce high margins under administered payment systems, so hospitals subsidize these service lines with capital and support staff, even when population need does not justify capacity. The result is excess capacity in profitable specialties and under-provision of lower-margin primary care and mental health services.

The supply-side dynamics of healthcare markets reflect a complex interplay of information asymmetry, payment incentives, professional norms, and regulatory constraints. Providers are neither pure profit maximizers nor perfect patient agents but operate as imperfect fiduciaries whose behavior responds elastically to financial incentives, constrained by ethical norms and legal liability. Payment system design—FFS versus capitation versus bundling—fundamentally determines whether providers face marginal incentives to increase or decrease utilization, and competition manifests along quality dimensions when prices are administered but along price dimensions when negotiated. Technology adoption follows prestige and reimbursement signals rather than rigorous cost-effectiveness criteria, and organizational integration shifts incentive structures but does not eliminate the underlying agency problem. Policy interventions to realign incentives—value-based payment, quality reporting, tort reform—produce measurable but modest effects, constrained by gaming, risk selection, and the multidimensional nature of healthcare quality. A comprehensive understanding of provider behavior requires integrating microeconomic agency theory with institutional knowledge of payment systems, organizational forms, and the clinical realities that shape physician decision-making.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

Provider behavior in healthcare markets operates through a system of interconnected decision nodes where information asymmetry, payment structure, and competitive positioning jointly determine utilization patterns, quality provision, and technology adoption. Understanding this architecture requires mapping the data flows, decision points, and feedback mechanisms that translate incentives into observable practice patterns.

The foundational decision unit is the **physician-patient encounter**, where the physician holds informational advantage across multiple dimensions. At the diagnostic stage, the physician interprets symptoms, orders tests, and synthesizes results into a diagnosis. Diagnosis is not a mechanical mapping but involves discretion: borderline test results admit multiple interpretations, and clinical guidelines often specify ranges rather than thresholds, permitting judgment calls. The decision tree branches at each node, with the physician choosing whether to investigate further, initiate treatment, refer to a specialist, or adopt watchful waiting.

Payment structure injects a price vector into this decision tree. Under fee-for-service, each branch has an associated revenue value $p_i$, and the physician's income function is the sum of realized fees: $I = \sum_i p_i \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{service } i \text{ chosen}]$. The indicator function captures the discrete nature of service decisions—a test is either ordered or not—though intensity can be continuous along dimensions like follow-up frequency. The physician maximizes utility $U(I, \theta)$ where $\theta$ represents the disutility of violating clinical appropriateness norms. The first-order condition for service $i$ is:

$$\frac{\partial U}{\partial I} \cdot p_i = \frac{\partial U}{\partial \theta} \cdot \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial q_i}$$

where $\partial \theta / \partial q_i > 0$ when additional services deviate from optimal care. At the margin, the physician balances income gains against the ethical cost of over-treatment. This trade-off is patient-specific: ambiguous cases have lower $\partial \theta / \partial q_i$ (less clear-cut inappropriateness), so the physician tolerates more discretionary utilization. Payment rate changes shift the margin: a higher fee $p_i$ increases the left-hand side, inducing more utilization unless the ethical constraint binds absolutely.

The capitation regime inverts this calculation. The physician receives $C$ per enrollee per period and incurs marginal cost $MC_i$ for each service provided. The relevant expression becomes:

$$\frac{\partial U}{\partial I} \cdot (-MC_i) = \frac{\partial U}{\partial \theta} \cdot \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial q_i}$$

where now $\partial U / \partial I$ pushes toward minimizing services (since services reduce net income), and the ethical constraint $\partial \theta / \partial q_i$ discourages under-provision. Capitation shifts the default from over-provision to under-provision unless quality monitoring or reputation penalties provide countervailing incentives.

Bundled payment introduces a fixed revenue $B$ for an episode of defined scope (e.g., total knee replacement including surgery, hospitalization, and 90-day post-acute care). The provider now optimizes episode cost, choosing among substitutable inputs (home health versus skilled nursing, branded versus generic drugs) to minimize $\sum_i MC_i q_i$ subject to clinical constraints. The decision architecture becomes:

$$\max_{q_1, \ldots, q_n} \left[ B - \sum_{i=1}^n MC_i q_i \right]$$

subject to quality thresholds $Q(q_1, \ldots, q_n) \geq \bar{Q}$ that prevent extreme cost-cutting. The provider internalizes substitution possibilities within the bundle—discharging patients home rather than to a skilled nursing facility saves the bundle cost without sacrificing surgical reimbursement—but maintains incentives to attract marginal patients since each new episode generates additional revenue $B$.

The information flow from patient to physician to payer creates opportunities for strategic behavior at each stage. Patients present symptoms, but symptom reporting is subjective and influenced by pain tolerance, health literacy, and fear of bad news. Physicians interpret symptoms through diagnostic tests, but test ordering is discretionary: a patient with chest pain can receive immediate cardiac catheterization or start with non-invasive stress testing. The payer observes claims data—CPT procedure codes, ICD diagnosis codes, place of service—but does not observe the clinical presentation that justified the services. This information asymmetry enables **upcoding** (documenting higher-severity diagnoses to justify more intensive billing) and **unbundling** (billing component procedures separately rather than using a lower-reimbursed bundled code).

Claims data exhibit systematic patterns that reveal strategic behavior. Distributions of procedure codes cluster just above reimbursement thresholds: when Medicare pays substantially more for evaluation-and-management codes at the "moderate complexity" level versus "low complexity," observed coding distributions spike at the moderate threshold and exhibit missing mass just below it, consistent with physicians migrating marginal cases upward. Silverman and Skinner (2004) documented that procedure rates spike at age 65, precisely when Medicare eligibility begins, for discretionary services (back surgery, cataract surgery) but not for non-discretionary acute care (hip fracture repair). This discontinuity at the eligibility threshold suggests that insurance-driven demand—enabled by physician agency—drives much discretionary utilization rather than patient need alone.

Hospital competition introduces a spatial dimension to the decision architecture. Patients value proximity, but they also value quality, amenities, and physician reputation. The hospital choice problem can be modeled as a discrete choice over alternatives $h \in H$:

$$U_{ih} = \alpha_i Q_h - \beta_i d_{ih} + \gamma_i A_h + \epsilon_{ih}$$

where $Q_h$ is observable quality (mortality rates, infection rates), $d_{ih}$ is distance from patient $i$ to hospital $h$, $A_h$ represents amenities (private rooms, meal quality, parking), and $\epsilon_{ih}$ captures idiosyncratic preferences. Patients choose hospital $h^*$ that maximizes $U_{ih}$. Hospitals compete by investing in $Q_h$ and $A_h$, but quality investment has delayed returns (reputation updating is slow) and amenities are more immediately observable, so competition may over-weight amenities relative to clinical effectiveness.

Hospital quality $Q_h$ is produced by a combination of physician skill, nurse staffing, technology capital, and organizational processes. A reduced-form production function might take the Cobb-Douglas form:

$$Q_h = A \cdot K_h^\alpha \cdot L_h^\beta \cdot S_h^\gamma$$

where $K_h$ is capital stock (imaging equipment, surgical robots), $L_h$ is labor input (nurse-to-patient ratios), and $S_h$ is physician skill. The parameter $A$ captures total factor productivity, reflecting organizational efficiency and care coordination. Hospitals choose inputs to maximize profit $\pi_h = p_h q_h - r_K K_h - w_L L_h - w_S S_h$ where $p_h$ is the reimbursement rate, $q_h$ is patient volume as a function of quality $q_h(Q_h)$, and $r_K, w_L, w_S$ are input costs. In administered-price settings like Medicare, $p_h$ is fixed by regulation, so hospitals cannot compete on price. Instead, they maximize volume by raising quality, yielding first-order conditions:

$$\frac{\partial q_h}{\partial Q_h} \cdot \frac{\partial Q_h}{\partial K_h} \cdot p_h = r_K$$

The left-hand side is the marginal revenue product of capital via its effect on quality and thence volume. Hospitals invest in capital until marginal revenue product equals marginal cost. Importantly, if patients are only weakly responsive to quality ($\partial q_h / \partial Q_h$ small), hospitals under-invest in quality relative to the social optimum. Conversely, if patients over-weight observable amenities, hospitals over-invest in amenities.

The competitive dynamics in negotiated-price markets differ fundamentally. When hospitals bargain with insurers over reimbursement rates, market power determines the split of surplus. A hospital with local monopoly power can extract $p_h$ above competitive levels by threatening to exit the insurer's network, which would reduce the network's attractiveness to consumers. The Nash bargaining solution allocates surplus proportional to each party's outside option value. Hospitals in concentrated markets capture larger shares, raising $p_h$ without commensurate quality improvement. Gowrisankaran, Nevo, and Town (2015) estimated a structural model of hospital-insurer bargaining and found that eliminating the second-largest hospital system in a market would increase the remaining system's negotiated prices by 50 percent on average, with effects concentrated in markets where the remaining system would approach monopoly.

Physician referral networks constitute a critical information channel. Primary care physicians serve as gatekeepers, directing patient flow to specialists and hospitals. Referral decisions are influenced by perceived specialist quality, historical relationships, convenience, and sometimes financial arrangements. When primary care physicians hold ownership stakes in imaging centers, ambulatory surgery centers, or specialty practices, referral patterns shift toward owned facilities. Medicare claims data show that self-referral to physician-owned ancillary services generates 20-40 percent higher utilization than referral to independently-owned facilities, controlling for patient characteristics (Mitchell 2010). The mechanism operates through both intensive margin (more services per referral) and extensive margin (more patients referred).

The structure of referral networks can be represented as a directed graph where nodes are providers and edges are referral relationships with weights proportional to referral volume. Formally, let $R_{ij}$ denote the referral rate from provider $i$ to provider $j$. The network exhibits clustering when $R_{ij}$ and $R_{jk}$ are positively correlated—provider $i$ refers to $j$, and $j$ refers to $k$, forming triangles. Such clustering enables informal risk-sharing and reciprocal referral agreements that sustain volume for all participants. Anti-kickback statutes prohibit explicit quid-pro-quo arrangements, but implicit reciprocity operates through social norms and repeated interactions.

Technology adoption follows a diffusion process shaped by reimbursement incentives, peer effects, and competitive positioning. Let $\tau_i(t)$ be an indicator that provider $i$ has adopted technology $T$ by time $t$. Adoption timing depends on expected net present value:

$$NPV_i = \int_0^\infty e^{-rt} \left[ \Delta R_{iT}(t) - C_{iT}(t) \right] dt$$

where $\Delta R_{iT}(t)$ is the incremental revenue from adopting $T$ (via volume gains or reimbursement premiums), $C_{iT}(t)$ is the cost stream (capital, training, consumables), and $r$ is the discount rate. Providers adopt when $NPV_i > 0$. Early adopters face higher costs (immature technology, learning curves) but capture first-mover advantages in patient attraction. Late adopters benefit from cost declines and learning spillovers but risk losing market share. The resulting diffusion curve typically follows an S-shape: slow initial uptake by innovators, rapid adoption as the technology proves viable, and saturation as the marginal provider's NPV approaches zero.

Peer effects accelerate diffusion through multiple channels. Physicians observe colleagues' adoption and update their beliefs about technology efficacy and patient demand. Social learning reduces perceived risk. Competitive pressure builds as early adopters advertise their capabilities, forcing laggards to adopt defensively. Bundorf, Chernew, Rosen, and Yegian (2005) found that hospital adoption of drug-eluting stents exhibited strong spatial correlation even after controlling for patient demographics, consistent with competitive interdependence rather than independent evaluation of net benefits.

Practice style variation—the persistent differences in treatment intensity across geographic regions that cannot be explained by patient health or preferences—reflects the influence of local norms on individual physician behavior. The Dartmouth Atlas documents that Miami, Florida residents face twice the Medicare spending of Minneapolis, Minnesota residents, driven primarily by more frequent specialist visits, more diagnostic testing, and more inpatient days, not by higher prices or sicker patients. Molitor (2018) decomposed this variation by following physicians who relocate across regions. If physician effects dominate, movers should maintain their practice style in the new location. If place effects dominate, movers should converge to the local norm. The data strongly support place effects: physicians shift toward the local intensity level within 2-3 years of relocation, and the convergence is fastest for physicians joining established groups. The mechanism appears to be peer influence and local institutional routines—standardized order sets, referral pathways, and shared decision protocols—that embed higher or lower intensity defaults.

Malpractice liability injects an asymmetric penalty for false negatives (missed diagnoses) relative to false positives (unnecessary testing), skewing incentives toward defensive medicine. The decision tree at the diagnostic node includes a branch where low-probability but high-severity conditions (e.g., pulmonary embolism in a patient with chest pain) warrant testing despite low prior probability, because failing to diagnose and subsequently being sued generates large expected costs. Let $L$ be the expected malpractice loss from a missed diagnosis, $p$ the prior probability of disease, and $c$ the cost of a diagnostic test. The physician orders the test if:

$$p \cdot L > c$$

even when the expected health benefit is low. Malpractice pressure inflates $L$ via both the direct legal costs and the reputational damage of publicized lawsuits. Kessler and McClellan exploited state-level tort reforms that capped non-economic damages and found that regions adopting caps saw hospital spending decline 5-9 percent for cardiac patients, suggesting that defensive medicine accounts for a material share of baseline utilization.

Quality measurement and reporting create a feedback loop where observed performance influences patient choice, which affects provider volume and revenue. Let $Q_h^{obs}$ be the publicly reported quality metric (e.g., risk-adjusted mortality rate) for hospital $h$. Patient demand responds to reported quality: $q_h = q(Q_h^{obs}, X_h)$ where $X_h$ includes other demand drivers (location, amenities). However, true quality $Q_h^{true}$ differs from observed quality due to measurement error and risk adjustment imperfections:

$$Q_h^{obs} = Q_h^{true} + \epsilon_h$$

where $\epsilon_h$ includes unobserved patient risk heterogeneity and stochastic outcome variation. Hospitals can manipulate $Q_h^{obs}$ through patient selection (avoiding high-risk cases) or coding intensity (documenting more comorbidities to inflate risk scores), introducing strategic distortion. Dranove et al. (2003) found that after New York began publishing cardiac surgery mortality rates, hospitals reduced their acceptance of high-risk patients, particularly uninsured and Medicaid patients who contributed negatively to reported performance. This cream-skimming improved reported metrics but reduced access for the most vulnerable, illustrating the multitasking distortion where agents optimize the measured dimension at the expense of unmeasured social objectives.

The interaction between payment systems and organizational form creates distinct behavioral regimes. Solo practitioners under fee-for-service face sharp volume incentives and full residual claimancy, yielding:

$$\max_{q_1, \ldots, q_n} \sum_i p_i q_i - \sum_i MC_i q_i - \theta(q - q^*)$$

where $\theta(q - q^*)$ is the ethical penalty for deviation from optimal care. In contrast, salaried physicians in a large group practice face compensation $W = \bar{w} + \lambda \sum_i p_i q_i$ where $\bar{w}$ is base salary and $\lambda < 1$ is a productivity multiplier. The first-order condition becomes:

$$\lambda p_i = MC_i + \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial q_i}$$

The attenuation factor $\lambda$ reduces volume incentives relative to solo practice but does not eliminate them. Group practices also introduce peer monitoring: colleagues observe each other's practice patterns, and outliers face social pressure. Peer effects can either amplify or dampen individual propensities depending on the group's aggregate norms. In high-intensity groups, new physicians drift toward aggressive practice styles; in conservative groups, aggressive physicians moderate.

Vertical integration between hospitals and physicians alters the decision architecture by consolidating control over the care pathway. When physicians are employed by the hospital, the hospital can direct referrals internally, steer patients toward higher-margin services, and coordinate utilization decisions across the care continuum. The integrated entity maximizes joint profit:

$$\max_{q_1, \ldots, q_n, R} \sum_i (p_i - MC_i^{hosp} - w_i^{phys}) q_i + \sum_j p_j^{referral} R_j$$

where $R_j$ denotes referrals to internal specialists and $p_j^{referral}$ is the profit margin on referred services. Integration internalizes externalities—a primary care physician's referral generates profit for the hospital's specialty group—so incentives align toward maximizing system-wide revenue. However, this alignment can manifest as increased utilization rather than efficiency gains, particularly when site-of-service payment differentials allow the same service to be billed at higher rates in a hospital setting than in a freestanding practice.

Empirically, vertical integration studies employ difference-in-differences designs comparing outcomes for practices acquired by hospitals versus matched controls. Neprash et al. (2015) found that physician practices acquired by hospitals increased their Medicare spending by $100 per patient per year, driven by both higher billing rates (hospital facility fees) and increased ancillary utilization (imaging, lab tests). The mechanism is a shift to hospital outpatient department billing status, which qualifies for higher reimbursement under Medicare's outpatient prospective payment system (OPPS) relative to physician fee schedule rates. Integration thus raises costs without commensurate quality improvement, violating the theoretical prediction that integration should enhance efficiency through coordination.

The decision architecture for hospital technology investment involves dynamic considerations and strategic interdependence. Hospitals anticipate rivals' actions and make preemptive investments to capture market share. A dynamic game structure can be formalized as:

- Stage 1: Hospital $h$ decides whether to invest in technology $T$, incurring fixed cost $F$.
- Stage 2: Patients observe each hospital's technology portfolio and choose where to receive care.
- Stage 3: Hospitals provide care and earn profits.

Backward induction solves for the subgame-perfect equilibrium. At Stage 2, patients allocate based on perceived quality, which includes technology. At Stage 1, hospitals invest if the volume gain from adopting exceeds the fixed cost. If hospitals are identical, symmetric equilibria may involve both investing (medical arms race) or neither investing (efficient coordination), and the realized outcome depends on strategic preemption timing. Dranove, Simon, and White (2002) found that hospital pairs in competitive markets adopted MRI scanners within one year of each other more frequently than monopolistic hospitals, consistent with investment racing rather than efficient sequential adoption.

The mechanics of supplier-induced demand hinge on the physician's ability to shift the patient's perceived marginal benefit of services. The patient's demand curve is latent—they do not know ex ante which services are beneficial—so the physician simultaneously reveals information and makes recommendations. Formally, let $\tilde{B}_i$ be the patient's perceived benefit of service $i$ after receiving the physician's recommendation. The physician can influence $\tilde{B}_i$ by framing marginal cases as warranting intervention. The patient accepts the recommendation if $\tilde{B}_i > P_i$ where $P_i$ is the out-of-pocket price (often zero under insurance). The physician chooses the informativeness of the signal: more transparent communication reduces $\tilde{B}_i$ for low-benefit services but also reduces the physician's income. The equilibrium level of transparency balances income motives against ethical constraints and reputational concerns.

Data on physician responses to payment changes provide direct tests of SID. Gruber and Owings (1996) exploited the fertility decline in the 1970s-1980s, which exogenously reduced the patient pool for obstetricians. The model predicts that obstetricians facing demand shortfalls would increase the cesarean rate (a higher-reimbursed alternative to vaginal delivery) to maintain income. The data confirm: in areas where fertility declined most, C-section rates rose substantially. The elasticity of C-sections to physician density was approximately 0.15, indicating that a 10 percent increase in obstetrician supply raises C-section rates by 1.5 percentage points, controlling for patient characteristics. This response is inconsistent with passive price-taking behavior and consistent with active demand induction.

Similarly, Yip (1998) studied Medicare's fee reduction for thoracic surgeons in 1989 and found that surgeons increased volume by 15 percent in response to the 20 percent fee cut, partially offsetting the income loss. The volume response was concentrated in discretionary procedures (chest wall reconstruction) rather than non-discretionary emergencies (trauma surgery), confirming that surgeons exercised discretion at the margin. These natural experiments provide causal identification of SID because the payment changes are policy-driven and plausibly exogenous to individual physician characteristics.

The architecture of healthcare supply thus operates as a multi-agent system where physicians, hospitals, insurers, and patients interact through information channels distorted by asymmetry, payment rules that create divergent incentives, and competitive forces that manifest along quality dimensions when prices are regulated but along price dimensions when negotiated. Payment system design—the choice of FFS, capitation, bundling, or value-based hybrids—fundamentally rewires the incentive topology, shifting the marginal returns to utilization, quality investment, and patient selection. Organizational integration consolidates decision rights and internalizes externalities but does not eliminate the underlying agency problem; it merely reconfigures it. Quality measurement and public reporting introduce feedback loops that can drive improvement or distortion depending on the observability and manipulability of metrics. Technology adoption follows revenue signals and peer effects rather than rigorous cost-effectiveness analysis, producing socially excessive investment in reimbursed modalities and under-investment in poorly reimbursed preventive and chronic disease management. The mechanics of this system are not deterministic but probabilistic: incentives shift distributions of practice patterns, and the aggregate outcomes emerge from millions of individual decisions, each one a marginal calculation balancing income, ethics, liability, and professional identity.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Optimizing healthcare provider behavior at the system level requires designing payment and regulatory mechanisms that align private incentives with social welfare while remaining robust to strategic manipulation and implementable within administrative constraints. The first-best outcome—physicians acting as perfect agents recommending only services where marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost, hospitals competing on true clinical quality, and technology adopted when net social value is positive—is unattainable given irreducible information asymmetry. Policy design therefore targets second-best mechanisms that induce reasonably efficient behavior despite information rents and agency costs.

The starting point for payment system optimization is recognizing the trade-off between incentive power and risk exposure. High-powered incentives (pure FFS or full capitation) sharply align provider income with observed metrics—volume under FFS, cost reduction under capitation—but expose providers to financial risk from outcome variance beyond their control. Low-powered incentives (fixed salaries) insulate providers from risk but weaken effort provision. The optimal payment mechanism balances these forces using the informativeness principle: pay should be sensitive to outcomes that are informative about provider effort but not to noise outside provider control.

Bundled payment with quality thresholds represents a practical implementation of this principle. The bundle internalizes complementarities and substitutes within an episode, reducing incentives for wasteful intra-episode utilization while maintaining incentives to attract appropriate patients. Quality thresholds provide a floor preventing excessive cost-cutting. The design parameters are the episode definition (time window, included services), the payment level (calibrated to cover efficient provision plus margin), and the quality metrics with penalty functions. Medicare's Comprehensive Care for Joint Replacement (CJR) model, launched in 2016, bundled all hip and knee replacement costs from admission through 90 days post-discharge, with quality screens for readmissions, complications, and patient experience. Early evaluations found 2-3 percent spending reductions driven primarily by shorter inpatient stays and substitution toward less-expensive post-acute care settings, with no detected quality degradation on measured dimensions (Finkelstein et al. 2018). However, the model did not fundamentally transform surgical practice or care pathways; instead, providers made incremental adjustments at the discharge margin where flexibility was greatest and financial impact largest.

Scaling bundled payment beyond procedural episodes to chronic disease management encounters attribution and risk adjustment challenges. For a diabetes bundle encompassing all care over a year, which providers are accountable—the primary care physician, the endocrinologist, the nephrologist treating diabetic kidney disease? Attribution rules must allocate patients to a single accountable entity, but patients see multiple providers and attribution by plurality of visits or spending is gameable. Risk adjustment must control for baseline severity to prevent providers from avoiding sick patients, but risk scores are themselves endogenous to documentation practices. Medicare Advantage and Pioneer ACO programs use hierarchical condition categories (HCC) risk adjustment, where documented diagnoses map to risk weights. Plans and providers responded by increasing diagnostic coding intensity—documenting every possible chronic condition at each encounter—without changing care delivery, inflating risk scores by 4-9 percentage points over several years (Geruso and Layton 2020). This coding creep allows providers to receive higher capitation payments for the same patients, undermining the budget neutrality of risk adjustment.

Optimal risk adjustment requires continuously recalibrating weights and auditing documentation to prevent gaming, but the regulatory burden is substantial and audit precision is limited. A theoretically superior alternative is **prospective risk adjustment**, where payment is based on diagnoses observed in a prior baseline period, eliminating the incentive to upcode during the performance period. However, prospective adjustment introduces lag: patients' health deteriorates over time, and payment based on two-year-old diagnoses increasingly mismatches current need. The trade-off between contemporaneous (gameable but accurate) and prospective (robust but stale) adjustment is fundamental and cannot be fully resolved.

Pay-for-performance program optimization hinges on selecting metrics that are valid, reliable, attributable, and non-gameable. Validity requires that the metric correlates with patient welfare: mortality is valid for heart attacks but not for asymptomatic preventive care. Reliability requires that the metric exhibits low noise relative to signal, so providers are not punished for random outcome variation. Attributability requires that the measured outcome is plausibly influenced by the incentivized provider's actions rather than by other providers or external factors. Non-gameability requires that the metric cannot be manipulated through documentation or patient selection without genuine quality improvement.

Composite measures improve reliability by aggregating multiple indicators, reducing noise, but introduce weighting judgments: should blood pressure control count equally with smoking cessation counseling? Clinical practice guidelines provide one source of weights, but guidelines often lack rigorous cost-effectiveness foundations. Empirical approaches use factor analysis or latent variable models to extract common factors across correlated indicators, but extracted factors may not correspond to meaningful clinical constructs. The CMS Hospital Value-Based Purchasing (VBP) program uses a weighted composite of process measures, mortality rates, patient experience scores, and cost efficiency, with weights updated periodically based on stakeholder input. Early evaluations found no significant mortality improvement attributable to VBP, though hospitals made incremental gains on patient experience scores, suggesting that incentive power was too weak or too diffuse to drive substantive clinical change (Ryan et al. 2017).

Threshold versus continuous incentives represent another design margin. Threshold incentives pay bonuses for exceeding a target: achieve 90 percent blood pressure control in diabetics, receive payment; fall below, receive nothing. Continuous incentives pay proportionally to performance: each percentage point improvement earns incremental payment. Thresholds create sharp incentives near the margin but induce bunching at the threshold and minimal effort above it. Continuous functions smooth effort but dilute marginal incentives. Theoretical optimal contracts often feature kinked piecewise-linear functions: minimal pay below a threshold, linear increases in an intermediate range, and flat pay above an upper threshold where marginal effort is no longer cost-justified.

Risk adjustment in P4P programs faces identical challenges to risk adjustment in bundled payment but with higher stakes because performance metrics are publicly reported and tied to reputation. Surgeons whose patients have worse observed outcomes face volume losses even when outcomes reflect patient selection rather than surgeon skill. The trade-off between transparency and risk aversion is stark: if outcome reporting is too granular and poorly risk-adjusted, high-skill surgeons avoid high-risk patients to protect rankings. If reporting is too aggregated or risk-adjusted to the point of erasing signal, consumers cannot distinguish quality. Empirical evidence from cardiac surgery report cards shows that surgeons responded to public reporting by declining high-risk patients; the net effect was reduced access for sick patients without aggregate health improvement (Dranove et al. 2003). Optimal reporting policy may involve private (provider-only) feedback for learning purposes and public reporting only after risk adjustment has been validated and gaming mechanisms closed.

Competition policy in healthcare markets deviates from standard antitrust frameworks because price competition is largely absent under administered payment systems, and quality competition does not necessarily improve welfare when quality is imperfectly observed or multidimensional. Hospital merger policy faces a trade-off between market power (raising negotiated prices in private-payer markets) and potential efficiency gains (consolidating duplicative services, improving care coordination). The FTC and DOJ evaluate mergers using concentration metrics (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index), patient flow data, and econometric estimates of price effects. Mergers that substantially increase concentration in geographically concentrated markets face presumptive challenges. However, hospital systems argue that integration enables population health management and value-based contracting, requiring scale to bear financial risk.

Empirical evidence supports the market power channel more than the efficiency channel. Gaynor and Town (2012) surveyed hospital merger studies and concluded that consolidation raises prices 20-40 percent on average in concentrated markets, with minimal evidence of offsetting quality improvements or cost reductions. Recent mergers increasingly involve cross-market hospital systems rather than in-market competitors, complicating enforcement because traditional geographic market definitions do not capture bargaining leverage from large multi-market footprints. Insurers operating statewide negotiate simultaneously across markets with multi-market systems, and the system can credibly threaten to exit all markets if the insurer does not meet price demands across the portfolio. This all-or-nothing bargaining gives systems leverage even in markets where they lack concentration, a phenomenon not addressed by traditional antitrust screens.

Vertical integration regulation faces similar identification challenges. Physician-hospital integration can improve care coordination, reduce duplicative testing through integrated records, and enable bundled payment participation. Yet empirical studies consistently find that integration raises spending without commensurate quality gains, driven by site-of-service payment differentials. Medicare pays hospitals substantially more for the same outpatient services than it pays freestanding physician practices, creating incentives to convert acquired practices to hospital outpatient departments (HOPDs). The spending increase is a pure transfer from Medicare to providers, with no clinical justification.

Regulatory response options include equalizing payment rates across sites of service (site-neutral payment), prohibiting conversion to HOPD status for acquired practices, or scrutinizing vertical transactions more aggressively. Site-neutral payment is economically first-best but faces political resistance from hospital lobbies. The Medicare Payment Advisory Commission (MedPAC) has repeatedly recommended site-neutral payment, and Congress adopted limited site neutrality for off-campus HOPDs in 2015, but on-campus facilities remain exempt. Extending site neutrality would eliminate the integration arbitrage but requires Congress to override hospital opposition.

Technology adoption can be optimized through reimbursement policy that rewards cost-effective technologies and withholds payment for marginally beneficial innovations. Coverage with evidence development (CED) conditionally approves reimbursement for new technologies pending real-world outcomes data collection. Medicare implemented CED for implantable cardiac defibrillators in 2005, requiring participating hospitals to enroll patients in registries tracking outcomes. The policy aimed to slow diffusion of ICDs into populations where trial evidence was weak while gathering data to refine clinical indications. Evaluation found that CED modestly slowed adoption but did not prevent use in off-label populations, because hospitals and physicians found workarounds to code encounters as registry-eligible (Tunis and Pearson 2006).

Value-based pricing for pharmaceuticals and devices represents a theoretically optimal mechanism: manufacturers set prices, and payers reimburse up to a threshold derived from cost-effectiveness analysis (e.g., $100,000 per quality-adjusted life year gained). The UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) employs this framework, rejecting reimbursement for therapies that exceed cost-effectiveness thresholds unless special circumstances apply. U.S. Medicare lacks statutory authority to use cost-effectiveness in coverage decisions, but commercial insurers increasingly negotiate value-based contracts where payment is contingent on real-world outcomes. For example, payers negotiated risk-sharing agreements with Novartis for Kymriah (CAR-T therapy): if the patient does not achieve remission within one month, the payer receives a rebate. Such contracts shift risk to manufacturers, incentivizing selection of patients most likely to benefit and improving dynamic efficiency by rewarding truly effective innovations.

Failure modes in healthcare supply systems arise when strategic behavior exploits design weaknesses in payment, quality measurement, or competition regulation. The most pervasive failure mode is **metric fixation**: when payment or reputation depends on measured performance, providers optimize the metric rather than the underlying objective. Teaching-to-the-test manifests across pay-for-performance programs, quality reporting, and bundled payment. Hospitals improve publicly reported mortality rates through patient selection and risk score inflation rather than clinical process improvement. Physicians meet diabetes blood pressure targets by documenting more baseline comorbidities (raising the risk-adjusted target) rather than by intensifying blood pressure management. ACOs achieve shared savings by avoiding high-cost patients and selecting favorable attribution populations rather than by improving care efficiency.

Preventing metric gaming requires continuous recalibration, audit, and diversification of metrics to cover unmeasured dimensions. However, expanding the metric set dilutes incentive power across dimensions and increases administrative complexity. The optimal breadth-depth trade-off is context-dependent: narrow metrics with strong incentives work well for well-defined, high-volume conditions where gaming is detectable (e.g., door-to-balloon time for heart attacks), but broad composites with weak incentives are needed for complex, heterogeneous populations where unintended consequences are harder to anticipate.

A second failure mode is **risk selection** or cream-skimming, where providers avoid sicker patients who will deteriorate the provider's reported performance or exceed capitation payments. Risk adjustment mitigates but does not eliminate this incentive because unobserved heterogeneity (patient motivation, social support, adherence) predicts outcomes beyond coded diagnoses. Providers observe these signals through clinical interaction and can discourage enrollment subtly—long wait times for new patients, inconvenient office hours, reluctance to accept Medicare or Medicaid. Ellis and McGuire (1986) showed theoretically that competitive insurance markets with imperfect risk adjustment lead to welfare-reducing service distortions: insurers underprovide services valued by unprofitable (high-cost) enrollees and overprovide services valued by profitable (low-cost) enrollees, distorting the service mix to attract favorable risk. Analogous distortions occur in provider markets under capitation or bundled payment.

Empirical detection of risk selection is challenging because enrollment decisions are endogenous: patients select providers based on convenience, reputation, and recommendations, so sicker patients may naturally sort to academic medical centers with specialized capabilities. Distinguishing selection from capability requires exogenous variation in patient-provider matching, such as emergency transports where the patient cannot choose the hospital. Doyle, Graves, and Gruber (2019) used ambulance company assignment as an instrument for hospital choice and found that higher-spending hospitals (traditionally interpreted as lower quality in prior observational studies) actually reduce mortality, suggesting that previous findings confounded hospital quality with patient severity.

A third failure mode is **threshold effects** in bundled payment, where providers reduce care intensity for patients just above and increase intensity for patients just below the spending threshold defining shared savings. If an ACO receives 50 percent of savings below a benchmark, the marginal incentive changes discontinuously at the benchmark. Providers have incentives to reduce intensity for patients well below the benchmark (where incremental savings accrue) but increase intensity for patients near the benchmark (where moving them above the threshold avoids penalties). Empirical tests search for discontinuities in treatment patterns at benchmark thresholds, though power is limited by sample sizes and the difficulty of observing real-time spending relative to benchmarks.

A fourth failure mode is **organizational collapse of accountability** in large integrated systems. When hospitals employ hundreds of physicians across multiple specialties and sites, assigning accountability for individual patient outcomes becomes intractable. Diffuse accountability dilutes incentives: no single physician feels responsible for the patient's total cost or outcome, so each makes locally optimal but globally suboptimal decisions. Hospital systems address this through care coordinators, utilization review committees, and clinical pathways, but these bureaucratic mechanisms introduce administrative costs and reduce physician autonomy, potentially degrading morale and increasing turnover. Physician satisfaction data show that employed physicians report lower professional autonomy and higher burnout than practice owners, suggesting that organizational scale imposes psychic costs even if it improves coordination.

A fifth failure mode is **malpractice distortion persistence** despite tort reforms. Even when states cap non-economic damages, reducing expected liability costs, defensive medicine persists because physicians are boundedly rational and loss-averse. The psychological salience of rare but catastrophic lawsuits induces risk-averse behavior disproportionate to expected costs. Malpractice premiums function as a signal of risk, and once premiums fall, the signal weakens, but physician behavior adjusts slowly because practice habits are sticky. Currie and MacLeod (2008) found that malpractice reform reduced cesarean rates but increased adverse neonatal outcomes, suggesting that some defensive cesareans did in fact prevent complications, and tort reform shifted physicians too far toward non-intervention.

Addressing these failure modes requires multi-dimensional policy interventions. Payment reform must combine bundled or capitated payment to control volume with process and outcome quality metrics to guard against under-provision, plus risk adjustment robust to gaming and continuous recalibration. Competition policy must recognize that hospital and physician market power operates through bargaining leverage in negotiated-price markets, not through classical price collusion, and apply antitrust scrutiny to consolidation that increases negotiating leverage even without raising concentration in narrow geographic markets. Quality reporting must protect against patient selection through robust risk adjustment, private feedback loops for learning, and delayed public reporting until gaming mechanisms are closed. Technology coverage policy should adopt value-based frameworks requiring cost-effectiveness evidence before reimbursement approval, with conditional coverage during evidence development phases. Malpractice reform should cap non-economic damages to reduce defensive medicine but preserve malpractice liability's disciplinary function by maintaining caps above levels where deterrence vanishes.

The high-performance configuration for healthcare supply systems balances multiple objectives: incentivizing efficient utilization, rewarding true quality improvement, maintaining access for high-risk patients, fostering beneficial technology innovation, and preserving physician professionalism and autonomy. No single mechanism achieves all objectives simultaneously. Bundled payment with quality screens approximates this balance for episodic acute care. Capitation with pay-for-performance approximates it for chronic disease management. Value-based insurance design, where patient cost-sharing varies by the clinical value of services (zero cost-sharing for high-value preventive services, higher cost-sharing for low-value discretionary imaging), can align patient and provider incentives, but evidence on VBID effectiveness is mixed, and implementation requires granular service classification and adjudication.

The frontier of payment reform explores **reference pricing**, where payers set a maximum reimbursement rate for a bundled service (e.g., hip replacement) and patients choosing higher-priced providers pay the difference out-of-pocket. Reference pricing introduces price competition by making patients cost-sensitive at the margin while preserving access to all providers willing to accept the reference price. CalPERS implemented reference pricing for joint replacement in 2011, setting a $30,000 cap. Utilization shifted toward lower-priced hospitals, and some high-priced hospitals reduced their prices below the cap to retain volume. Spending per procedure fell 20 percent with no detected quality degradation (Robinson and Brown 2013). However, reference pricing requires that patients can identify prices in advance, that prices correlate weakly with quality (so patients are not penalized for choosing efficient providers), and that provider networks remain adequate. These conditions hold for standardized high-volume procedures but not for complex, rare, or emergency conditions.

Accountable care organizations represent an attempt to integrate care coordination and payment reform under an umbrella entity accepting financial risk for a defined population. The theory posits that ACOs internalize externalities across settings (hospital, post-acute, primary care) and across time, incentivizing investments in preventive care and chronic disease management that reduce downstream costs. Early ACO results are modest: Medicare Shared Savings Program ACOs reduced spending by 1-2 percent on average, with savings concentrated in durable medical equipment, post-acute care, and imaging (McWilliams et al. 2016). Hospital admissions and primary care utilization changed minimally, suggesting that ACOs optimized around the margins where financial impact was largest and clinical stakes lowest, rather than fundamentally redesigning care. Selection effects likely inflate measured savings: ACOs are voluntary, and providers with favorable patient panels or efficient baseline practices are more likely to enter, so observed savings partly reflect selection rather than behavioral response.

Scaling ACOs to population-wide coverage requires mandatory assignment, comprehensive risk adjustment, and sufficient upside and downside financial risk to change behavior. The Next Generation ACO model increased downside risk and gave ACOs more flexibility to waive cost-sharing for high-value services, but uptake was limited because few provider groups were willing to accept full financial risk. Provider reluctance reflects both risk aversion (concerns about adverse patient selection or random outcome variance) and organizational unreadiness (lack of data infrastructure, care management capabilities, and capital reserves to absorb losses). Building organizational capabilities for population health management requires upfront investment, yet payment systems typically operate on annual cycles with limited tolerance for transition losses.

International comparisons offer insights into alternative models. Germany's sickness funds combine capitation, pay-for-performance, and negotiated fee schedules, with competition among insurers for enrollees and negotiation with provider associations over reimbursement rates. The Netherlands implements regulated competition: insurers must accept all enrollees at community-rated premiums, risk adjustment transfers funds from insurers with favorable selection to those with adverse selection, and insurers negotiate with hospitals and physicians over quality and price. Empirical evidence suggests that Dutch reforms increased efficiency modestly—hospitals reduced lengths of stay and standardized pathways—but did not transform quality or patient experience (Schäfer et al. 2010). The UK's National Health Service funds hospital trusts via block grants with performance targets, eliminating volume incentives but requiring central planning and administrative monitoring to prevent shirking. Long waiting times for elective procedures suggest that NHS block funding induces under-provision, but cross-country health outcome comparisons are confounded by population health differences, so net welfare implications remain ambiguous.

The optimal payment and regulatory configuration depends on the condition being treated, the information environment, the organizational maturity of providers, and the political economy constraints of the jurisdiction. Acute episodic care with clear endpoints and measurable outcomes is well-suited to bundled payment with quality thresholds. Chronic disease management with long time horizons and multidimensional outcomes is better addressed through capitation with comprehensive quality measurement and robust risk adjustment. Preventive care with deferred benefits may require value-based insurance design that eliminates cost-sharing and employs outreach and reminders to overcome behavioral inertia. High-cost specialty drugs and devices require value-based contracting with manufacturers, tying payment to real-world effectiveness. Hospital market concentration requires aggressive antitrust enforcement and, where mergers have already occurred, potentially structural remedies such as divestiture or behavioral conditions on network contracting practices. Physician-hospital integration requires site-neutral payment to eliminate arbitrage and may warrant greater regulatory skepticism about integration's claimed coordination benefits given empirical evidence of spending increases without quality improvement.

Healthcare supply optimization is not a one-time design problem but a dynamic regulatory process requiring continuous monitoring, evaluation, and adaptation as providers develop new strategies to optimize under prevailing rules. Payment models must evolve faster than gaming mechanisms, quality metrics must expand to cover newly observable dimensions as data infrastructure improves, and competition policy must anticipate consolidation strategies that skirt traditional market definitions. The regulatory state's capacity to stay ahead of strategic providers is limited by budgets, expertise, and political constraints, so the second-best realistic outcome is a stable equilibrium where providers earn modest information rents but deliver reasonably efficient care, rather than a first-best outcome where all rents are extracted and care perfectly aligns with social welfare. Achieving even this second-best equilibrium requires sophisticated payment design, credible enforcement, and institutional mechanisms that sustain regulatory capability over time despite political turnover and lobbying pressure.
