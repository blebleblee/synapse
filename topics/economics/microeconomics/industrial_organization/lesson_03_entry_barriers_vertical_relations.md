# Lesson 3: Entry Barriers, Vertical Integration, Contractual Restraints

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The classical model of perfect competition rests on the assumption that entry and exit are frictionless—that any firm observing positive economic profit in a market can instantaneously replicate the incumbent's production technology, achieve identical cost structures, and compete away supernormal returns. Yet empirical observation reveals persistent market concentration, durable profit differentials across firms, and strategic behavior designed explicitly to deter new rivals. Industrial organization addresses this gap by formalizing the mechanisms through which incumbents erect barriers to entry, leverage vertical relationships to foreclose rivals, and deploy contractual restraints that reshape competitive dynamics.

Entry barriers emerge from both structural and strategic origins. Structural barriers arise from the natural technological or demand characteristics of an industry: capital requirements that cannot be recovered upon exit, minimum efficient scale that requires substantial output to achieve unit cost parity with incumbents, and absolute cost advantages derived from proprietary knowledge or preferential access to scarce inputs. These barriers exist independently of incumbent behavior and reflect the underlying production function and market size. Strategic barriers, by contrast, are endogenously constructed by incumbents through choices of capacity, product design, pricing, and contracting. The incumbent commits to actions that alter the post-entry payoff structure, making entry unprofitable even when the potential entrant possesses identical technology.

The concept of sunk costs plays a central role in distinguishing meaningful entry barriers from merely high but recoverable investments. A sunk cost is an expenditure that cannot be recovered if the firm exits the market—investments in specialized physical capital with no alternative use, advertising and brand development expenditures that create firm-specific goodwill, or regulatory compliance costs tied to a particular jurisdiction. Sunk costs create asymmetry between incumbents and entrants: the incumbent has already incurred these expenditures and treats them as bygones in output and pricing decisions, while the entrant must bear them prospectively and incorporate them into the entry condition. This asymmetry enables credible commitment. An incumbent with high sunk capacity can threaten aggressive post-entry output expansion—a threat that would be incredible if capacity were costlessly adjustable, but becomes credible when capacity expansion required substantial irreversible investment that has already been made.

Baumol's theory of contestable markets formalizes the limiting case where sunk costs vanish entirely. A perfectly contestable market is one in which entry and exit are costless and instantaneous, so that potential entrants can engage in hit-and-run entry: enter if the incumbent prices above average cost, capture profit, and exit before the incumbent can retaliate. In such markets, the threat of entry disciplines incumbent behavior even in the absence of actual competition. The incumbent is constrained to price at average cost and produce efficiently, replicating the outcome of perfect competition despite market concentration. Contestability theory clarifies that market structure alone—measured by concentration ratios or Herfindahl indices—provides insufficient information about competitive conduct. The critical determinant is the height of entry and exit barriers, not the number of active firms. Empirically, however, few markets satisfy the stringent conditions for perfect contestability. Most markets exhibit some degree of sunk cost, product differentiation, or strategic interdependence that allows incumbents to sustain pricing above competitive levels.

Strategic entry deterrence involves the incumbent making commitments that alter the entrant's post-entry profit function. The classic models distinguish between accommodation, where the incumbent accepts entry and shares the market, and deterrence, where the incumbent takes actions that render entry unprofitable. The credibility of deterrence threats depends on subgame perfection: the threatened action must be optimal for the incumbent to carry out after entry has occurred, given the sunk nature of prior commitments. Dixit's investment in excess capacity model illustrates this logic. An incumbent facing a potential entrant in a Cournot duopoly can invest in production capacity before the entry decision is made. Once capacity is installed and represents a sunk cost, the incumbent's marginal cost of production from that capacity is low, making it optimal to produce aggressively in the post-entry subgame. Anticipating this response, the entrant recognizes that entry will trigger a price war and chooses to stay out. The capacity investment functions as a credible commitment device because it alters the incumbent's cost structure and thereby changes its optimal post-entry behavior.

Entry deterrence strategies extend beyond capacity commitments to include predatory pricing, limit pricing, and product proliferation. Limit pricing involves setting price below the short-run profit-maximizing level to signal low costs or low demand to potential entrants, inducing them to update their beliefs about post-entry profitability. Predatory pricing involves temporarily pricing below cost to inflict losses on entrants or rivals, forcing exit and establishing a reputation for aggressive response that deters future entry. Antitrust treatment of predatory pricing requires showing both pricing below an appropriate measure of cost (typically average variable cost or marginal cost) and a dangerous probability of recoupment—that the predator will be able to raise prices above competitive levels after rivals exit and that the present value of recoupment profits exceeds the losses incurred during the predatory period.

Product proliferation operates as an entry barrier in industries with horizontal differentiation and localized competition. An incumbent firm can fill the product space with multiple brands or variants, occupying niches that might otherwise be filled by entrants. Ready-to-eat breakfast cereals provide the canonical example: incumbents introduced dozens of brands across flavor and nutritional dimensions, leaving little room for differentiated entry. The proliferation strategy exploits fixed costs of product introduction and brand development, forcing entrants to either enter with multiple products simultaneously—incurring higher sunk costs—or enter in niches where demand is already partially served. The welfare implications are ambiguous: product proliferation may enhance consumer variety but also dissipate resources in redundant brand development and create artificial barriers to more efficient entry.

Vertical integration—the combination of successive production stages within a single firm—fundamentally alters competitive dynamics in markets with imperfect competition at multiple levels. The transaction cost theory of vertical integration, developed by Coase and Williamson, emphasizes efficiency motivations: firms integrate to eliminate double marginalization, reduce contracting costs in the presence of relationship-specific investments, and mitigate hold-up problems when parties must make sunk investments in assets with limited alternative value. Double marginalization arises when an upstream monopolist sells to a downstream monopolist. Each stage applies its own markup over marginal cost, resulting in a final price above the level a fully integrated monopolist would charge and a reduction in total industry profit. Vertical integration eliminates the successive markups, lowering price and increasing both profit and consumer surplus.

Yet vertical integration can also serve exclusionary purposes, foreclosing rivals from access to essential inputs or distribution channels. The Chicago School critique, articulated by Bork and Posner, argues that vertical foreclosure is generally unprofitable absent a second distortion. A monopolist upstream maximizes profit by selling to all downstream firms, extracting full monopoly rent through the input price. Refusing to deal with some downstream firms and favoring an integrated subsidiary reduces the monopolist's own profit by shrinking the market for its input. This logic implies that vertical integration is typically efficiency-enhancing, and vertical restraints serve to mitigate contracting problems rather than exclude rivals.

Post-Chicago models incorporate strategic considerations and demonstrate conditions under which vertical foreclosure can be profitable. Ordover, Saloner, and Salop show that an upstream monopolist may refuse to supply a downstream rival to induce exit, creating a downstream monopoly that facilitates coordination and raises equilibrium prices. Hart and Tirole formalize the commitment value of vertical integration: by integrating downstream, the upstream firm credibly commits not to supply downstream rivals on favorable terms, reducing rivals' incentive to enter or expand. The profitability of foreclosure depends on the elasticity of downstream demand, the degree of product differentiation, and the distribution of bargaining power in input contracting. Empirically, vertical foreclosure appears most likely in markets with high concentration both upstream and downstream, substantial barriers to entry at both levels, and limited availability of alternative supply sources.

Contractual vertical restraints—resale price maintenance, exclusive dealing, tying, and territorial restrictions—allow upstream firms to influence downstream pricing and distribution without full integration. These restraints generate tension between efficiency and exclusion theories. Efficiency theories emphasize that vertical restraints solve coordination problems, prevent free-riding on promotional efforts, and facilitate quality certification. Resale price maintenance (RPM), where a manufacturer sets minimum or maximum resale prices, can prevent discount retailers from free-riding on the promotional services provided by full-service retailers. If retailers invest in demonstrations, knowledgeable sales staff, and post-sale service, consumers may learn from these services but purchase from discounters, undermining the incentive to provide the services. RPM ensures that all retailers maintain sufficient margins to fund service investments, enhancing interbrand competition even while restricting intrabrand price competition.

Exclusive dealing, where a buyer commits to purchase exclusively from a single supplier or a supplier commits to sell exclusively through a single distributor, can encourage relationship-specific investment and solve commitment problems. A manufacturer may be unwilling to invest in training a retailer's sales force or developing a distribution network if the retailer can switch to a rival supplier after the investment is made. Exclusive dealing contracts provide assurance of continued business, supporting efficient investment. The Chicago School perspective holds that exclusive dealing is rarely anticompetitive because foreclosure requires contracts covering enough buyers to deny rivals minimum efficient scale, a coordination problem that is difficult to solve. Moreover, buyers will refuse exclusive dealing contracts unless compensated for the opportunity cost of foreclosure, limiting the profitability of the strategy.

Anticompetitive theories of exclusive dealing emphasize naked exclusion and the raising rivals' costs. Rasmusen, Ramseyer, and Wiley demonstrate that an incumbent can profitably pay buyers to accept exclusive dealing contracts even when buyers would prefer to maintain access to alternative suppliers. The key insight is that foreclosing a critical mass of buyers imposes a negative externality on non-contracting buyers by preventing entry or causing rival exit, which reduces competitive pressure and raises prices for all buyers. Each buyer, acting individually, may accept compensation for exclusivity without internalizing the effect on other buyers. Exclusive dealing can thus be profitable for the incumbent even when it reduces total welfare. The effectiveness of excluslosure depends on economies of scale—whether the foreclosed rival requires a minimum efficient scale to survive—and the contestability of buyers. If buyers can easily switch suppliers or renegotiate contracts, foreclosure is less credible.

Tying arrangements, where the sale of one product is conditioned on the purchase of a second product, generate similar ambiguities. Efficiency theories emphasize that tying can reduce transaction costs, ensure quality compatibility, facilitate metering of usage for price discrimination, and protect brand reputation. The classic example is razor handles and blades: the manufacturer ties blade purchases to ensure compatibility and quality, preventing consumer dissatisfaction from using inferior third-party blades. Leverage theories, articulated by the Harvard School, argue that a firm with monopoly power in one market can extend that power into a second market through tying, foreclosing rivals in the tied market and extracting additional monopoly profit. The Chicago School response, developed by Bowman, Director, and Posner, notes that a monopolist in the tying market already extracts full monopoly profit through the price of the tying good and gains no additional profit from monopolizing the tied market unless there is a second distortion, such as price regulation in the tying market that creates an incentive to evade regulation through bundling.

Modern tying theories incorporate strategic and dynamic considerations. Whinston shows that tying can deter entry into the tied market by committing the incumbent to aggressive pricing post-entry, forcing the entrant to compete in both markets simultaneously rather than entering sequentially. Carlton and Waldman demonstrate that tying can serve as a mechanism to preserve monopoly in the primary market by foreclosing complementary products that could serve as platforms for entry. The Microsoft antitrust case illustrated these dynamics: the bundling of Internet Explorer with Windows was alleged to foreclose Netscape's browser, which could have served as a platform for applications that competed with Windows. The essential facility doctrine, applied in vertical contexts, requires that a dominant firm with control over an essential input or distribution channel provide access to rivals on reasonable terms, preventing strategic foreclosure.

The welfare analysis of entry barriers, vertical integration, and contractual restraints requires careful attention to both static and dynamic effects. Static efficiency considers the allocation of resources at a point in time: do these practices lead to higher prices, reduced output, and deadweight loss relative to a competitive benchmark? Dynamic efficiency considers incentives for innovation, quality improvement, and relationship-specific investment over time. Entry barriers may reduce static efficiency by sustaining supracompetitive pricing but enhance dynamic efficiency by allowing incumbents to appropriate returns from innovation and long-term investments that would be competed away under free entry. Vertical integration and contractual restraints may restrict short-run competition but facilitate coordination, prevent free-riding, and support investment in brand reputation and distribution networks that enhance long-run consumer welfare. The rule of reason approach in antitrust, particularly after *Continental T.V., Inc. v. GTE Sylvania Inc.*, reflects this balance, requiring a fact-intensive inquiry into the specific effects of vertical restraints in each market context rather than applying per se rules based on form alone.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The formal architecture of strategic entry deterrence rests on sequential-move game theory with commitment. Consider a two-stage game in which an incumbent (firm 1) moves first by choosing capacity $k_1$, which is sunk and irreversible, and then a potential entrant (firm 2) observes $k_1$ and decides whether to enter. If firm 2 enters, both firms compete in quantities (Cournot) or prices (Bertrand) in the post-entry subgame. The equilibrium concept is subgame perfection: we solve backward from the post-entry subgame to determine the incumbent's optimal capacity choice.

In the post-entry Cournot subgame, each firm $i$ chooses quantity $q_i$ to maximize profit given the rival's quantity. The inverse demand function is $P(Q) = a - bQ$, where $Q = q_1 + q_2$. The incumbent's marginal cost of production from installed capacity $k_1$ is $c$ for $q_1 \leq k_1$ and rises discontinuously above $k_1$. The entrant's marginal cost is $c + \epsilon$, reflecting a small disadvantage. The first-order condition for firm 1 is:

$$\frac{\partial \pi_1}{\partial q_1} = P(Q) + q_1 P'(Q) - c = 0$$

This yields the reaction function $q_1^*(q_2) = \frac{a - c - b q_2}{2b}$. Symmetrically, firm 2's reaction function is $q_2^*(q_1) = \frac{a - (c+\epsilon) - b q_1}{2b}$. Solving the system of reaction functions gives the Cournot-Nash equilibrium quantities:

$$q_1^{CN} = \frac{a - c + \epsilon}{3b}, \quad q_2^{CN} = \frac{a - c - 2\epsilon}{3b}$$

If the incumbent installs capacity $k_1 > q_1^{CN}$, this capacity commitment does not directly change the Cournot equilibrium if the capacity constraint does not bind. However, if the incumbent commits to produce up to capacity regardless of the entrant's output—modeling capacity as a Stackelberg leadership commitment—the incumbent's quantity is fixed at $k_1$, and the entrant best-responds by choosing $q_2^*(k_1) = \frac{a - (c+\epsilon) - b k_1}{2b}$. The incumbent can then choose $k_1$ to maximize profit, recognizing the entrant's reaction:

$$\pi_1(k_1) = [a - b(k_1 + q_2^*(k_1))] k_1 - c k_1 - F$$

where $F$ is the sunk cost of capacity installation. Substituting $q_2^*(k_1)$ and differentiating yields the Stackelberg quantity:

$$k_1^S = \frac{a - c}{2b}$$

The entrant's profit at this output level is:

$$\pi_2 = \left[\frac{a - c - 2\epsilon}{4}\right]^2 \frac{1}{b} - F_E$$

where $F_E$ is the entrant's fixed cost. Entry is deterred if $\pi_2 < 0$, which occurs when $F_E$ is sufficiently large relative to the squared margin $(a - c - 2\epsilon)^2$. The incumbent can also choose a capacity level $k_1^{limit}$ that drives the entrant's profit exactly to zero, known as limit capacity. This is the minimum capacity that deters entry and is found by solving $\pi_2(k_1^{limit}) = 0$:

$$k_1^{limit} = \frac{a - (c + \epsilon) - 2\sqrt{b F_E}}{b}$$

The incumbent's choice between accommodation (setting $k_1^S$ and allowing entry) and deterrence (setting $k_1^{limit}$) depends on comparing profits:

- Accommodation profit: $\pi_1^{acc} = [a - b(k_1^S + q_2^*(k_1^S))] k_1^S - c k_1^S - F$
- Deterrence profit: $\pi_1^{det} = [a - b k_1^{limit}] k_1^{limit} - c k_1^{limit} - F$

Deterrence is optimal when $\pi_1^{det} > \pi_1^{acc}$, which occurs when the entrant's fixed cost $F_E$ is intermediate—not so low that even limit capacity cannot deter entry, and not so high that entry is naturally deterred without strategic overinvestment.

The credibility of the deterrence threat hinges on sunk capacity. If capacity were costlessly adjustable, the incumbent's threat to produce $k_1^{limit}$ would not be credible: after entry, the incumbent would find it optimal to reduce output to the Cournot level, and the entrant, anticipating this, would enter anyway. Sunk capacity transforms the threat into a commitment because, once installed, the marginal cost of production from that capacity is low, making high output optimal in the post-entry subgame.

Vertical integration alters the payoff structure in markets with successive monopolies. Consider a linear vertical chain with an upstream monopolist producing an input at marginal cost $c_u$ and selling to a downstream monopolist that uses one unit of input per unit of final output and incurs additional marginal cost $c_d$. Final demand is $P(Q) = a - bQ$. Without integration, the upstream firm sets input price $w$ to maximize $\pi_u = (w - c_u) Q_d(w)$, where $Q_d(w)$ is the downstream firm's demand for the input. The downstream firm, treating $w$ as its marginal cost, sets final price to maximize $\pi_d = [P(Q) - w - c_d] Q$. Solving backward, the downstream firm's quantity choice satisfies:

$$P(Q) + Q P'(Q) = w + c_d$$

which yields $Q_d(w) = \frac{a - w - c_d}{2b}$. The upstream firm then chooses $w$ to maximize:

$$\pi_u = (w - c_u) \frac{a - w - c_d}{2b}$$

Differentiating with respect to $w$ gives the upstream optimal input price:

$$w^* = \frac{a + c_u - c_d}{2}$$

Substituting back, the final quantity is:

$$Q_{NI} = \frac{a - c_u - c_d}{4b}$$

and final price is:

$$P_{NI} = \frac{3a + c_u + c_d}{4}$$

Total industry profit is the sum of upstream and downstream profits. Now consider a vertically integrated firm that internalizes both stages. The integrated firm chooses $Q$ to maximize:

$$\pi_{int} = [P(Q) - c_u - c_d] Q = [a - bQ - c_u - c_d] Q$$

The first-order condition yields:

$$Q_{I} = \frac{a - c_u - c_d}{2b}$$

and final price:

$$P_{I} = \frac{a + c_u + c_d}{2}$$

Comparing the two structures, $Q_I = 2 Q_{NI}$ and $P_I < P_{NI}$. The integrated firm produces twice the quantity and charges a lower price than the non-integrated chain. Total industry profit under integration is:

$$\pi_I = \frac{(a - c_u - c_d)^2}{4b}$$

which exceeds the sum of non-integrated profits:

$$\pi_{NI} = \pi_u + \pi_d = \frac{(a - c_u - c_d)^2}{8b}$$

Integration eliminates double marginalization, increasing both profit and consumer surplus. This demonstrates the Coasian insight that vertical integration can be privately profitable and welfare-enhancing by internalizing successive markups.

Vertical foreclosure requires modifying this framework to include competing downstream firms. Suppose the upstream monopolist supplies two downstream firms that compete in Cournot fashion. If the upstream firm integrates with one downstream firm, it can raise the input price $w$ to the unintegrated rival, increasing the rival's marginal cost and shifting output toward the integrated subsidiary. The upstream firm's profit-maximizing input price $w$ trades off the direct markup on input sales against the strategic effect of raising the rival's cost. Let $q_1$ be the output of the integrated subsidiary and $q_2$ be the output of the unintegrated rival. The upstream firm chooses $w$ to maximize:

$$\pi = [P(q_1 + q_2) - c_u - c_d] q_1 + (w - c_u) q_2$$

The rival's first-order condition, treating $w$ as its marginal cost, is:

$$P(q_1 + q_2) + q_2 P'(q_1 + q_2) = w + c_d$$

This implicitly defines $q_2(w, q_1)$. The upstream firm's choice of $w$ affects $q_2$ and thereby the final price. If the upstream firm sets $w$ sufficiently high, it can drive the rival's profit negative, inducing exit and creating a downstream monopoly. This is profitable if the gain from monopolizing the downstream market exceeds the loss of input sales to the rival.

Exclusive dealing contracts are modeled as a contracting game with externalities. Suppose an incumbent upstream firm offers exclusive dealing contracts to $n$ buyers, each of whom demands one unit. A rival supplier can enter if at least $m$ buyers remain uncontracted, where $m$ is the minimum efficient scale. The incumbent offers a payment $T$ to each buyer to sign an exclusive contract. If buyer $i$ signs, the rival cannot enter (assuming other buyers also sign), and the incumbent charges monopoly price $p_m$. If buyer $i$ rejects the contract and other buyers also reject, the rival enters and competition drives price to $p_c < p_m$. The externality is that buyer $i$'s decision affects the profitability of entry and thereby the prices faced by other buyers.

Let $v$ be each buyer's valuation and assume buyers are identical. If all buyers reject exclusive contracts, the rival enters and each buyer receives surplus $v - p_c$. If all buyers accept exclusive contracts, the rival is foreclosed, the incumbent charges $p_m$, and each buyer receives $v - p_m + T$. For buyer $i$ to accept, we require:

$$v - p_m + T \geq v - p_c \quad \Rightarrow \quad T \geq p_m - p_c$$

The incumbent's cost of signing all $n$ buyers is $n T$. The incumbent's profit from foreclosure is the monopoly profit $\pi_m = n(p_m - c)$, where $c$ is marginal cost. Without foreclosure, the incumbent earns duopoly profit $\pi_d = n(p_c - c)/2$ (assuming Bertrand competition drives price to marginal cost, we set $\pi_d = 0$ for simplicity). The incumbent finds foreclosure profitable if:

$$\pi_m - n T \geq \pi_d$$

Substituting $T = p_m - p_c$:

$$n(p_m - c) - n(p_m - p_c) \geq 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad n(p_c - c) \geq 0$$

This condition is always satisfied if $p_c > c$, meaning that exclusive dealing can be profitable even when it harms buyers in aggregate. The key insight is that each buyer, acting individually, does not internalize the effect of accepting the contract on other buyers' prices. In equilibrium, all buyers accept the contract, yet all buyers would be better off if none accepted, illustrating a coordination failure.

The profitability of exclusive dealing increases with economies of scale (higher $m$) and with the cost differential between the incumbent and the entrant. If the entrant has a significant cost disadvantage, the incumbent needs to contract fewer buyers to prevent entry, reducing the total payment required. Antitrust policy toward exclusive dealing requires analyzing whether the contracts foreclose a substantial share of the market, whether the rival has alternative distribution channels, and whether buyers were compensated for the opportunity cost of exclusivity.

Resale price maintenance can be formalized as a principal-agent problem with retailer services. Suppose a manufacturer sells through multiple retailers that provide promotional effort $e_i$ at cost $c(e_i)$. Effort increases demand but creates a free-rider problem: consumers learn from high-service retailers but purchase from low-price discounters. Manufacturer profit depends on total demand $D(\sum e_i, p)$, where $p$ is the retail price. Without RPM, each retailer chooses effort and price independently, treating other retailers' effort as given. The retailer's profit is:

$$\pi_i = (p_i - w) D_i(e_i, p_i, e_{-i}, p_{-i}) - c(e_i)$$

In equilibrium, retailers free-ride on each other's effort, providing suboptimal service. The manufacturer can set a minimum resale price $\bar{p}$ that ensures sufficient margin for effort provision. The optimal $\bar{p}$ is chosen to maximize manufacturer profit subject to the constraint that retailers earn non-negative profit. This prevents destructive price competition and sustains investment in service quality, enhancing total demand and benefiting the manufacturer. The welfare effect depends on whether the increased service quality outweighs the higher retail price.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Strategic entry deterrence faces implementation challenges related to commitment credibility and capacity utilization. An incumbent seeking to deter entry through overinvestment in capacity must ensure that the capacity is truly sunk—that it cannot be costlessly liquidated or redeployed to alternative uses. If capacity can be sold on secondary markets, the commitment loses credibility because the incumbent retains the option to reduce capacity post-entry, returning to accommodation equilibrium. Asset specificity is thus central to deterrence effectiveness: the more specialized the capacity to the particular market or technology, the stronger the deterrent signal. Examples include custom production lines for a specific product, investments in local distribution networks with no alternative customers, and dedicated R&D for a proprietary technology with limited licensing potential.

The utilization rate of installed capacity provides an observable signal of commitment strength. An incumbent operating at high capacity utilization demonstrates that the capacity is actively employed in production, making aggressive post-entry output expansion feasible. Conversely, substantial idle capacity may indicate that the capacity was installed solely for strategic signaling and that actual production costs remain high, weakening the deterrent threat. Entrants scrutinize capacity utilization data and industry structure to assess whether capacity commitments are credible or merely paper tigers. Regulatory filings, trade association reports, and satellite imagery of manufacturing facilities provide information that potential entrants use to evaluate the seriousness of deterrence strategies.

Limit pricing as an entry deterrent suffers from information asymmetry problems. If the incumbent's true costs are private information, a low price may signal low costs (deterring entry) or may reflect a deliberate sacrifice of short-run profit to mislead the entrant about cost conditions. The entrant must solve a signaling game, inferring the incumbent's type from observed pricing behavior. Milgrom and Roberts demonstrate that limit pricing can serve as a separating equilibrium in which low-cost incumbents price below the monopoly level to credibly reveal their cost advantage, while high-cost incumbents cannot profitably mimic this signal. The effectiveness of limit pricing depends on the prior distribution of cost types and the magnitude of informational asymmetry. If the entrant already has good information about industry costs—through public disclosures, trade publications, or reverse engineering—limit pricing loses its signaling value and becomes an unprofitable sacrifice of monopoly rents.

Predatory pricing faces both execution and legal constraints. Economically, predatory pricing requires that the predator sustain below-cost pricing long enough to drive the rival to exit and then raise prices sufficiently to recoup the losses. The predator must have deeper financial resources than the target, preventing the target from matching the low prices or waiting out the predatory episode. Financial markets complicate this calculus: if both firms have access to capital markets, the target can borrow to finance losses during the predatory period, and investors, recognizing that predatory pricing is unsustainable, will fund the target in anticipation of future profits. Predatory pricing is more feasible when capital markets are imperfect and when the target faces binding liquidity constraints or higher borrowing costs than the incumbent.

The Areeda-Turner test proposes that pricing above average variable cost (AVC) should be presumptively legal, while pricing below AVC constitutes predation. The rationale is that a profit-maximizing firm never prices below AVC in the short run because it would lose less by shutting down. Pricing below AVC thus signals an intent to exclude rather than compete. However, the test has limitations: marginal cost is the economically correct benchmark, but it is difficult to measure, especially in multi-product firms with joint costs; and temporary below-cost pricing may reflect promotional strategies, inventory clearance, or introductory pricing rather than predation. Courts have added a recoupment requirement, holding that plaintiffs must prove both below-cost pricing and a dangerous probability that the predator will recoup its losses through subsequent supracompetitive pricing. This requirement screens out false positives where below-cost pricing reflects competition rather than exclusion.

Vertical integration carries transaction costs and agency problems that limit its optimality. The Coasian theory of the firm holds that integration is optimal when the costs of transacting through markets exceed the costs of internal organization. Market transaction costs include search costs, bargaining costs, contract enforcement costs, and hold-up risks when investments are relationship-specific. Internal organization costs include bureaucratic inefficiencies, reduced high-powered incentives, influence costs, and the difficulty of replicating market discipline within firm boundaries. As firms grow through vertical integration, marginal governance costs rise due to diseconomies of scale in management and information processing. Optimal firm boundaries equate the marginal cost of market transactions with the marginal cost of internal organization.

Agency problems arise when the integrated subsidiary no longer operates as a profit center but as a cost center within the larger firm. Transfer pricing between divisions becomes a source of conflict: upstream divisions seek high transfer prices to inflate their profits, while downstream divisions seek low transfer prices to improve their margins. Headquarters must set transfer prices that balance incentive alignment with efficient resource allocation. The economically correct transfer price is marginal cost when there is no external market for the intermediate good, but measuring marginal cost accurately and preventing strategic misreporting is administratively costly. Many firms resort to negotiated transfer prices or external market prices as benchmarks, but these create distortions when internal production costs differ from market prices or when no competitive external market exists.

Vertical foreclosure through refusal to deal faces constraints from the essential facilities doctrine and antitrust scrutiny. The essential facilities doctrine holds that a firm controlling an input essential for downstream competition and not reasonably replicable by competitors must provide access on reasonable and non-discriminatory terms. Application of the doctrine requires showing that the input is truly essential (no feasible substitutes), that providing access is technically feasible, and that refusal to deal would create or maintain monopoly power downstream. Courts have narrowed the doctrine in recent decisions, emphasizing that forced access reduces incentives to invest in infrastructure and that firms generally have no duty to deal with rivals. The *Trinko* decision held that antitrust law does not impose a general duty to aid competitors and that refusal to deal is presumptively lawful absent evidence of anticompetitive intent and harm to consumer welfare.

Exclusive dealing contracts face enforceability and renegotiation risks. Buyers may breach exclusive contracts if the rival offers sufficiently attractive terms or if antitrust enforcement makes the contracts unenforceable. The incumbent must structure contracts with sufficient penalties for breach to deter switching, but excessively punitive penalties may themselves trigger antitrust scrutiny as unreasonable restraints of trade. Liquidated damages clauses must be reasonably calibrated to actual harm rather than serving purely to coerce performance. Renegotiation risk arises if market conditions change: if the rival's technology improves or if the incumbent's quality deteriorates, buyers will seek to renegotiate or exit contracts, and courts may refuse specific performance on grounds of commercial impracticability or unconscionability.

Long-term exclusive contracts also face timing and coordination challenges. If contracts expire at different dates, the incumbent may be unable to maintain foreclosure because the rival can sign buyers as contracts expire, gradually gaining scale. The incumbent can stagger contract renewal dates to prevent synchronous expiration, but this increases administrative complexity. Forward-looking buyers, anticipating that foreclosure may not be sustained, may refuse to sign exclusive contracts, creating a time-consistency problem for the incumbent. The incumbent must credibly commit to maintaining foreclosure over the long term, which may require vertical integration or other irreversible investments that align the incumbent's incentives with sustaining foreclosure.

Resale price maintenance faces enforcement difficulties when manufacturers lack monitoring capacity. Manufacturers must detect and punish deviations from minimum resale prices, but detecting price cuts is costly, especially in markets with frequent promotions, bundling, and negotiated discounts. Retailers have incentives to cheat on RPM agreements by offering secret discounts, selective price cuts to high-volume customers, or bundling the RPM product with unrestricted products to effectively lower the price. Manufacturers can use mystery shopping, audit retail prices, and impose penalties for non-compliance, but these measures are imperfect and costly. Online retail and price transparency platforms exacerbate enforcement challenges because price deviations are immediately visible to consumers and competitors, intensifying competitive pressure to discount.

The antitrust treatment of RPM has oscillated between per se illegality and rule of reason analysis. The *Dr. Miles* decision held RPM to be per se illegal as a restraint of trade, but this was overruled in *Leegin Creative Leather Products v. PSKS*, which held that RPM should be analyzed under the rule of reason, considering both procompetitive justifications (preventing free-riding, ensuring service quality) and anticompetitive effects (facilitating manufacturer or retailer cartels, softening interbrand competition). The rule of reason requires plaintiffs to show actual harm to competition, not merely to individual competitors, and to demonstrate that the restraint's anticompetitive effects outweigh its procompetitive benefits. This places a heavier evidentiary burden on plaintiffs and has reduced successful RPM challenges.

Tying and bundling arrangements face computational and pricing complexity in multi-product firms. Mixed bundling, where products are sold both separately and as a bundle, requires setting three prices: $p_A$ for product A alone, $p_B$ for product B alone, and $p_{AB}$ for the bundle. The profit-maximizing pricing structure depends on the correlation of consumer valuations for A and B. If valuations are negatively correlated (consumers who value A highly tend to value B low), mixed bundling allows the firm to extract more surplus by offering a bundle that appeals to moderate-valuation consumers while still charging high prices to consumers with extreme valuations for one product. If valuations are positively correlated, pure bundling (selling only the bundle) may be optimal because it reduces dispersion in total valuations, allowing the firm to set a single high bundle price without losing too many customers.

The profitability of tying as a foreclosure strategy depends on the degree of complementarity and the threat of entry in the tied market. If products A and B are perfect complements (consumed in fixed proportions), the monopolist in A can extract full surplus through the price of A and gains nothing from tying B. Tying becomes valuable when B exhibits variable proportions or when entry in the B market could threaten the monopoly in A by providing a platform for competing systems. The Microsoft case illustrated this dynamic: Windows was threatened by the potential for browsers to serve as a middleware platform that reduced applications' dependence on Windows. By tying Internet Explorer to Windows, Microsoft aimed to prevent Netscape from achieving scale that would allow it to support a competing application ecosystem.

Failure modes in strategic entry deterrence include overinvestment in capacity that remains idle, misjudgment of entrant resolve or capabilities, and reputational damage from aggressive tactics. Overinvestment dissipates profit without effectively deterring entry if the entrant has access to better technology, alternative distribution channels, or differentiated positioning that insulates it from price competition. The incumbent's capacity commitment becomes a sunk cost burden that impairs financial flexibility and competitiveness. Misjudging entrant capabilities is particularly acute in markets with rapid technological change, where incumbents invest in deterring entry based on current technology, only to find that entrants leapfrog with next-generation products that render the incumbent's capacity obsolete.

Reputational damage from predatory or exclusionary conduct can manifest in multiple dimensions: legal exposure from antitrust enforcement, boycotts from customers or suppliers who object to exclusionary practices, and loss of trust among business partners who fear hold-up or opportunistic behavior. Antitrust litigation is costly even when the defendant prevails, diverting management attention and creating adverse publicity. Exclusionary conduct may also provoke regulatory intervention, including forced divestiture, compulsory licensing, or conduct remedies that restrict future business practices. The optimal strategy balances the profit gains from deterrence and exclusion against the expected costs of legal exposure and reputational harm, incorporating risk aversion and the option value of maintaining cooperative relationships with stakeholders.
