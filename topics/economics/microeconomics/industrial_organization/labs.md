## Objective & Operational Lab Setup

Industrial organization stands as the empirical battlefield where microeconomic theory confronts market reality. These laboratories transform abstract models of firm behavior, market structure, and strategic interaction into quantifiable empirical exercises that reveal the mechanics of competition, collusion, entry deterrence, and regulatory intervention. The practitioner's objective is not merely to solve stylized textbook problems but to operationalize the econometric and computational apparatus that antitrust economists, competition authorities, and corporate strategists deploy when analyzing real-world market power.

The analytical environment for industrial organization requires a synthesis of structural econometric estimation, game-theoretic simulation, and computational optimization. Unlike pure theoretical exercises, these labs demand working proficiency with empirical demand estimation, cost function recovery, and equilibrium computation under alternative behavioral assumptions. The core toolkit integrates statistical software for discrete choice demand estimation, nonlinear optimization solvers for Bertrand-Nash and Cournot equilibrium computation, and simulation frameworks for repeated game dynamics and entry game analysis.

Your operational laboratory environment centers on three integrated components. First, statistical computation platforms—R with packages `mlogit` for discrete choice demand estimation and `gmm` for generalized method of moments structural estimation, or Python with `statsmodels` and `scipy.optimize` for maximum likelihood and equilibrium solvers. Second, spreadsheet-based numerical optimization environments for transparent parameter sensitivity analysis and visual verification of equilibrium properties. Third, case study databases containing actual merger filings, market concentration indices, and pricing data from regulatory proceedings that provide empirical grounding for structural model estimation.

The operational verification protocol for industrial organization labs follows a three-stage empirical workflow mirroring professional antitrust economic analysis. Stage one estimates demand systems from market-level or consumer-level data, recovering price elasticities and substitution patterns that govern competitive interaction. Stage two inverts first-order conditions to recover marginal cost functions or conduct parameters that reveal the intensity of competition versus collusion. Stage three constructs counterfactual equilibria under alternative market structures—post-merger, under regulatory price caps, or with hypothetical entry—computing welfare changes and evaluating competitive effects. Each exercise progresses through this demand estimation, supply-side recovery, and counterfactual simulation pipeline, building operational fluency with the structural econometric methods that antitrust authorities and competition economists apply in merger review and market investigation.

Diagnostic rigor demands continuous verification of identification assumptions, convergence properties, and economic plausibility of recovered parameters. Demand estimation requires valid instrumental variables uncorrelated with unobserved product quality shocks—typically cost shifters, characteristics of competing products in other markets, or Hausman instruments constructed from rival product attributes. Supply-side inversion assumes correct specification of competitive conduct—Bertrand price competition, Cournot quantity competition, or collusive behavior—with misspecification producing implausible negative marginal costs or explosive markups. Counterfactual equilibrium computation requires checking that numerical optimization converges to true Nash equilibria by verifying first-order condition satisfaction and stability under perturbation. Every lab exercise incorporates explicit diagnostic checkpoints that flag identification failures, convergence problems, and economically implausible parameter estimates.

The operational reference guide at the conclusion provides rapid diagnostic protocols for common estimation failures: weak instrument diagnostics when demand elasticities explode, grid search strategies when gradient-based optimizers fail to locate Bertrand-Nash equilibria, and specification tests for conduct parameter restrictions that distinguish competitive from collusive behavior. This laboratory manual equips you to execute the complete structural estimation pipeline that professional industrial organization economists deploy when advising competition authorities on merger clearance, market definition, and abuse of dominance investigations.

## Exercise 1: Foundational Practical Implementation

### Demand Estimation and Market Power Measurement in a Discrete Choice Setting

The foundational exercise establishes operational competence in discrete choice demand estimation and markup recovery, the bedrock empirical methods for quantifying market power and competitive effects. You will estimate a multinomial logit demand system from simulated product-level market data, recover implied price elasticities and cross-price elasticities, invert the pricing first-order conditions to back out marginal costs, and compute firm-level markups under Bertrand-Nash equilibrium assumptions. This exercise replicates the structural approach used in landmark merger simulations and market power assessments by competition authorities worldwide.

Begin by generating a synthetic market dataset that mimics the structure of differentiated product markets such as ready-to-eat cereal, automobiles, or telecommunications services. Create a market with twenty products distributed across four firms, each product characterized by observable attributes—price, quality index, advertising intensity—and an unobserved vertical quality component that enters consumer utility but may correlate with price, generating the classic endogeneity problem in demand estimation. Simulate consumer choices under a random utility framework where consumer $i$ choosing product $j$ receives utility $u_{ij} = \alpha_i p_j + \beta' x_j + \xi_j + \epsilon_{ij}$, with $\alpha_i$ representing heterogeneous price sensitivity, $x_j$ capturing observable product characteristics, $\xi_j$ denoting unobserved quality, and $\epsilon_{ij}$ following a Type I extreme value distribution generating the multinomial logit choice probabilities.

Construct the data generation process in R or Python with explicit care for the endogeneity structure. Define four firms, each offering five products. Assign each product a vector of characteristics: a quality index drawn from a uniform distribution $x_{\text{quality}} \sim U(1, 10)$, an advertising intensity $x_{\text{ads}} \sim U(0, 5)$, and a marginal cost drawn from $mc_j \sim U(2, 8)$. Generate the unobserved quality shock $\xi_j \sim N(0, 1)$ independently, then construct equilibrium prices under Bertrand-Nash competition where firms set prices to maximize profits taking rivals' prices as given. For the multinomial logit, the pricing first-order condition for product $j$ owned by firm $f$ is $s_j + \sum_{k \in \mathcal{J}_f} (p_k - mc_k) \frac{\partial s_k}{\partial p_j} = 0$, where $s_j$ is the market share and $\frac{\partial s_k}{\partial p_j} = -\alpha s_j (1 - s_k)$ for the own-price derivative and $\frac{\partial s_k}{\partial p_j} = \alpha s_j s_k$ for the cross-price derivative under logit demand.

Implement the equilibrium price solver numerically. In R, define a function that computes the vector of market shares given prices and parameters, then constructs the profit gradient for each product, and iterates until the gradient vector vanishes. Use `nloptr` with the `NLOPT_LD_SLSQP` algorithm for constrained optimization, imposing non-negativity constraints on prices and reasonable bounds to ensure convergence. In Python, employ `scipy.optimize.fsolve` to locate the fixed point of the pricing system or `scipy.optimize.minimize` with the negative profit objective and gradient information. Initialize the solver at marginal cost plus a markup, such as $p_j^{(0)} = mc_j + 2$, providing a reasonable starting point for the Nash equilibrium search.

Once equilibrium prices are computed, simulate market shares by drawing a large sample of consumers—say, ten thousand—and computing choice probabilities via the logit formula $s_j = \frac{\exp(\alpha p_j + \beta' x_j + \xi_j)}{1 + \sum_{k=1}^{J} \exp(\alpha p_k + \beta' x_k + \xi_k)}$, where the outside option utility is normalized to zero. Aggregate individual choices to construct observed market shares, introducing sampling noise that reflects finite market size. Store the resulting dataset: product identifiers, firm ownership, prices, characteristics $x_j$, and market shares $s_j$. Critically, the unobserved quality $\xi_j$ is latent to the econometrician and must be treated as an estimation error term.

Now confront the endogeneity problem. Naive ordinary least squares regression of log market shares on price and characteristics yields biased coefficients because price correlates with unobserved quality through the equilibrium pricing condition—firms set higher prices for higher-quality products. Construct valid instrumental variables to address this endogeneity. The most powerful instruments are cost shifters that affect marginal cost but do not directly enter demand. In the simulation, introduce exogenous cost shifters such as input price indices or factor cost draws $z_j \sim U(0, 2)$ that shift marginal costs $mc_j = mc_j^{\text{base}} + z_j$ without entering consumer utility. Additionally, construct Hausman instruments: use the characteristics of products offered by the same firm in other geographic markets, or compute the sum of rival product characteristics $\sum_{k \neq j} x_k$ under the assumption that rivals' characteristics correlate with the focal product's price through competitive interaction but not directly with its unobserved quality shock.

Estimate the demand system via two-stage least squares (2SLS) or generalized method of moments (GMM). In R using `gmm`, transform the market share data into the multinomial logit regression form by computing the log-odds ratio $\ln(s_j / s_0)$, where $s_0$ is the outside good share (remaining market share after summing all inside products). Regress this transformed dependent variable on price, product characteristics, and a constant, instrumenting price with cost shifters and Hausman instruments. The moment conditions are $E[\xi_j \cdot z_j] = 0$ for each instrument $z_j$. Estimate via `gmm(g, x = instruments, ...)` where `g` computes the residuals $\xi_j = \ln(s_j / s_0) - \alpha p_j - \beta' x_j$. In Python, use `statsmodels.IV2SLS` or manually implement the 2SLS estimator by first-stage regression of price on instruments, predicting fitted price, then second-stage regression of log-odds shares on fitted price and characteristics.

Verify identification strength by inspecting first-stage F-statistics. In R, run the first-stage regression explicitly via `lm(price ~ instruments + characteristics)` and extract the F-statistic for the excluded instruments. A rule-of-thumb threshold is $F > 10$ for strong identification, though Stock-Yogo critical values provide more rigorous weak instrument diagnostics. If the F-statistic falls below ten, your instruments are weak, and the demand elasticity estimates will be unreliable—either find stronger cost shifters, expand the instrument set with additional Hausman instruments, or reconsider the data generation process to ensure sufficient cost variation orthogonal to demand shocks.

Extract the estimated coefficients: the price coefficient $\hat{\alpha}$ and characteristic coefficients $\hat{\beta}$. Compute implied own-price and cross-price elasticities. For product $j$, the own-price elasticity under logit demand is $\eta_{jj} = \alpha p_j (1 - s_j)$, measuring the percentage change in quantity demanded in response to a one-percent price increase. The cross-price elasticity between products $j$ and $k$ is $\eta_{jk} = -\alpha p_k s_k$, quantifying substitution patterns across products. Construct the full $J \times J$ elasticity matrix and inspect its structure: own-price elasticities should be negative and economically reasonable (typically between -1 and -5 for differentiated products), while cross-price elasticities should be positive, reflecting substitutability, with larger magnitudes for closer substitutes.

Invert the pricing first-order conditions to recover marginal costs. Under Bertrand-Nash competition, the pricing equation rearranges to $mc_j = p_j + \frac{s_j}{\alpha s_j (1 - s_j)} = p_j - \frac{1}{\alpha (1 - s_j)}$. Compute implied marginal costs for each product using observed prices, estimated market shares, and the recovered price coefficient $\hat{\alpha}$. Compare these backed-out marginal costs to the true simulated marginal costs (known in your synthetic data) to validate the structural estimation procedure. Calculate the percentage bias in cost recovery as $(mc_j^{\text{implied}} - mc_j^{\text{true}}) / mc_j^{\text{true}}$. If the median absolute bias exceeds ten percent, investigate whether the assumed competitive conduct (Bertrand-Nash) matches the data generation process, whether instrumental variables are sufficiently strong, or whether the logit functional form adequately captures substitution patterns.

Compute firm-level markups as $(p_j - mc_j) / p_j$, the Lerner index measuring proportional markup over marginal cost. Aggregate markups to the firm level by taking a sales-weighted average across products within each firm. Compare markups across firms to assess the distribution of market power. Calculate the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) as $\text{HHI} = \sum_{f=1}^{F} \left( \sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_f} s_j \right)^2 \times 10000$, measuring market concentration. An HHI below 1500 indicates unconcentrated markets, 1500–2500 signals moderate concentration, and above 2500 denotes high concentration under U.S. Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission merger guidelines. Cross-reference HHI with average markups to verify that concentration correlates with pricing power as theory predicts.

Perform a diagnostic robustness check by re-estimating the demand system under alternative instrument sets. Drop one instrument at a time and observe stability of the price coefficient $\hat{\alpha}$. If the estimate swings wildly (say, changing sign or doubling in magnitude), your identification is fragile and relies precariously on a single instrument. Conduct an over-identification test (Hansen J-statistic) when you have more instruments than endogenous variables. In R with `gmm`, the package automatically reports the J-statistic and its p-value. A p-value below 0.05 suggests rejection of the over-identifying restrictions, indicating that some instruments may violate the exogeneity assumption—correlated with the demand error term $\xi_j$—and should be excluded from the estimation.

Visualize the demand estimation results. Plot the estimated own-price elasticities against product prices in a scatterplot to verify the expected negative relationship—higher-priced products often face more elastic demand as consumers substitute toward cheaper alternatives. Generate a heatmap of the cross-price elasticity matrix to illustrate substitution patterns across products: darker colors indicate closer substitutes with stronger cross-price effects. Construct a bar chart comparing true versus implied marginal costs for each product, overlaying error bars representing the standard errors of the backed-out costs (computed via the delta method from the variance-covariance matrix of $\hat{\alpha}$ and $\hat{\beta}$).

Document your findings in a structured estimation report. Report the demand coefficient estimates with standard errors, first-stage F-statistics, and over-identification test results in a summary table. Present the elasticity matrix in a formatted table with products in rows and columns, highlighting the diagonal (own-price elasticities) and off-diagonal (cross-price elasticities). Summarize the cost recovery diagnostics: mean and median percentage bias, the distribution of markups across firms, and the market HHI. Conclude with an interpretation: does the estimated demand system produce economically plausible elasticities? Are the backed-out marginal costs sensible relative to industry cost benchmarks? Does the market structure—concentration, markup distribution—align with observed competitive intensity?

This foundational exercise establishes the operational pipeline for structural demand estimation and market power measurement. You have confronted the central identification challenge—price endogeneity—constructed valid instruments, estimated demand via GMM, recovered marginal costs through first-order condition inversion, and quantified market power via markups and concentration indices. These methods form the empirical backbone of merger simulation, antitrust market definition, and competitive effects analysis in industrial organization practice.

## Exercise 2: Parameter Variation & Empirical Analysis

### Merger Simulation and Counterfactual Equilibrium Analysis

The second exercise advances your operational capability to counterfactual equilibrium computation and merger simulation, the core deliverable in antitrust merger review. You will use the demand system and cost functions recovered in Exercise 1 to simulate a hypothetical merger between two firms, compute the post-merger Nash equilibrium prices and quantities, and evaluate the competitive effects through price changes, consumer surplus loss, and the Upward Pricing Pressure (UPP) index. This exercise replicates the structural merger simulation that antitrust economists perform when advising competition authorities on merger clearance decisions.

Begin by loading the demand estimates and recovered marginal costs from the foundational exercise. You have in hand the estimated price coefficient $\hat{\alpha}$, characteristic coefficients $\hat{\beta}$, product-level marginal costs $mc_j$, and the pre-merger market structure with four firms each offering five products. Select two firms to merge—say, Firm 1 and Firm 2—such that the merged entity will own ten products total while Firms 3 and 4 remain independent. The critical economic mechanism is that the merged firm internalizes pricing externalities between the merging parties' products: pre-merger, Firm 1 ignored the profit impact of its pricing on Firm 2's products, and vice versa, but post-merger, the combined entity accounts for these cross-product cannibalization effects, generally leading to higher prices unless efficiencies offset the loss of competition.

Formulate the post-merger pricing equilibrium as a nonlinear system of first-order conditions. Under Bertrand-Nash competition with differentiated products and logit demand, each firm $f$ sets prices for its portfolio of products to maximize total profit $\pi_f = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_f} (p_j - mc_j) s_j M$, where $M$ is market size. The first-order condition for product $j$ owned by firm $f$ is $s_j + \sum_{k \in \mathcal{J}_f} (p_k - mc_k) \frac{\partial s_k}{\partial p_j} = 0$. Under multinomial logit, the market share derivatives are $\frac{\partial s_j}{\partial p_j} = \alpha s_j (s_j - 1)$ and $\frac{\partial s_k}{\partial p_j} = \alpha s_j s_k$ for $k \neq j$. In matrix form, define the ownership matrix $\Omega$ where $\Omega_{jk} = 1$ if products $j$ and $k$ are owned by the same firm and $\Omega_{jk} = 0$ otherwise. The pricing system becomes $s + (\Omega \odot \Delta)(p - mc) = 0$, where $\Delta$ is the matrix of share derivatives and $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication.

Construct the pre-merger ownership matrix $\Omega^{\text{pre}}$ where products 1–5 belong to Firm 1, products 6–10 to Firm 2, products 11–15 to Firm 3, and products 16–20 to Firm 4. This is a block-diagonal matrix with four $5 \times 5$ blocks of ones on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere. Construct the post-merger ownership matrix $\Omega^{\text{post}}$ where products 1–10 now belong to the merged entity (Firm 1+2), so the first block expands to $10 \times 10$ ones, while the remaining structure stays unchanged. The difference $\Omega^{\text{post}} - \Omega^{\text{pre}}$ captures the internalized pricing externalities: the upper-right and lower-left $5 \times 5$ sub-blocks connecting Firm 1 and Firm 2 products flip from zero to one, indicating that the merged firm now accounts for cross-product profit impacts that were previously ignored.

Solve for the pre-merger equilibrium prices numerically to establish the baseline. Although you already computed these in the data generation step of Exercise 1, re-solve the pricing system explicitly using the estimated demand parameters $\hat{\alpha}$ and $\hat{\beta}$ and the recovered marginal costs to ensure consistency. Implement a fixed-point iteration or Newton-Raphson solver. Define a function `compute_equilibrium_prices(alpha, beta, X, mc, Omega)` that takes demand parameters, product characteristics, marginal costs, and the ownership matrix, then iterates the pricing first-order conditions until convergence. Initialize at the observed pre-merger prices, compute implied market shares via the logit formula, construct the share derivative matrix $\Delta$, and update prices via $p^{(t+1)} = mc + (\Omega \odot \Delta)^{-1} s$ (inverting the linear system for the markup vector). Iterate until $\|p^{(t+1)} - p^{(t)}\| < 10^{-6}$, ensuring tight convergence.

Verify that the pre-merger equilibrium prices match the observed data prices within numerical tolerance. Discrepancies larger than one percent indicate either numerical instability in the solver or misspecification in the demand system or cost recovery. Diagnose by inspecting the conditioning of the $(\Omega \odot \Delta)$ matrix: if the condition number exceeds $10^6$, the system is near-singular, suggesting collinearity issues in the product space or extreme market shares near zero or one that blow up the logit derivatives. Regularize by trimming products with market shares below 0.1% or introducing a small ridge penalty in the inversion.

Solve for the post-merger equilibrium prices using the merged ownership matrix $\Omega^{\text{post}}$. Call `compute_equilibrium_prices(alpha, beta, X, mc, Omega_post)` and iterate to convergence. Store the post-merger prices $p^{\text{post}}$ and compare to the pre-merger prices $p^{\text{pre}}$. Compute the price change for each product as $\Delta p_j = p_j^{\text{post}} - p_j^{\text{pre}}$ and the percentage price change as $\Delta p_j / p_j^{\text{pre}} \times 100\%$. Theory predicts that the merging firms' products will experience price increases due to the internalization of pricing externalities, while non-merging firms may raise or lower prices depending on strategic complementarity or substitutability in the product space.

Quantify the aggregate competitive effect by computing the sales-weighted average price change $\bar{\Delta p} = \sum_{j=1}^{J} s_j^{\text{pre}} \cdot (\Delta p_j / p_j^{\text{pre}})$. A positive weighted average indicates net consumer harm from higher prices post-merger. Calculate the change in market shares: some products that raised prices significantly will lose share to rivals or the outside good, while products with smaller price increases may gain share. Recompute the post-merger HHI as $\text{HHI}^{\text{post}} = \sum_{f} (\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_f} s_j^{\text{post}})^2 \times 10000$ and compare to the pre-merger HHI. The increase $\Delta \text{HHI} = \text{HHI}^{\text{post}} - \text{HHI}^{\text{pre}}$ measures the change in market concentration. U.S. merger guidelines flag mergers with $\Delta \text{HHI} > 200$ in highly concentrated markets ($\text{HHI} > 2500$) as presumptively anticompetitive, though efficiencies and entry considerations can rebut this presumption.

Compute the change in consumer surplus to quantify welfare effects. Under logit demand, the consumer surplus in monetary terms is $CS = \frac{1}{\alpha} \ln \left( 1 + \sum_{j=1}^{J} \exp(\alpha p_j + \beta' x_j + \xi_j) \right)$. Evaluate this expression at pre-merger and post-merger prices using the estimated parameters, then compute $\Delta CS = CS^{\text{post}} - CS^{\text{pre}}$. Since $\alpha < 0$, an increase in prices reduces the log-sum term, decreasing consumer surplus. Multiply by the market size $M$ to obtain the total consumer harm in dollar terms. Report this as a percentage of pre-merger consumer surplus to contextualize the magnitude.

Calculate the Upward Pricing Pressure (UPP) index for each merging firm's products, a screening tool used by competition authorities to rapidly assess merger incentives without full equilibrium simulation. For a product $j$ owned by Firm 1 merging with Firm 2, the UPP is $UPP_j = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{J}_2} D_{jk} \cdot m_k$, where $D_{jk} = - \frac{\partial s_j}{\partial p_k} / \frac{\partial s_j}{\partial p_j}$ is the diversion ratio (the fraction of lost sales from product $j$ that divert to product $k$) and $m_k = (p_k - mc_k) / p_k$ is the margin on product $k$. Under logit demand, $D_{jk} = s_k / (1 - s_j)$. The UPP quantifies the immediate incentive to raise price on product $j$ post-merger: by raising $p_j$, the merged firm loses sales on $j$ but recaptures a fraction through diverted sales to its other products, earning margin $m_k$ on those recaptured sales. A positive UPP indicates upward pricing pressure. Aggregate UPP across all merging products and compare to the actual price changes from the equilibrium simulation: UPP provides a first-order approximation, while the full simulation accounts for rivals' strategic responses and feedback effects.

Perform a sensitivity analysis on marginal cost assumptions. Mergers often generate cost efficiencies—economies of scale, elimination of duplicative fixed costs, or synergistic production integration—that can offset the anticompetitive price effects. Introduce a hypothetical marginal cost reduction for the merged entity's products: reduce $mc_j$ by 5%, 10%, and 15% for products 1–10, re-solve the post-merger equilibrium at each efficiency level, and recompute price changes and consumer surplus effects. Plot the relationship between efficiency magnitude and average price change: there exists a threshold efficiency level at which the cost reduction fully offsets the pricing externality internalization, leaving prices unchanged or even decreased. This threshold efficiency is a critical input for competition authority decisions: if the merging parties can credibly demonstrate efficiencies exceeding this threshold, the merger may receive clearance despite concentration increases.

Visualize the merger simulation results comprehensively. Create a side-by-side bar chart comparing pre-merger and post-merger prices for all twenty products, color-coded by firm ownership, with the merged entity highlighted. Overlay the percentage price changes as annotations. Generate a scatterplot of UPP versus actual price change for each product, adding a 45-degree reference line to assess the accuracy of the UPP approximation. Construct a line plot showing consumer surplus and producer surplus as functions of the marginal cost efficiency parameter, identifying the break-even efficiency level where total surplus remains constant. Include a table summarizing the key metrics: pre- and post-merger HHI, $\Delta \text{HHI}$, sales-weighted average price change, consumer surplus loss, and threshold efficiency.

Extend the analysis by simulating alternative merger scenarios. Instead of Firm 1 merging with Firm 2, simulate a Firm 1–Firm 3 merger or a three-way merger combining Firms 1, 2, and 3. Compute the competitive effects for each scenario and rank them by consumer harm magnitude. This comparative statics exercise reveals which merger configurations pose the greatest anticompetitive risk: typically, mergers between closer substitutes (higher diversion ratios) generate larger price increases than mergers between distant competitors in product space.

Conduct a robustness check on the demand specification by re-estimating the merger simulation under a nested logit demand system, which relaxes the restrictive substitution patterns of the plain multinomial logit (the Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives property). In nested logit, products are grouped into nests—e.g., high-quality versus low-quality segments—and the correlation of unobserved utility within nests is governed by a nesting parameter $\lambda \in [0,1]$. Re-estimate the demand system with nesting, recover marginal costs under the nested logit first-order conditions, and re-simulate the merger. Compare the predicted price effects: nested logit typically yields different cross-price elasticities and diversion ratios, which can materially alter merger assessments, particularly when the merging products reside in the same nest (closer substitutes) versus different nests (more distant competition).

Document your merger simulation findings in a comprehensive antitrust economic report. Present the demand estimation foundation (recapitulating Exercise 1 results), describe the merger scenario and post-merger ownership structure, report the equilibrium price changes with statistical uncertainty (bootstrap the demand estimation to propagate standard errors through the simulation), quantify consumer harm via surplus loss and aggregate price effects, compute the HHI change, and evaluate the UPP screen. Conclude with a policy recommendation: does the merger likely substantially lessen competition under the statutory standard? What efficiency threshold would be required for clearance? Are there remedial measures—product divestitures, behavioral commitments—that could mitigate competitive harm?

This exercise has operationalized the structural merger simulation toolkit, the gold standard empirical method for ex-ante merger evaluation. You have computed counterfactual equilibria under alternative ownership structures, quantified price and welfare effects, deployed screening tools like UPP and HHI, and incorporated efficiency defenses through marginal cost sensitivity analysis. ## Exercise 3: Stress Testing, Boundary Failure & Recovery Protocol

### Collusion Detection, Conduct Parameter Estimation, and Market Power Inference Under Model Misspecification

The third exercise stress-tests your structural estimation framework by confronting model misspecification, identification failure, and alternative behavioral assumptions. You will extend the demand estimation and equilibrium analysis to distinguish competitive conduct from collusive behavior through conduct parameter estimation, diagnose identification breakdown when instruments are weak or behavioral assumptions are violated, and implement recovery protocols that salvage economic inference from problematic data environments. This exercise mirrors the empirical challenges industrial organization economists face when analyzing markets where competitive conduct is ambiguous, data quality is poor, or structural models fail to fit observed behavior.

Begin by revisiting the data generation process from Exercise 1, but now introduce alternative behavioral regimes. Instead of Bertrand-Nash price competition, simulate market outcomes under three distinct conduct scenarios: perfect collusion (joint profit maximization), Cournot quantity competition, and a hybrid conduct parameter model that nests both competitive and collusive extremes. This multi-regime simulation provides ground truth for evaluating your ability to correctly infer competitive conduct from observational data—a central challenge in antitrust investigations where the intensity of competition is unobserved.

Formulate the conduct parameter model by generalizing the pricing first-order condition to include a conduct parameter $\theta \in [0,1]$ that governs the degree of internalization of competitive externalities. The first-order condition becomes $s_j + \sum_{k \in \mathcal{J}_f} (p_k - mc_k) \frac{\partial s_k}{\partial p_j} + \theta \sum_{k \notin \mathcal{J}_f} (p_k - mc_k) \frac{\partial s_k}{\partial p_j} = 0$. When $\theta = 0$, firms ignore cross-product effects on rivals, yielding Bertrand-Nash competition. When $\theta = 1$, firms fully internalize effects on all products as if jointly maximizing industry profit, yielding perfect collusion. Intermediate values $\theta \in (0,1)$ represent partial collusion or tacit coordination. Re-write in matrix form: $s + [(\Omega + \theta (1 - \Omega)) \odot \Delta](p - mc) = 0$, where $1$ is the all-ones matrix. The ownership matrix $\Omega$ captures within-firm internalization, while $\theta (1 - \Omega)$ adds cross-firm internalization weighted by the conduct parameter.

Generate three synthetic datasets corresponding to $\theta = 0$ (Bertrand-Nash), $\theta = 0.5$ (partial collusion), and $\theta = 1$ (full collusion). For each conduct regime, solve for equilibrium prices given the same marginal costs and demand parameters from Exercise 1, varying only the conduct assumption. Observe that collusive equilibria yield higher prices, higher markups, and lower consumer surplus compared to competitive equilibria. Store each dataset separately: prices, market shares, and product characteristics, withholding the true conduct parameter as the object to be inferred.

Estimate the conduct parameter econometrically by augmenting the cost recovery procedure. In Exercise 1, you inverted the Bertrand-Nash first-order condition to back out marginal costs assuming $\theta = 0$. Now, estimate $\theta$ jointly with marginal cost parameters by treating the first-order condition as a moment restriction. Specifically, posit a marginal cost function $mc_j = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 w_j + \omega_j$, where $w_j$ are observed cost shifters (e.g., input prices, factor endowments) and $\omega_j$ is a cost shock orthogonal to demand. Substitute this cost function into the conduct-augmented first-order condition and estimate $\gamma$ and $\theta$ via GMM, using cost shifters $w_j$ as instruments for both the cost function and the markup equation. The moment conditions are $E[\omega_j \cdot z_j] = 0$ for instruments $z_j$ and $E[\text{FOC}(p, s, \hat{mc}, \theta) \cdot z_j] = 0$, where FOC denotes the pricing first-order condition residual.

Implement the conduct parameter estimation in R using the `gmm` package or in Python via `scipy.optimize` with a custom GMM objective. Define the moment function as a stacked vector: the cost function residual $\omega_j = mc_j - \gamma_0 - \gamma_1 w_j$ and the pricing equation residual $\text{FOC}_j = s_j + [(\Omega + \theta (1 - \Omega)) \odot \Delta](p - mc)_j$. ## Operational Reference Guide & Diagnostic Checklist

This reference guide consolidates the critical diagnostic protocols, troubleshooting procedures, and empirical best practices for industrial organization structural estimation. It serves as a rapid operational manual when confronting identification failures, convergence problems, and robustness challenges in professional antitrust economic consulting and regulatory analysis.

### Demand Estimation Diagnostics

**Instrument Validity and Strength:** First-stage F-statistic must exceed 10 for strong identification; values below 5 indicate weak instruments requiring additional cost shifters or Hausman instruments. Compute the Kleibergen-Paap rk statistic for under-identification: rejection confirms that instruments are relevant. Conduct the Hansen J-test for over-identification: p-value above 0.10 suggests instruments satisfy exogeneity. If J-test rejects, sequentially drop instruments and re-test to identify the offending endogenous instrument.

**Elasticity Plausibility Bounds:** Own-price elasticities for differentiated consumer products typically range from -0.5 to -5. Elasticities outside this range flag misspecification or identification failure. Extremely inelastic demand (elasticity near zero) suggests inadequate price variation or collinearity between price and quality. Explosively elastic demand (elasticity below -10) indicates weak instruments amplifying measurement error. Cross-price elasticities should be positive for substitutes and small in magnitude relative to own-price elasticities, except for very close substitutes where cross-elasticities can approach half the own-elasticity.

**Marginal Cost Recovery Validation:** Backed-out marginal costs must be positive and less than observed prices. Negative marginal costs indicate specification failure—wrong competitive conduct assumption, demand misspecification, or insufficient instrument strength. Marginal costs exceeding prices signal implausible markups (negative margins), suggesting the pricing first-order condition is violated, possibly due to dynamic pricing, capacity constraints, or non-Nash behavior. Cross-reference recovered costs with industry benchmarks: if backed-out costs deviate from reported COGS or engineering cost estimates by more than 30%, revisit the demand specification or conduct assumption.

### Equilibrium Computation Troubleshooting

**Convergence Failure in Fixed-Point Iteration:** If the pricing system fails to converge after 1000 iterations, reduce the step size by introducing a damping factor: $p^{(t+1)} = \lambda p^{\text{new}} + (1 - \lambda) p^{(t)}$ with $\lambda = 0.5$. If convergence remains elusive, switch to Newton-Raphson with analytical Jacobian. If Newton diverges, check for multiple equilibria by running the solver from 10–20 random initial price vectors and comparing the resulting equilibria. Select the equilibrium with the lowest average markup or highest consumer surplus as the focal equilibrium.

**Singular Ownership Matrix:** The matrix $(\Omega \odot \Delta)$ may become near-singular when products have extreme market shares (near zero or one) or when the product space is highly collinear. Regularize by adding a small ridge penalty $(\Omega \odot \Delta + \epsilon I)$ with $\epsilon = 10^{-4}$ or by trimming products with shares below 0.1%. If singularity persists, aggregate products within firms into composite "inside goods" to reduce dimensionality, sacrificing product-level granularity for numerical stability.

**Negative Post-Merger Prices or Shares:** If the post-merger equilibrium solver produces negative prices or market shares, the model has failed fundamentally. Diagnose by checking whether marginal costs are credible: implausibly high marginal costs force the solver into infeasible regions. Re-estimate costs under alternative specifications. If costs are reasonable, the failure suggests the merged entity's portfolio is too large or too homogeneous, generating extreme internalization that overshoots competitive pricing. Introduce capacity constraints or product differentiation to restore equilibrium existence.

### Conduct Parameter Estimation Diagnostics

**Weak Identification of Conduct:** Plot the GMM objective function over $\theta \in [0,1]$. A flat objective indicates weak identification—cost and conduct parameters trade off such that multiple $(\gamma, \theta)$ pairs fit the data equally well. Identification requires independent variation in costs and markups. Strengthen identification by including additional cost shifters, exploiting time-series variation in competitive intensity (e.g., before and after market liberalization), or imposing partial identification bounds on $\theta$ based on auxiliary evidence.

**Conduct Parameter Out of Bounds:** If $\hat{\theta} < 0$ or $\hat{\theta} > 1$, the model is misspecified. Negative conduct parameters are economically meaningless. Conduct exceeding one implies supra-collusive behavior (firms coordinate to harm themselves), also implausible. Investigate whether the demand specification is wrong (logit when nested logit is appropriate), whether the competitive game is mischaracterized (Bertrand assumed when Cournot prevails), or whether dynamic pricing or capacity constraints invalidate the static Nash assumption.

**Conduct Parameter Imprecision:** Standard errors on $\hat{\theta}$ exceeding 0.3 indicate the conduct parameter is poorly estimated. Confidence intervals spanning [0, 1] provide no informative inference. Report bounds rather than point estimates: "the data are consistent with conduct parameters between 0.2 and 0.8, reflecting ambiguity between moderately competitive and partially collusive behavior." Do not over-interpret precise point estimates when standard errors are large.

### Merger Simulation Robustness

**Sensitivity to Demand Specification:** Re-run merger simulations under logit, nested logit, and BLP demand. If predicted price effects swing by more than 50% across specifications, the simulation is fragile. Report the range of effects rather than a single point estimate. Document which specification best fits the data based on out-of-sample prediction or information criteria (AIC, BIC).

**Efficiency Defense Calibration:** Compute the threshold marginal cost reduction that neutralizes the anticompetitive price effect. If this threshold is implausibly high (exceeding 30%), the merger's anticompetitive harm is robust to reasonable efficiency claims. If the threshold is low (below 5%), even modest efficiencies justify the merger. Report the threshold explicitly with the caveat that only merger-specific efficiencies (cost reductions achievable only through the merger, not via internal restructuring) count under antitrust standards.

**Diversion Ratio Cross-Validation:** Compare computed diversion ratios from demand estimation to natural experiment estimates (if available) from temporary stockouts, regional price variation, or discrete choice surveys. Structural diversion ratios should align with reduced-form switching evidence. Discrepancies suggest substitution patterns are misestimated, undermining merger simulation validity.

### Model Misspecification Detection

**Residual Correlation Tests:** Compute the spatial or product-line correlation matrix of demand residuals $\hat{\xi}_j$. If residuals cluster by geographic region, brand family, or product tier, the independence assumption underlying logit demand is violated. Residual clustering suggests unobserved quality shocks are correlated across related products, requiring nested logit or random coefficients to model properly.

**Over-Fitting vs. Under-Fitting Trade-Off:** Compare in-sample fit (R-squared, log-likelihood) to out-of-sample prediction accuracy. High in-sample fit with poor out-of-sample performance indicates over-fitting—too many parameters chasing noise. Conversely, poor fit on both dimensions suggests under-fitting—missing key variables or functional form misspecification. Use cross-validation or information criteria to select the appropriate model complexity.

**Specification Test Battery:** Run a suite of tests: (1) Hausman test comparing OLS to IV estimates—rejection confirms endogeneity; (2) Durbin-Wu-Hausman test for exogeneity of suspected endogenous regressors; (3) Sargan-Hansen J-test for over-identifying restrictions; (4) heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors to guard against misspecified error structure; (5) reset test for functional form misspecification. Document which tests pass and which fail, using failures to guide model refinement.

### Regulatory Communication Protocol

**Framing Empirical Uncertainty:** Competition authorities require honest assessment of estimation uncertainty. Report point estimates with confidence intervals, conduct robustness checks across specifications, and quantify the range of plausible competitive effects. Avoid false precision: a merger simulation predicting a 3.7% price increase is meaningless if the 95% confidence interval spans [0.5%, 7.2%]. Report ranges and emphasize directional findings (price increase likely) over spurious precision.

**Disclosure of Identification Assumptions:** Structural estimates rely on non-testable assumptions—instrument exogeneity, correct specification of competitive conduct, functional form of demand. Document these assumptions explicitly. If identification rests on a single controversial assumption, conduct sensitivity analysis relaxing that assumption and report how conclusions change. Adversarial legal proceedings will challenge your identification strategy; pre-emptive robustness analysis defends against such attacks.

**Distinction Between Statistical and Economic Significance:** A statistically significant price increase may be economically trivial (0.1% price rise on a product with 0.5% market share), while a statistically insignificant but large point estimate (5% price increase with wide standard errors) may warrant regulatory concern under precautionary principles. Discuss both statistical precision and economic magnitude when advising authorities.

Internalize these diagnostics to ensure your structural analyses survive peer review, adversarial scrutiny, and the evidential standards of antitrust enforcement.
