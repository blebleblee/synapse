# Lesson 3: Wage Determination, Compensating Differentials, Discrimination

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The neoclassical model of perfectly competitive labor markets predicts that workers with identical productive characteristics should earn identical wages, yet empirical wage distributions exhibit substantial variance even after controlling for observable human capital measures. This persistent heterogeneity arises from three fundamental channels that extend beyond the simple marginal productivity framework: compensating differentials that equilibrate labor markets across non-wage job attributes, systematic earnings returns to accumulated human capital that compound through experience and training, and discrimination mechanisms that create wedges between productivity and compensation for identifiable demographic groups.

The hedonic wage framework, pioneered by Sherwin Rosen in his 1974 equilibrium model, treats wages as implicit prices for bundles of job characteristics rather than payments for homogeneous labor input. A worker choosing between two occupations compares not merely nominal wage rates but the entire package of attributes: physical risk exposure, shift schedules, workplace autonomy, promotion prospects, geographic location, and employer-provided benefits. Compensating differentials arise when employers must offer wage premiums to attract workers into jobs with undesirable non-pecuniary characteristics or when workers accept wage discounts for positions with valued amenities. The equilibrium differential equates the marginal rate of substitution between wages and job attributes for the marginal worker with the marginal rate of transformation for the marginal firm.

Consider the fundamental hedonic wage equation for a worker $i$ in occupation $j$ offering attribute vector $\mathbf{x}_j$:

$$
w_{ij} = w_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_k x_{jk} + \epsilon_{ij}
$$

where $w_0$ represents baseline compensation for standard working conditions, $\beta_k$ captures the implicit price or compensating differential for attribute $k$, and $\epsilon_{ij}$ aggregates idiosyncratic match quality. The coefficient $\beta_k$ measures the market-clearing wage adjustment required to compensate workers for a marginal unit change in attribute $x_k$ holding other characteristics constant. For occupational fatality risk measured in deaths per 100,000 workers annually, empirical hedonic wage studies consistently estimate positive risk premiums between $\$50,000$ and $\$300,000$ per statistical life, with substantial variation across sample periods, occupational coding schemes, and econometric specifications.

The identification challenge in estimating hedonic wage functions stems from non-random sorting of workers across occupations. Workers with higher risk tolerance, lower time preference, or stronger preferences for workplace autonomy select into jobs offering those attributes, generating correlation between unobserved worker characteristics and observed job attributes. This selection mechanism biases ordinary least squares estimates of compensating differentials unless researchers instrument job attributes with exogenous variation in technology, regulation, or market structure. The value of a statistical life derived from hedonic wage regressions represents the wage-risk tradeoff for the marginal worker indifferent between safe and risky occupations, not the average valuation across the entire workforce. Workers in high-risk jobs have revealed preferences indicating lower willingness to pay for safety, so their observed risk premiums understate the compensating differential required to induce low-risk workers into hazardous employment.

The Mincer earnings function, formulated by Jacob Mincer in his 1974 monograph *Schooling, Experience, and Earnings*, decomposes log wage variation into components attributable to formal education and labor market experience. The canonical specification takes the semilogarithmic form:

$$
\ln(w_i) = \alpha + \beta_s S_i + \beta_1 \text{Exp}_i + \beta_2 \text{Exp}_i^2 + u_i
$$

where $S_i$ denotes years of completed schooling, $\text{Exp}_i$ represents potential labor market experience typically proxied by age minus schooling minus six, and the quadratic term captures concave age-earnings profiles arising from depreciation of human capital and declining investment intensity over the lifecycle. The schooling coefficient $\beta_s$ estimates the percentage wage return to an additional year of education, commonly interpreted as the private rate of return to human capital investment assuming constant marginal returns and zero option value of continued schooling.

The theoretical foundation for the Mincer equation derives from the human capital investment model where individuals allocate time between market work generating current earnings and training activities that raise future productivity. In a simplified two-period framework, the present discounted value of lifetime earnings for a worker investing $s$ years in schooling is:

$$
V(s) = \int_s^T e^{-rt} w(s) \, dt
$$

where $w(s)$ represents the wage function conditional on schooling, $r$ denotes the discount rate, and $T$ marks retirement age. Optimizing individuals choose schooling duration to equate the marginal cost of foregone earnings with the marginal present value of enhanced future wages. Under the assumptions of constant returns to schooling and infinite horizon approximation, this first-order condition yields the Mincerian log-linear relationship between schooling and earnings with slope coefficient equal to the internal rate of return on education.

Empirical estimates of the Mincer schooling coefficient range from 0.05 to 0.15 depending on country, cohort, and estimation methodology, implying that each additional year of education raises earnings by five to fifteen percent on average. These estimates face severe endogeneity concerns since schooling decisions correlate with unobserved ability, family background, and non-cognitive skills that independently affect productivity. Instrumental variable strategies exploiting compulsory schooling laws, distance to college, or draft lottery status as exogenous shifters of educational attainment generally produce larger return estimates than ordinary least squares, suggesting that individuals induced into additional schooling by instruments have higher marginal returns than the average student or that ability bias in cross-sectional regressions is negative due to comparative advantage sorting.

The experience profile coefficients $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ capture the dynamic accumulation and depreciation of general human capital over the working life. Positive $\beta_1$ and negative $\beta_2$ generate the characteristic concave age-earnings profile observed in cross-sectional wage data, with earnings growth rates declining monotonically as workers age. The concavity arises from two mechanisms: older workers optimally reduce training investments as the remaining payoff horizon shrinks, and previously accumulated skills depreciate through technological obsolescence or biological aging. The turning point of the quadratic experience profile occurs at $\text{Exp}^* = -\beta_1 / (2\beta_2)$, typically falling between 25 and 35 years of experience depending on occupation and education level.

Recent cohort-based panel data studies reveal substantial heterogeneity in age-earnings profiles across education groups and birth cohorts. College graduates exhibit steeper earnings growth through mid-career and later peak ages compared to high school graduates, consistent with greater investment in firm-specific training and occupation-specific expertise. Successive birth cohorts entering the labor market after 1980 have experienced flatter age-earnings profiles than earlier cohorts at comparable ages, suggesting reduced returns to experience driven by faster technological change, declining employer-provided training, or increased labor market turbulence.

Discrimination represents systematic differential treatment of workers based on demographic characteristics unrelated to productive capacity. Two foundational theoretical frameworks dominate the labor economics literature: Gary Becker's taste-based discrimination model and the statistical discrimination model developed by Edmund Phelps and Kenneth Arrow. These approaches generate contrasting predictions about the persistence of wage gaps, the role of market competition, and the effectiveness of anti-discrimination policy interventions.

Becker's taste-based discrimination model, formulated in his 1957 doctoral dissertation *The Economics of Discrimination*, posits that employers, coworkers, or customers hold prejudicial preferences against demographic groups such that they experience disutility from interaction with minority workers. An employer with discrimination coefficient $d$ toward group $M$ acts as if minority workers impose psychic cost $d$ per unit of labor, so the effective wage from the employer's perspective is $w_M + d$ even when the actual wage paid equals $w_M$. Profit-maximizing discriminatory employers hire minority workers only if the minority wage discount exceeds the discrimination coefficient: $w_M \leq w_D - d$, where $w_D$ denotes the majority group wage.

In competitive equilibrium with heterogeneous employer discrimination coefficients distributed according to density $f(d)$, minority workers sort into employment with the least prejudiced firms. The marginal employer indifferent between majority and minority workers has discrimination coefficient $d^*$ satisfying:

$$
w_M = w_D - d^*
$$

The equilibrium wage gap equals the discrimination coefficient of the marginal employer, which depends on the relative supply of minority workers and the distribution of employer prejudice. If non-discriminatory employers ($d = 0$) have sufficient hiring capacity to absorb the entire minority workforce, competitive forces eliminate wage differentials despite widespread prejudice among other firms. Conversely, if all employers harbor positive discrimination coefficients, minorities face economy-wide wage penalties determined by market-clearing conditions.

The critical prediction of Becker's model is that taste-based discrimination should dissipate over time in competitive markets through entry of non-discriminatory firms earning higher profits by hiring equally productive minority workers at discount wages. Empirical persistence of substantial racial and gender wage gaps six decades after publication challenges this competitive arbitrage mechanism, suggesting either that labor markets exhibit pervasive frictions preventing profit-maximizing entry or that alternative discrimination channels beyond employer taste operate in real-world settings.

Statistical discrimination models provide an alternative framework where differential treatment arises from rational Bayesian inference under imperfect information about individual worker productivity. When employers observe demographic group membership but not precise productivity prior to hiring, they rationally condition wage offers and employment decisions on group average productivity and the precision of group-level versus individual-level signals. This mechanism generates discriminatory outcomes even when employers hold no prejudicial preferences and groups have identical mean productivity.

Consider an employer observing noisy signal $s_i = \theta_i + \epsilon_i$ about worker $i$'s true productivity $\theta_i$, where $\epsilon_i \sim N(0, \sigma_\epsilon^2)$ represents measurement error. If the employer also observes the worker's demographic group $g \in \{A, B\}$ and holds prior beliefs $\theta | g \sim N(\mu_g, \sigma_g^2)$, the Bayesian posterior expectation conditional on both group membership and individual signal is:

$$
E[\theta_i | s_i, g] = \frac{\sigma_g^2}{\sigma_g^2 + \sigma_\epsilon^2} s_i + \frac{\sigma_\epsilon^2}{\sigma_g^2 + \sigma_\epsilon^2} \mu_g
$$

The posterior weights the individual signal and group mean in proportion to their relative precision. Crucially, even if groups have identical mean productivity ($\mu_A = \mu_B$), differences in within-group variance ($\sigma_A^2 \neq \sigma_B^2$) generate differential treatment. Workers from the low-variance group receive wage offers more heavily weighted toward individual signals, while workers from the high-variance group face greater regression toward the group mean. High-ability workers from the high-variance group suffer wage penalties relative to observationally equivalent workers from the low-variance group, while low-ability workers from the high-variance group benefit from pooling.

Statistical discrimination has particularly pernicious dynamic consequences when productivity is endogenous to investment incentives. If workers from the high-variance group anticipate that employers will heavily discount individual signals in favor of group stereotypes, they face reduced returns to human capital investment, creating rational disincentives for skill acquisition. This generates a self-fulfilling equilibrium where initial group differences in signal precision, even if arbitrary or historically contingent, perpetuate through depressed investment and widening productivity distributions. Breaking such equilibria requires either credible signaling mechanisms that separate high-ability workers from group stereotypes or policy interventions that subsidize human capital investment for disadvantaged groups.

The Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition methodology, developed independently by Ronald Oaxaca and Alan Blinder in 1973, provides the canonical empirical framework for partitioning observed wage gaps between demographic groups into components attributable to differences in productive characteristics versus differences in returns to characteristics. The decomposition proceeds by estimating separate Mincer-style wage regressions for majority group $D$ and minority group $M$:

$$
\ln(w_D) = \mathbf{X}_D \boldsymbol{\beta}_D + \epsilon_D
$$

$$
\ln(w_M) = \mathbf{X}_M \boldsymbol{\beta}_M + \epsilon_M
$$

where $\mathbf{X}$ includes human capital measures, occupational controls, and other productivity-related covariates. The mean log wage differential decomposes as:

$$
\overline{\ln(w_D)} - \overline{\ln(w_M)} = (\overline{\mathbf{X}}_D - \overline{\mathbf{X}}_M) \boldsymbol{\beta}_D + \overline{\mathbf{X}}_M (\boldsymbol{\beta}_D - \boldsymbol{\beta}_M)
$$

The first term measures the portion of the wage gap explained by differences in observable characteristics evaluated at majority group returns. The second term captures the unexplained component arising from differential returns to identical characteristics, commonly interpreted as an upper bound on discrimination. Alternative decompositions weight the characteristic differences by minority group coefficients or pooled coefficients, producing numerically distinct but conceptually equivalent partitions.

Empirical Oaxaca-Blinder decompositions of the Black-White wage gap in the United States consistently attribute 30 to 50 percent of raw log wage differentials to observable human capital disparities, with the unexplained component accounting for the remainder. The gender wage gap exhibits greater explanatory power from observables, particularly occupational segregation and work interruptions associated with childrearing, though substantial unexplained portions persist even after extensive covariate controls. The interpretation of the unexplained component as discrimination requires strong assumptions: it aggregates all omitted variables correlated with group membership, including pre-market skill differences not captured by years of schooling, differential school quality, non-cognitive skills, and discriminatory barriers to human capital accumulation.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The hedonic wage estimation architecture requires careful attention to sample construction, functional form specification, and identification strategy to recover consistent estimates of implicit prices for job attributes. The core econometric challenge is that workers and firms endogenously sort across attribute bundles based on heterogeneous preferences and production technologies, generating correlation between job characteristics and unobserved worker quality. Standard cross-sectional hedonic wage regressions identify implicit prices only under restrictive assumptions about the joint distribution of tastes and technologies that are unlikely to hold in practice.

The cleanest identification of compensating differentials exploits sharp exogenous changes in job attributes driven by technological innovation, regulatory mandates, or natural experiments that shift the menu of available attribute bundles without directly affecting worker composition. The introduction of airbags in commercial trucking during the 1980s reduced occupational fatality risk by approximately 25 percent for long-haul drivers without altering other job characteristics, providing quasi-experimental variation to estimate the value of statistical life from wage adjustments. Difference-in-differences specifications comparing wage changes for affected occupations relative to unaffected control occupations before and after airbag mandates yield value of statistical life estimates near the upper end of the $\$50,000$ to $\$300,000$ range, consistent with larger wage-risk tradeoffs when risk changes occur exogenously rather than through worker sorting.

The functional form relating wages to continuous job attributes requires theoretical guidance from the underlying utility and production structure. If workers have constant absolute risk aversion preferences and firms face quadratic costs of reducing workplace hazards, the hedonic wage function takes a linear form in risk levels. Alternative preference specifications generate nonlinear hedonic price schedules where the marginal compensating differential varies with the level of risk exposure. Empirical hedonic wage studies typically estimate both linear and quadratic specifications, testing for curvature in the wage-risk relationship. Statistically significant quadratic terms often reflect sample composition effects from worker sorting rather than genuine nonlinearity in the hedonic price function, underscoring the importance of controlling for detailed worker observables and occupation fixed effects.

The multidimensional nature of job attribute bundles poses additional identification challenges when attributes co-vary across occupations. High-risk jobs frequently offer irregular schedules, limited advancement prospects, and geographic isolation, making it difficult to disentangle the compensating differential for any single attribute. Principal component analysis or factor analysis can reduce dimensionality by constructing orthogonalized attribute indices, but interpretation of implicit prices for synthetic factors lacks economic clarity. Hedonic wage specifications incorporating rich vectors of job characteristics require large cross-sectional samples with detailed occupational coding and face multicollinearity when attributes are strongly correlated.

The Mincer earnings function estimation proceeds via ordinary least squares regression of log wages on schooling, experience, and experience squared. The semilogarithmic specification has several appealing properties: the schooling coefficient directly estimates percentage returns to education, the functional form accommodates proportional wage growth over the lifecycle, and the model exhibits homoskedasticity in percentage terms even when wage levels display increasing variance with age. The log transformation also partially addresses right-skewness in the unconditional wage distribution and reduces sensitivity to extreme outliers, though researchers must carefully consider sample truncation for workers with zero or negative earnings.

Sample selection issues arise when wage observations are missing non-randomly. Labor force participation decisions correlate with education, experience, and unobserved productivity, particularly for married women and older workers. Heckman's two-step selection correction models wage equation residuals as truncated normal variates conditional on participation, estimating the participation probit and wage regression jointly to recover consistent coefficients. The identifying assumption that exclusion restrictions affect participation decisions but not wage levels conditional on participation is often tenuous, requiring variables like non-labor income, fertility, or spousal characteristics that plausibly influence work decisions without directly entering the wage determination process.

The endogeneity of schooling to unobserved ability represents the central econometric obstacle to causal interpretation of Mincer returns to education. Individuals with higher cognitive ability, stronger non-cognitive skills, or more advantageous family backgrounds acquire more education and earn higher wages even conditional on schooling, generating upward bias in ordinary least squares estimates if these omitted factors have positive partial effects on both outcomes. The standard instrumental variable approach instruments schooling with policy-induced variation in education access or costs: compulsory schooling laws that force low-propensity students to complete additional years, proximity to colleges that reduce enrollment costs, or cohort-level shocks like draft lottery vulnerability that affect college attendance.

The interpretation of instrumental variable estimates as local average treatment effects (LATE) has reshaped understanding of heterogeneous returns to education. Compulsory schooling instruments identify returns for marginal students induced into additional education by legal requirements, who likely have below-average returns compared to students who would attend voluntarily. College proximity instruments capture returns for students on the margin of enrollment whose college-going decisions respond to distance costs. These LATE estimates generally exceed ordinary least squares, suggesting either that ability bias is negative for marginal students due to comparative advantage sorting or that marginal returns to schooling exceed average returns in the presence of credit constraints.

The experience profile estimation strategy must confront cohort, age, and time effects that confound identification of lifecycle wage growth patterns. Cross-sectional Mincer regressions identify age-earnings profiles from between-worker variation under the implicit assumption that older workers today experienced the same wage-experience relationship when young that current young workers face. This assumption fails when technical change, institutional shifts, or macroeconomic conditions generate cohort-specific earnings trajectories. Panel data following workers over time allow estimation of within-worker wage growth rates net of cohort effects, though age-period-cohort collinearity prevents full identification without parametric restrictions.

Recent synthetic cohort methods construct pseudo-panels by grouping workers into cells defined by birth year and education, tracking cell means across repeated cross-sections to estimate cohort-specific age-earnings profiles. These approaches reveal substantial flattening of experience profiles for post-1980 cohorts relative to earlier generations at comparable ages, particularly for workers with less than college education. The decline in returns to experience coincides with rising wage inequality, suggesting that skill-biased technical change and declining employer-sponsored training have increased the importance of formal schooling relative to on-the-job learning.

The Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition implementation requires several practical decisions about functional form, coefficient weighting, and covariate selection. The baseline specification estimates separate log wage regressions for each demographic group via ordinary least squares, computing the decomposition using group-specific coefficients and sample means. Alternative weighting schemes use coefficients from a pooled regression including a group indicator, coefficients from the non-discriminated group, or coefficients estimated at the sample means of the two groups. These choices affect the magnitude of explained versus unexplained components but do not alter the fundamental insight that observables account for only a portion of raw wage gaps.

The choice of covariates involves a tradeoff between explanatory power and endogeneity. Including detailed occupation and industry indicators increases the explained component by capturing segregation into lower-paying jobs, but occupational placement itself may reflect discrimination in hiring, promotion, or steering. A narrow decomposition using only pre-market human capital variables like education and potential experience isolates discrimination in wage-setting conditional on qualifications, while a broad decomposition including occupation, industry, and union status attributes differential sorting across jobs to explained differences. Both approaches provide valuable perspectives: the narrow decomposition bounds pure wage discrimination for observationally equivalent workers, while the broad decomposition quantifies total labor market disadvantage including occupational segregation.

The detailed Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition partitions the explained component into contributions from each covariate, revealing which observable differences account for the largest shares of the wage gap. For the gender wage gap, experience and tenure differences arising from work interruptions for childrearing consistently emerge as dominant contributors, reflecting permanent wage scarring from career breaks and reduced incentives for firm-specific investment. Education differences contribute little to the contemporary gender wage gap given near-parity in college attainment rates, though field-of-study differences with women concentrated in lower-paying humanities and social science majors rather than engineering and computer science account for additional wage variation.

Quantile regression extensions of the Oaxaca-Blinder framework estimate decompositions at different points of the conditional wage distribution, testing whether discrimination intensifies or attenuates at the upper or lower tails. The Black-White wage gap exhibits relative stability across quantiles, with similar explained and unexplained components for low-wage and high-wage workers, suggesting that discrimination operates uniformly across the skill distribution. The gender wage gap displays greater compression at lower quantiles where minimum wages and collective bargaining institutions bind more tightly, widening substantially in the upper tail where discretionary pay-setting and negotiation dynamics favor male workers.

The identification assumption underlying the causal interpretation of unexplained wage gaps as discrimination is that all productivity-relevant characteristics are either observed or uncorrelated with group membership conditional on observables. This assumption fails if groups differ in unmeasured dimensions such as school quality, neighborhood effects, non-cognitive skills, or pre-market discrimination affecting human capital accumulation. Audit studies sending fictitious resumes with randomized names signaling race or gender to employers provide more credible evidence of hiring discrimination, consistently documenting callback rate penalties for minority applicants with equivalent qualifications. These correspondence tests identify discrimination in the initial hiring stage but cannot measure within-firm wage discrimination conditional on employment.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Empirical estimation of hedonic wage functions confronts severe measurement challenges in constructing comprehensive, accurate job attribute data. Researchers typically merge worker-level wage data from household surveys with occupation-level attribute measures from external sources such as the Occupational Information Network (O\*NET), Bureau of Labor Statistics fatality statistics, or establishment-level workplace safety inspections. This two-sample instrumental variable strategy introduces measurement error in the attribute variables, attenuating estimated compensating differentials toward zero. The attenuation bias is most severe when within-occupation variance in attributes is large relative to between-occupation variance, as individual workers within the same occupation code face heterogeneous risk exposure, schedule flexibility, or autonomy that aggregate measures fail to capture.

Linked employer-employee data combining administrative wage records with establishment-specific attribute measures provide cleaner identification by exploiting within-occupation, across-establishment variation in job characteristics. Danish and Swedish registry data linking workers to firms with measured safety records, shift patterns, and workplace policies enable estimation of compensating differentials controlling for detailed worker fixed effects and occupation-industry interactions. These studies document larger risk premiums than conventional cross-sectional hedonic regressions, confirming that measurement error and worker sorting attenuate standard estimates. The challenge with administrative data is limited coverage of non-pecuniary job attributes beyond basic safety and scheduling variables, restricting analysis to a narrow subset of the full attribute space workers and firms trade off.

Optimization of hedonic wage estimation requires balancing sample size against attribute measurement precision. Large national surveys like the Current Population Survey or American Community Survey provide representative wage samples but lack detailed job attribute data beyond broad occupation codes. Smaller specialized surveys like the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth or Panel Study of Income Dynamics collect richer attribute information but suffer from limited statistical power and non-representative samples. Researchers increasingly adopt hierarchical modeling strategies that estimate hedonic wage functions in large samples using coarse attributes, then scale coefficients using validation samples with detailed measures to correct for attenuation bias.

The Mincer earnings function estimation is highly sensitive to sample restrictions and variable definitions that researchers often implement without adequate justification or robustness checks. Restricting the sample to full-time workers excludes part-time employment where returns to education and experience may differ, inducing selection bias if part-time status correlates with unobserved productivity. Defining experience as age minus schooling minus six assumes continuous labor force participation with no career interruptions, severely mis-measuring actual experience for women and minorities with non-monotonic work histories. Alternative experience measures based on retrospective employment calendars or Social Security earnings records provide more accurate lifecycle profiles but require specialized longitudinal data.

The quadratic functional form for experience imposes strong parametric restrictions that may inadequately capture nonlinear lifecycle wage dynamics. Quartic or quintic experience polynomials provide greater flexibility at the cost of multicollinearity and sensitivity to outliers at extreme ages. Spline regression specifications with knots at 5, 10, 20, and 30 years of experience allow discontinuous slope changes at key career stages: the transition from entry-level to mid-career, peak earnings periods, and late-career wage trajectories approaching retirement. Comparing wage profiles across parametric specifications tests the robustness of estimated peak ages and lifecycle wage growth rates.

The log wage specification assumes constant percentage returns to education across the wage distribution, but quantile regression estimates reveal substantial heterogeneity with larger returns for high-wage workers. This pattern may reflect complementarity between schooling and unobserved ability, with high-ability workers gaining more from education through access to complex occupations or managerial roles. Alternatively, institutional factors like credential requirements and occupational licensing create discrete wage jumps at degree thresholds that proportional log wage specifications average across continuous years. Estimating returns at degree completion points (high school, bachelor's, master's, doctorate) rather than continuous years frequently produces larger coefficients, suggesting sheepskin effects where the credential itself generates returns beyond the knowledge acquired.

Measurement error in self-reported schooling attenuates Mincer coefficients toward zero, particularly in samples of older workers with recall error about educational attainment decades earlier. Validation studies comparing survey responses to transcript data document error rates of 5 to 10 percent for years of schooling and 15 to 25 percent for degree completion, with more severe misreporting among workers with some college attendance who incorrectly claim bachelor's degrees. Instrumental variable estimation using parental education or sibling schooling as instruments can partially correct for classical measurement error, though these instruments also capture family background effects that confound ability bias adjustments.

The interpretation of instrumental variable estimates as causal returns to education requires careful evaluation of instrument validity. Compulsory schooling laws affect educational attainment but may also correlate with unobserved state-level economic conditions, labor market institutions, or education quality that independently influence earnings. Difference-in-differences specifications comparing affected cohorts before and after law changes to unaffected cohorts control for these confounds but require parallel trends assumptions that are difficult to verify with limited pre-treatment periods. Distance to college instruments raise concerns about differential selection of students who attend college when nearby versus those who would attend regardless of distance, violating the monotonicity assumption underlying local average treatment effect interpretation.

Oaxaca-Blinder decompositions face fundamental identification limits rooted in the path-dependence of detailed decompositions. The explained and unexplained components depend on the ordering of covariates and the choice of reference group coefficients, with different orderings producing numerically distinct results when covariates are correlated. The Shapley value decomposition addresses this path-dependence by averaging over all possible orderings, providing a unique partition of the explained component across covariates. This approach substantially increases computational burden for specifications with more than a handful of covariates, requiring calculation of $K!$ regressions for $K$ covariates, but ensures decomposition results are invariant to arbitrary ordering choices.

The standard error calculation for Oaxaca-Blinder decompositions requires accounting for sampling variability in both coefficient estimates and group means. Researchers commonly report standard errors computed via the delta method under the assumption of independent sampling across groups, but this ignores covariance between majority and minority coefficient estimates when estimated in the same sample. Bootstrap resampling methods provide more accurate standard errors by repeatedly sampling workers within groups, re-estimating regressions, and constructing empirical distributions of explained and unexplained components. Clustered bootstrap procedures that resample at higher levels of aggregation (state, industry, or occupation) account for correlation in regression residuals within clusters.

The dynamic extension of Oaxaca-Blinder decompositions to panel data allows decomposition of wage gap changes over time into components attributable to changing endowments, changing returns, and changing group composition. The Juhn-Murphy-Pierce decomposition partitions wage gap trends into quantities effect (changes in $\overline{\mathbf{X}}_D - \overline{\mathbf{X}}_M$), prices effect (changes in $\boldsymbol{\beta}_D - \boldsymbol{\beta}_M$), and unobservables effect (changes in residual distributions). Applying this framework to Black-White wage gap trends reveals that narrowing educational attainment differences and declining returns to education compressed the gap during the 1970s and 1980s, while stagnant progress in the 1990s and 2000s reflects stable endowment differences and rising residual inequality.

Machine learning methods provide tools for flexible covariate adjustment and prediction of counterfactual wages under alternative group assignments. Causal forest algorithms estimate heterogeneous treatment effects of group membership conditional on rich covariate vectors, identifying subpopulations where discrimination is most severe. Doubly robust estimators combine outcome regression and propensity score weighting to construct consistent discrimination estimates under weaker identifying assumptions than pure regression or reweighting approaches. These methods scale more effectively to high-dimensional covariate spaces but sacrifice interpretability of mechanisms relative to linear decompositions.

The fundamental limitation of all decomposition methods is the impossibility of cleanly separating discrimination from pre-market human capital differences when unobserved skills and preferences correlate with demographic characteristics. Even exhaustive controls for schooling, experience, occupation, and industry cannot rule out discrimination affecting the quality of schooling received, the types of occupations accessible, or the formation of preferences over work versus leisure. Structural models of lifecycle human capital accumulation that explicitly model discrimination at each decision node provide a more coherent framework for welfare analysis and policy evaluation, though they require strong parametric assumptions about preferences, technology, and the form of discrimination that remain contentious.
