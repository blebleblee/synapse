# Lesson 1: Labor Supply, Demand Foundations, Market Equilibrium

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Labor markets represent the most fundamental allocation mechanism in modern economies, governing how individuals trade their time and effort for compensation while firms simultaneously compete for productive capacity. Unlike goods markets where transactions involve discrete units of physical commodities, labor markets trade human capital services through contractual relationships that embed complex principal-agent problems, search frictions, and non-convexities arising from indivisible working hours and firm-specific human capital investments. The equilibrium wage rate emerging from this decentralized matching process determines income distribution across society, aggregate consumption capacity, and the efficiency frontier of resource allocation between leisure and production.

At the microeconomic foundation, individual labor supply decisions emerge from a constrained optimization problem where workers maximize utility over consumption and leisure subject to a budget constraint defined by the wage rate. This optimization yields the canonical labor supply curve, but unlike textbook supply curves for produced goods, the labor supply curve can bend backward at sufficiently high wages due to income effects dominating substitution effects. When wages rise, two opposing forces simultaneously affect labor supply: the substitution effect raises the opportunity cost of leisure, inducing workers to supply more hours, while the income effect allows workers to afford more of all normal goods including leisure, inducing them to supply fewer hours. This duality generates the empirically observed phenomenon where high-income professionals often work fewer hours than mid-income workers despite facing substantially higher hourly wages.

The reservation wage forms the critical threshold below which workers optimally choose zero labor supply. This reservation wage depends on non-labor income sources, the value of home production, unemployment insurance benefits, and the psychic costs of labor market participation including commuting time and workplace disamenities. Heterogeneity in reservation wages across the population generates the extensive margin of labor supply where policy interventions such as earned income tax credits or childcare subsidies shift individuals from non-participation to market work. The distinction between extensive margin responses and intensive margin responses becomes paramount for predicting behavioral responses to tax policy reforms and minimum wage legislation.

On the demand side, firms hire labor according to marginal productivity theory, derived from profit maximization under production function constraints. The firm's labor demand emerges as a derived demand contingent on output prices and the technological parameters governing how labor combines with capital and intermediate inputs to generate revenue. In the short run with fixed capital stock, the law of diminishing marginal returns ensures that the marginal product of labor declines as employment expands, generating a downward-sloping labor demand curve in wage-employment space. The firm hires workers up to the point where the marginal revenue product of labor equals the wage rate, establishing the fundamental equilibrium condition $w = P \cdot MPL$ where $w$ denotes the nominal wage, $P$ the output price, and $MPL$ the marginal product of labor.

This profit-maximization condition reveals that labor demand depends not only on technological productivity but also on product market conditions through the output price term. A negative demand shock in the product market immediately translates into reduced labor demand even when production technology remains unchanged, explaining procyclical employment fluctuations over the business cycle. Furthermore, the elasticity of labor demand with respect to wages depends critically on the elasticity of substitution between labor and capital in the production function, the price elasticity of product demand, and the share of labor costs in total production costs. These structural parameters determine how employment responds to minimum wage policy, payroll tax changes, and immigration-induced shifts in labor supply.

Market equilibrium emerges where aggregate labor supply equals aggregate labor demand at the market-clearing wage. In competitive markets with homogeneous labor, this equilibrium is unique and Pareto efficient, maximizing the sum of consumer and producer surplus. However, labor markets deviate substantially from the frictionless competitive ideal through several empirically relevant channels. Search frictions generate unemployment even in equilibrium as workers and firms engage in costly matching processes with incomplete information about match quality. Wage rigidities arising from efficiency wage considerations, implicit contracts, or union bargaining power prevent wages from adjusting instantaneously to clear markets, generating persistent involuntary unemployment. Monopsony power on the demand side, particularly relevant in geographically isolated labor markets or specialized occupational niches, allows firms to pay wages below marginal revenue product while restricting employment below the competitive level.

The comparative statics of labor market equilibrium provide the analytical framework for evaluating policy interventions. A minimum wage set above the competitive equilibrium generates excess supply if labor demand is sufficiently elastic, but recent empirical evidence from local labor markets suggests demand elasticities may be lower than classical estimates predicted, particularly in low-wage sectors dominated by monopsonistic competition. Payroll taxes formally levied on employers shift the labor demand curve downward by the tax amount, but in competitive equilibrium with elastic labor supply, tax incidence falls primarily on workers through reduced wages rather than on firms through reduced profits. The statutory incidence of taxation diverges sharply from economic incidence, with the latter determined entirely by the relative elasticities of supply and demand according to the fundamental incidence formula $\frac{dw}{dt} = -\frac{\epsilon_D}{\epsilon_S + \epsilon_D}$ where $\epsilon_S$ and $\epsilon_D$ denote supply and demand elasticities respectively.

Immigration shocks provide natural experiments for identifying labor demand elasticities and wage formation mechanisms. When a sudden influx of workers increases labor supply, the short-run impact on native wages depends on the degree of substitutability between immigrant and native labor, the speed of capital adjustment, and the elasticity of product demand. If immigrants are perfect substitutes for natives, simple supply-demand logic predicts wage depression in affected skill groups. However, if immigrants and natives are imperfect substitutes, perhaps due to language barriers or credential recognition issues, then immigration may have minimal impact on native wages while expanding total employment and output. The Mariel Boatlift episode in Miami during 1980, when 125,000 Cuban refugees arrived within months, provided a canonical test case where subsequent research found surprisingly modest wage effects, suggesting either high labor demand elasticity or substantial complementarity between immigrants and native workers.

Dynamic considerations introduce intertemporal substitution in labor supply, where forward-looking workers respond to temporary wage changes differently than permanent wage changes. A temporary wage increase induces strong substitution effects as workers shift hours from future periods to the present when rewards are higher, generating higher labor supply elasticity than permanent wage changes which trigger larger income effects. This intertemporal margin becomes critical for understanding business cycle fluctuations in employment, where procyclical wages combined with intertemporal substitution can generate substantial employment volatility even with modest wage movements. Real business cycle models exploit this mechanism to generate realistic employment fluctuations from productivity shocks, though the quantitative magnitude requires implausibly high intertemporal elasticity of substitution to match observed employment volatility.

Human capital investment decisions add another dimension to labor supply by making current labor market choices dependent on expected future returns. Individuals choose educational attainment by comparing the present discounted value of lifetime earnings across education levels, accounting for direct tuition costs, foregone earnings during schooling, and differential unemployment risk. This forward-looking optimization generates the Mincer earnings equation relating log wages to years of schooling and potential experience, with the schooling coefficient interpreted as the rate of return to education. Estimating this return confronts identification challenges because unobserved ability simultaneously affects both educational attainment and earnings, generating upward bias in ordinary least squares estimates. Instrumental variable approaches exploiting changes in compulsory schooling laws or distance to colleges provide more credible estimates, typically finding returns to schooling around seven to ten percent per year in developed economies.

The hedonic wage function emerges when jobs differ in non-wage attributes such as occupational risk, schedule flexibility, or prestige. Workers with heterogeneous preferences sort across jobs, trading off wages against amenities. Equilibrium requires that no worker can improve utility by switching jobs, generating a hedonic price function $w(z)$ where $z$ represents the vector of job characteristics. The compensating differential for dangerous work, estimated by comparing wages across occupations with different mortality risk holding worker characteristics constant, provides revealed preference estimates of the value of statistical life used in cost-benefit analysis of safety regulations. Empirical estimates suggest compensating differentials of approximately seven to nine million dollars per statistical life in the United States, though estimates vary substantially across estimation methods and samples.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The individual labor supply decision begins with the worker's utility function defined over consumption $c$ and leisure $\ell$, typically specified as $U(c, \ell)$ with standard properties $U_c > 0$, $U_\ell > 0$, $U_{cc} < 0$, and $U_{\ell\ell} < 0$ ensuring positive but diminishing marginal utility in both arguments. The time constraint allocates total available time $T$ between market work $h$ and leisure: $h + \ell = T$. The budget constraint requires that consumption expenditure cannot exceed labor income plus non-labor income: $c = wh + m$ where $w$ denotes the hourly wage and $m$ represents non-labor income from assets, transfers, or other sources. Substituting the time constraint into the budget constraint yields the consolidated constraint $c = w(T - \ell) + m$, or equivalently $c + w\ell = wT + m$, revealing that the wage rate serves as the price of leisure in units of foregone consumption.

The worker maximizes utility subject to this budget constraint, forming the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = U(c, \ell) + \lambda(wT + m - c - w\ell)$. First-order conditions require $U_c = \lambda$ and $U_\ell = \lambda w$, which combine to yield the tangency condition $\frac{U_\ell}{U_c} = w$. This condition states that the marginal rate of substitution between leisure and consumption must equal the wage rate at the optimum. The marginal rate of substitution represents the worker's subjective valuation of leisure in consumption units, while the wage represents the market price of leisure. Optimality requires these to equalize, ensuring the worker cannot improve utility by marginally reallocating time between work and leisure.

The comparative static effect of a wage increase on labor supply decomposes into substitution and income effects through the Slutsky equation. Totally differentiating the first-order conditions and solving for $\frac{dh}{dw}$ yields:

$$\frac{dh}{dw} = \frac{\partial h^c}{\partial w} - h \frac{\partial h}{\partial m}$$

The first term $\frac{\partial h^c}{\partial w}$ represents the substitution effect, holding utility constant, which is unambiguously positive since higher wages increase the opportunity cost of leisure. The second term $-h \frac{\partial h}{\partial m}$ captures the income effect, where the negative sign reflects that wage increases effectively boost income by $h$ dollars, and if leisure is a normal good ($\frac{\partial h}{\partial m} < 0$), this income gain reduces labor supply. The net effect is ambiguous: at low wages the substitution effect typically dominates, generating upward-sloping labor supply, while at high wages the income effect can dominate, producing backward-bending labor supply.

The reservation wage $w^*$ emerges as the minimum wage at which the worker is indifferent between working and not working. At this threshold wage, the utility from working zero hours $U(m, T)$ equals the utility from the optimal choice at wage $w^*$. For wages below the reservation wage, the worker chooses corner solution $h = 0$. The reservation wage increases in non-labor income $m$ since higher income raises the value of non-market time, and decreases in fixed costs of work such as commuting expenses or childcare costs which reduce the net return to market work. Married individuals face joint labor supply decisions where spousal income affects reservation wages, generating the empirical observation that secondary earners exhibit higher labor supply elasticities than primary earners due to their marginal attachment to the labor force.

Empirical estimation of labor supply elasticities confronts identification challenges because wages and hours are simultaneously determined in equilibrium, and observed wage variation reflects both supply and demand shifts. Panel data methods exploiting within-individual wage variation over time provide more credible identification, though measurement error in wages biases elasticity estimates toward zero. Consensus estimates suggest modest labor supply elasticities along the intensive margin for prime-age males, typically in the range of 0.1 to 0.3, indicating that a one percent wage increase raises hours worked by 0.1 to 0.3 percent. Extensive margin elasticities are substantially larger, particularly for married women and older workers near retirement, where labor force participation responds more strongly to wage changes than hours worked conditional on employment.

On the firm side, labor demand derives from profit maximization under technological production constraints. Consider a firm with production function $Y = F(K, L)$ exhibiting constant returns to scale, producing output $Y$ from capital $K$ and labor $L$. The firm faces output price $P$ and input prices $r$ for capital and $w$ for labor, maximizing profit $\pi = PF(K, L) - rK - wL$. In the short run with fixed capital $\bar{K}$, the firm chooses employment $L$ to maximize $\pi = PF(\bar{K}, L) - wL - r\bar{K}$. The first-order condition yields $P \cdot F_L(\bar{K}, L) = w$, requiring the value of marginal product of labor to equal the wage.

This optimal employment condition defines the firm's labor demand function $L^d(w, P, \bar{K})$, which is decreasing in the wage holding output price and capital fixed. The slope of labor demand equals:

$$\frac{\partial L^d}{\partial w} = \frac{1}{P \cdot F_{LL}} < 0$$

The denominator is negative due to diminishing marginal returns ($F_{LL} < 0$), ensuring downward-sloping labor demand. The magnitude of this slope determines the wage elasticity of labor demand $\epsilon_D = \frac{w}{L}\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}$, which depends on the curvature of the production function and the output price. More concave production functions (stronger diminishing returns) generate more elastic labor demand, as do higher output prices which amplify the value of marginal product changes.

In the long run, capital adjusts optimally, introducing substitution between capital and labor. The firm simultaneously chooses $K$ and $L$ to maximize profit, yielding first-order conditions $P \cdot F_K = r$ and $P \cdot F_L = w$. These conditions determine the long-run factor demand functions $K^*(w, r, P)$ and $L^*(w, r, P)$. The long-run labor demand elasticity exceeds the short-run elasticity because firms can substitute capital for labor in response to wage increases. The magnitude of this substitution depends on the elasticity of substitution $\sigma$ in the production function, defined as:

$$\sigma = \frac{d \ln(K/L)}{d \ln(w/r)}$$

Cobb-Douglas production functions exhibit $\sigma = 1$, constant elasticity of substitution (CES) functions allow arbitrary $\sigma$, and Leontief functions impose $\sigma = 0$ with no substitution possible. Empirical estimates of $\sigma$ between capital and labor typically range from 0.4 to 1.0, suggesting moderate substitutability with long-run labor demand elasticities substantially higher than short-run elasticities.

The distinction between conditional and unconditional factor demands clarifies how product market effects amplify labor demand responses. Conditional factor demand $L^c(w, r, \bar{Y})$ holds output fixed, isolating the substitution effect from wage changes. Unconditional factor demand $L^*(w, r, P)$ allows output to adjust optimally through the product market. A wage increase has two effects on unconditional labor demand: the direct substitution effect captured by conditional demand, and the scale effect as firms optimally reduce output in response to higher costs. The scale effect reinforces the substitution effect, making unconditional labor demand more elastic than conditional demand.

Marshall's rules of derived demand formalize the determinants of labor demand elasticity. Labor demand is more elastic when: (1) the elasticity of substitution between labor and other inputs is higher, allowing greater substitution away from labor when wages rise; (2) the elasticity of product demand is higher, amplifying the scale effect as wage-induced cost increases translate to larger output reductions; (3) labor's share in total cost is higher, making cost changes more sensitive to wage movements; and (4) the supply of other inputs is more elastic, facilitating substitution by preventing complementary input prices from rising as labor is replaced. These principles guide empirical predictions about which sectors and occupations exhibit the most sensitive employment responses to wage shocks.

Market equilibrium equates aggregate labor supply $L^s(w)$ with aggregate labor demand $L^d(w)$ at the market-clearing wage $w^*$. Graphically, equilibrium occurs at the intersection of upward-sloping supply and downward-sloping demand curves. Stability requires that excess supply (demand exceeds supply) emerges when wages fall below equilibrium, creating upward pressure on wages, while excess demand (supply exceeds demand) emerges when wages rise above equilibrium, creating downward pressure. These stability conditions are satisfied when supply slopes upward less steeply than demand slopes downward, ensuring adjustment dynamics converge to equilibrium.

Comparative statics characterize how equilibrium responds to exogenous shocks. A rightward shift in labor demand, perhaps from technological progress that increases labor productivity, raises both equilibrium employment and wages. The magnitude of wage and employment changes depends on labor supply elasticity: with inelastic supply, wage increases absorb most of the adjustment, while with elastic supply, employment increases dominate. Conversely, a rightward shift in labor supply, such as from immigration or rising female labor force participation, reduces equilibrium wages and raises employment, with the wage reduction larger when labor demand is inelastic.

Tax incidence analysis reveals how payroll taxes distribute between workers and firms regardless of statutory incidence. Consider a payroll tax $t$ levied on employers, shifting labor demand downward by $t$ since firms now pay $w + t$ for each unit of labor generating marginal revenue product $w$. The new equilibrium features lower worker wage $w'$ and higher employer cost $w' + t$, with the pre-tax equilibrium wage $w^*$ lying between these. Workers bear fraction $\frac{\epsilon_D}{\epsilon_S + \epsilon_D}$ of the tax burden through reduced wages, while firms bear fraction $\frac{\epsilon_S}{\epsilon_S + \epsilon_D}$ through increased labor costs. When labor supply is perfectly inelastic ($\epsilon_S = 0$), workers bear the entire burden through wage reductions equal to the tax. When labor demand is perfectly elastic ($\epsilon_D = \infty$), firms bear the entire burden through increased costs with no wage reduction.

Minimum wage analysis extends this framework to quantity restrictions. A minimum wage $\bar{w}$ above the competitive equilibrium $w^*$ creates excess supply $L^s(\bar{w}) - L^d(\bar{w})$, with employment determined by the short side of the market at $L^d(\bar{w})$. The employment loss equals $L^* - L^d(\bar{w})$, depending on labor demand elasticity. Traditional competitive analysis predicts unambiguous employment losses from minimum wages, but monopsony models reverse this conclusion. Under monopsony, a single employer faces upward-sloping labor supply, paying wages below marginal revenue product and restricting employment below the competitive level. A moderate minimum wage in this setting can simultaneously raise both wages and employment by constraining the monopsonist's ability to restrict employment, though sufficiently high minimum wages eventually reduce employment even under monopsony.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Empirical estimation of labor supply elasticities requires careful treatment of selection bias arising from observing wages only for workers, not for non-participants whose reservation wages exceed market wages. This censoring generates classic sample selection problems where ordinary least squares regression of hours on wages using employed workers produces inconsistent estimates. Heckman's two-step correction addresses this by first estimating a probit model for labor force participation, then including the inverse Mills ratio as a regressor in the hours equation to control for selection. The exclusion restriction requires variables affecting participation but not hours conditional on working, typically achieved through variation in non-labor income or family structure affecting reservation wages but not preferences over hours conditional on employment.

Instrumental variable estimation of labor supply confronts the challenge that observed wage variation reflects both supply and demand shifts, with demand shifts generating negative correlation between wages and hours while supply shifts generate positive correlation. Valid instruments must shift labor supply without directly affecting labor demand or preferences. Natural experiments provide credible identification: policy reforms such as the Earned Income Tax Credit expansion in the 1990s shifted labor supply through changing budget sets, lottery winnings provide exogenous income variation isolating income effects, and disability insurance eligibility changes affect non-labor income without directly changing preferences for work. These quasi-experimental approaches generate more credible elasticity estimates than cross-sectional wage variation, typically finding smaller elasticities suggesting that much observed wage-hours correlation reflects demand shifts rather than supply responses.

Panel data estimation exploits within-individual wage variation over time, controlling for time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity in preferences and productivity through fixed effects. The identifying assumption requires that time-varying wage changes are uncorrelated with time-varying preference shocks, which remains questionable if wage growth and preference shifts both reflect life-cycle factors like aging or family formation. First-differencing removes fixed effects but exacerbates measurement error bias in wages, potentially requiring instrumental variables to address attenuation bias. Longer difference intervals reduce measurement error bias but increase the risk that exclusion restrictions fail as more life-cycle changes occur between periods.

Labor demand estimation distinguishes between establishment-level demand and market-level demand, with the former substantially more elastic than the latter. An individual firm faces nearly perfectly elastic labor supply at the market wage because workers can move between firms costlessly in competitive markets. Consequently, establishment-level employment responds sharply to idiosyncratic productivity shocks, generating high estimated labor demand elasticities in firm-level data. However, market-level labor demand faces upward-sloping aggregate labor supply, requiring wage increases to attract additional workers into the occupation or location. This distinction becomes critical for policy analysis: firm-specific wage subsidies generate large employment responses through reallocation without aggregate employment gains, while market-wide minimum wages face inelastic labor demand and generate smaller employment losses than establishment-level elasticities suggest.

Monopsony power arises in thin labor markets where workers face significant costs of switching employers, conferring wage-setting power even to small firms. Geographic isolation, occupation-specific skills, employer-sponsored health insurance, and collusive wage-setting all generate monopsony power. Recent research exploiting merger-induced changes in labor market concentration finds substantial employment and wage effects, suggesting monopsony may be empirically prevalent even in urban areas. The implication for minimum wage policy is profound: if labor markets are monopsonistic, moderate minimum wages can increase employment by constraining employers' ability to hold wages below marginal revenue product, though the optimal minimum wage depends on the degree of monopsony power which varies across markets and occupations.

Dynamic labor supply models incorporating human capital accumulation and intertemporal substitution generate richer predictions about responses to temporary versus permanent wage changes. The intertemporal elasticity of substitution governs how workers shift hours across time in response to predictable wage variation, while the Frisch elasticity measures labor supply responsiveness holding marginal utility of wealth constant. Life-cycle models predict that workers should increase hours during high-wage periods and reduce hours during low-wage periods, generating positive correlation between wages and hours over the business cycle. However, observed employment fluctuations substantially exceed predictions from intertemporal substitution given plausible preference parameters, suggesting that non-separabilities between consumption and leisure, borrowing constraints, or adjustment costs dampen intertemporal substitution.

Search and matching frictions introduce unemployment even in equilibrium as workers and firms engage in costly bilateral matching processes. The Diamond-Mortensen-Pissarides framework models labor market flows through a matching function $m(u, v)$ that converts unemployed workers $u$ and vacancies $v$ into job matches. Labor market tightness $\theta = v/u$ determines the rate at which workers find jobs and firms fill vacancies, with higher tightness raising worker job-finding rates and reducing firm vacancy-filling rates. Wages emerge from Nash bargaining between matched workers and firms, splitting the match surplus according to bargaining power. This framework generates equilibrium unemployment determined by job destruction and job creation rates, with unemployment persisting in steady state as a by-product of reallocation and heterogeneity.

Compensating differential estimation requires careful controls for unobserved worker heterogeneity because workers sort into jobs based on preferences over wages and amenities. High-ability workers may simultaneously earn higher wages and work in more pleasant conditions if firms offer package deals trading off multiple job attributes. Fixed effects estimation controls for time-invariant sorting but requires within-worker variation in job characteristics, limiting identification to workers who switch between jobs with different amenity levels. Hedonic regression approaches estimate the wage-amenity gradient across jobs, but the slope reflects both worker preferences and firm technology. Structural estimation approaches recover primitive preferences and technology parameters, but require strong functional form assumptions about utility and production functions.

Welfare analysis of labor market policies requires aggregating consumer and producer surplus accounting for tax distortions and distributional objectives. A binding minimum wage generates deadweight loss equal to the triangle between supply and demand curves over the range of lost employment, representing mutually beneficial trades prevented by the price floor. However, distributional considerations may favor minimum wages despite efficiency costs if society places higher welfare weight on low-wage workers. Optimal minimum wage formulas trade off redistribution gains against efficiency losses, with the optimal level increasing in the social welfare weight on low-wage workers and decreasing in labor demand elasticity. Transfer programs such as the Earned Income Tax Credit provide an alternative redistribution mechanism without directly distorting employment margins, though they require tax revenue with its own distortionary costs.

Equilibrium search models with on-the-job search generate wage dispersion for identical workers as firms post different wages to trade off vacancy costs against expected match duration. Higher wage firms attract more applicants and experience lower turnover, while lower wage firms accept higher vacancy duration and turnover rates. This framework explains why identical workers receive different wages across firms and why wage offers exhibit substantial dispersion even conditioning on detailed worker characteristics. The model predicts that minimum wages reduce wage dispersion by truncating the lower tail of the wage distribution, consistent with empirical evidence that minimum wage increases compress wage distributions.

Spatial equilibrium models recognize that workers choose both locations and labor supply, generating interdependence between housing markets and labor markets. Higher wages in a location attract in-migration, bidding up housing costs until marginal workers are indifferent between locations. This spatial arbitrage condition implies that nominal wage differences across locations primarily reflect cost-of-living differences rather than real wage differences, complicating interpretation of geographic wage variation. Local labor demand shocks generate employment responses through both labor supply adjustment along the intensive margin and migration along the extensive margin, with the relative importance depending on migration costs and housing supply elasticity. Locations with inelastic housing supply experience larger wage and housing cost increases but smaller employment increases in response to demand shocks.

Lifecycle labor supply models incorporate human capital accumulation, borrowing constraints, and demographic transitions to generate realistic age-hours profiles. Young workers invest in human capital through on-the-job training, reducing current earnings but raising future productivity. Prime-age workers smooth consumption by borrowing against future income, but borrowing constraints force excess labor supply if desired borrowing exceeds available credit. Older workers approaching retirement face declining human capital returns, reducing labor supply as retirement approaches. These lifecycle considerations generate hump-shaped hours profiles over the lifetime, peaking in middle age when human capital is highest and retirement is distant, then declining as retirement approaches and human capital depreciates.

Collective labor supply models treat household decisions as bargaining between spouses with different preferences rather than a unitary household maximizing a single utility function. The Pareto-efficient outcome depends on relative bargaining power determined by outside options, non-labor income shares, and social norms. Policy reforms that change the intrahousehold allocation of resources, such as directing child benefits to mothers rather than fathers, can affect labor supply and consumption patterns even holding total household income constant if bargaining power shifts. Empirical tests of collective models exploit policy reforms that exogenously shift bargaining power to test whether consumption and labor supply respond in ways consistent with intrahousehold bargaining rather than unitary decision-making.

Human capital investment decisions introduce dynamic complementarities where current schooling increases the return to future schooling and work experience, generating increasing returns to education over the lifecycle. The Ben-Porath model formalizes this through a production function for human capital where individuals allocate time between work and schooling, with optimal investment declining over the lifecycle as the horizon for recouping returns shortens. This framework explains why schooling concentrates early in life and predicts that technological change raising the return to skill should increase educational investment. Empirical evidence supports these predictions: returns to college education have risen substantially since 1980, coinciding with increased college enrollment rates, though rising tuition costs partially offset rising returns.

Discrimination in labor markets manifests through both taste-based and statistical mechanisms. Becker's taste-based discrimination model posits that some employers, workers, or customers have preferences against transacting with certain groups, generating wage differentials even for equally productive workers. Competitive pressure should erode taste-based discrimination as non-discriminating firms gain cost advantages by hiring undervalued workers, though discrimination may persist if customer preferences or worker co-operation patterns sustain it. Statistical discrimination arises when employers use observable characteristics correlated with productivity to make hiring decisions under imperfect information about individual worker quality. This generates disparate treatment of equally qualified workers based on group average differences, and may persist even in competitive equilibrium if productivity differences reflect human capital investments responding to anticipated discrimination.
