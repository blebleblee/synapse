# Lesson 4: Urban Public Goods, Local Public Finance, Tiebout Sorting

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Urban public economics confronts a foundational tension that neither classical microeconomic theory nor national-level public finance fully addresses: the spatial heterogeneity of public goods provision across fragmented local jurisdictions. When we analyze national defense or monetary policy, we can reasonably assume a single, unified provision domain. But schools, police protection, parks, and local infrastructure exist within a landscape of competing, overlapping municipalities, each with independent taxing authority, service portfolios, and resident populations that possess extraordinary mobility. This spatial fragmentation transforms public goods theory from a static problem of optimal provision into a dynamic equilibrium of household sorting, fiscal competition, and capitalization into land values.

The classical Samuelson condition for optimal public goods provision states that the sum of marginal rates of substitution across all consumers must equal the marginal rate of transformation in production. In mathematical terms, for a pure public good $G$ and private consumption $C$, efficiency requires $\sum_{i=1}^{N} MRS_i^{G,C} = MRT^{G,C}$. This condition captures the non-rival nature of public goods: one person's consumption does not diminish availability to others, so the vertical sum of willingness-to-pay must equal marginal cost. Yet this formulation assumes a benevolent planner with perfect information about heterogeneous preferences, a single jurisdiction with no exit option, and no spatial dimension to either the public good or the population.

Urban public finance shatters all three assumptions simultaneously. Households differ not only in income but in their valuation of school quality versus park acreage versus property tax rates. They face not a single jurisdiction but dozens or hundreds of municipal choices within commuting distance of employment centers. And critically, they can vote with their feet, relocating to jurisdictions that offer preferred bundles of taxes and services. This mobility introduces a pseudo-market discipline into local public goods provision, transforming the problem from one of voting and collective choice within a fixed population to one of locational equilibrium where capitalization and sorting reveal preferences.

Charles Tiebout's 1956 insight revolutionized urban public economics by recognizing that interjurisdictional competition could approximate market efficiency even for pure public goods. If households are perfectly mobile, possess complete information, face no employment constraints, and confront a sufficiently large number of jurisdictions offering diverse tax-service packages, then decentralized local provision can achieve allocative efficiency without requiring a planner to solve the Samuelson condition. Households self-sort into communities matching their preferences, and competition among jurisdictions disciplines fiscal policy. The mechanism operates through capitalization: jurisdictions offering superior public goods per dollar of taxation attract demand for housing, bidding up land rents and property values until marginal households are indifferent between locations. In equilibrium, property values fully capitalize fiscal differences, leaving no unexploited arbitrage opportunities and revealing the true willingness-to-pay for local amenities.

The core invariant of urban public finance is the **spatial fiscal equilibrium condition**: in a competitive landscape of local jurisdictions, the implicit price of public goods must equal their marginal cost, enforced not by voting but by capitalization into land values. Formally, consider a metropolitan area with jurisdictions indexed by $j$, each offering a public good level $g_j$ financed by property tax rate $\tau_j$ on housing consumption $h$. Household utility depends on private consumption, housing, and local public goods: $U(c, h, g_j)$. In spatial equilibrium, utility must be equalized across all occupied jurisdictions, otherwise households would relocate. With fixed land supply in each jurisdiction, housing prices $p_j$ adjust to clear markets.

The equilibrium housing price in jurisdiction $j$ satisfies the locational indifference condition: households with identical income $y$ achieve equal utility whether they reside in jurisdiction $j$ or any alternative $k$. This implies $U(y - p_j h - \tau_j p_j h, h, g_j) = U(y - p_k h - \tau_k p_k h, h, g_k)$ for all occupied jurisdictions. Rearranging, the housing price differential between jurisdictions must exactly compensate for differences in public goods and tax rates: $p_j - p_k = \frac{1}{h}\left[\frac{\partial U / \partial g}{\partial U / \partial c}(g_j - g_k) - (p_j \tau_j - p_k \tau_k)h\right]$. The first term represents capitalized willingness-to-pay for superior public goods, while the second captures the present value of differential tax liabilities. When households are mobile and informed, competition forces this equation to hold, translating fiscal differences into observable property value differentials.

This capitalization mechanism serves as the fulcrum of urban public finance. It transforms unobservable preference heterogeneity into revealed preference data encoded in housing markets. It disciplines local governments by making fiscal mismanagement immediately visible in property values. And it creates a tension between equity and efficiency: if high-income households cluster in jurisdictions with excellent schools funded by high property tax bases, low-income households face either exclusion from superior services or regressive implicit prices for public goods access. The efficiency of Tiebout sorting depends critically on the absence of externalities across jurisdictions, the excludability of local public goods, and the elasticity of jurisdiction boundaries to accommodate preference heterogeneity.

Property taxation introduces additional complexity because the tax base itself responds to the tax rate. A simple property tax levies rate $\tau$ on housing value $ph$, generating revenue $R = \tau ph$ per household. But housing consumption $h$ is endogenous: higher tax rates increase the effective price of housing from $p$ to $p(1 + \tau)$, inducing households to substitute toward other consumption. The elasticity of housing demand with respect to the tax-inclusive price determines how much of the statutory tax burden falls on landlords versus tenants. In partial equilibrium, with perfectly elastic housing supply, the entire burden falls on residents through higher gross-of-tax prices. But in spatial equilibrium with fixed land supply in each jurisdiction, the incidence shifts to landowners as households relocate to avoid high taxes. The incidence distribution depends on the relative elasticities of housing supply, housing demand, and interjurisdictional mobility.

Congestion externalities complicate the Tiebout paradigm by introducing non-convexities and strategic complementarities in location choice. Pure public goods are non-rival: one person's consumption does not reduce availability to others. But many local public goods are congestible: school quality deteriorates with overcrowded classrooms, roads congest with traffic volume, and parks lose value when overused. A congestible public good exhibits rivalry beyond a threshold population, introducing a wedge between average and marginal cost of population. If a jurisdiction finances public goods through property taxes, in-migration increases the tax base but also increases congestion, reducing per-capita service quality. The optimal jurisdiction size balances scale economies in public goods provision against congestion costs, a tradeoff first formalized by Henry George's analysis of urban land rent and extended by Buchanan's club theory of optimal membership.

Transportation networks amplify congestion externalities because road capacity is a local public good subject to acute rivalry during peak travel times. Each additional commuter imposes time costs on all other travelers, but individual route choice ignores this external cost. The social cost of a trip includes not only the private time cost but also the marginal congestion imposed on others. Without congestion pricing, commuters overuse road capacity, generating deadweight loss. The magnitude of inefficiency depends on the congestion function relating travel time to volume: if travel time increases linearly with volume, the deadweight loss is modest, but if congestion exhibits threshold effects or cascading breakdowns, the welfare cost escalates nonlinearly. Optimal congestion tolls internalize the external cost by charging each user the marginal congestion cost they impose, a policy analytically equivalent to Pigouvian taxation of pollution.

The spatial structure of employment and residential location creates a two-way interaction between housing markets and transportation networks. Households choose residential locations trading off housing costs, commute times, and local public goods. Firms choose employment locations balancing land costs against agglomeration economies and labor market access. In equilibrium, land rents decline with distance from employment centers, compensating workers for longer commutes. But commute costs depend on congestion, which depends on the spatial distribution of residences and jobs. This simultaneity generates multiple equilibria: concentrated employment with high-density housing near job centers minimizes commute distances but concentrates congestion, while dispersed employment spreads congestion but lengthens average commute distances. The welfare ranking of equilibria depends on the curvature of congestion costs and the elasticity of location choice with respect to commute time.

Fiscal capitalization and congestion externalities interact through household sorting. If high-income households value uncongested roads and superior schools more than low-income households, they will bid up housing prices in jurisdictions offering both low congestion and high public goods. This sorting can be self-reinforcing: as high-income households cluster, they increase the property tax base, enabling higher public goods provision without raising tax rates, further attracting high-income residents. Conversely, jurisdictions with deteriorating public goods and congested infrastructure experience out-migration of high-income households, eroding the tax base and triggering fiscal stress. This dynamic can generate stratification by income across jurisdictions, with welfare consequences that depend on whether local public goods exhibit peer effects or spillovers.

The efficiency of decentralized local public goods provision rests on assumptions that empirical evidence suggests are violated in practice. Perfect mobility requires zero moving costs, perfect information about jurisdiction characteristics, and no employment constraints. Yet transaction costs in housing markets are substantial, information about school quality and future infrastructure investment is incomplete, and job-specific human capital ties workers to particular employment centers. The assumption of a large number of competing jurisdictions holds in some metropolitan areas but not in others, limiting the disciplining effect of interjurisdictional competition. And many local public goods generate spillovers across jurisdictions: air quality, contagious disease control, and watershed management all exhibit regional externalities that undermine the efficiency of purely local provision.

These frictions transform urban public finance from a problem of pure Tiebout sorting into a constrained optimization where mobility costs, information asymmetries, and interjurisdictional externalities generate second-best tradeoffs. The presence of moving costs reduces the elasticity of location choice with respect to fiscal differences, weakening the capitalization mechanism and allowing greater divergence in public goods efficiency across jurisdictions. Asymmetric information about future fiscal policy or neighborhood trajectories can generate speculative dynamics in housing markets, disconnecting current property values from fundamental willingness-to-pay for public goods. And spillovers create a fiscal commons problem where individual jurisdictions under-provide goods generating regional benefits, requiring coordination mechanisms such as metropolitan planning authorities or state-level mandates.

The property tax incidence in spatial equilibrium represents one of the most subtle results in urban public economics, synthesized by Peter Mieszkowski and George Zodrow in the "new view" of property taxation. The traditional view treats property taxes as excise taxes on housing and capital, with incidence falling partly on consumers through higher housing prices and partly on capital owners through lower net returns. But in a system of competing jurisdictions with mobile capital, the average property tax rate is borne entirely by landowners, while deviations from the average rate distort capital allocation and are borne by residents through housing price adjustments. The logic operates through arbitrage: if one jurisdiction raises its property tax rate above the regional average, capital flows out, reducing housing supply and raising gross-of-tax prices until net returns equalize across jurisdictions. Immobile land absorbs the full burden of the average tax through capitalized reductions in land value, while marginal tax differences induce distortions borne by mobile factors.

This incidence result has profound implications for the efficiency and equity of local public finance. To the extent that property values capitalize fiscal differences, landowners at the time of policy changes bear windfall gains or losses. Households purchasing land after capitalization occurs pay implicit prices for public goods reflecting their true willingness-to-pay, achieving efficient sorting. But pre-existing landowners experience wealth transfers that may be viewed as inequitable, particularly if fiscal improvements are financed by general taxation but capitalized into land owned by a subset of residents. The distributional consequences depend on the correlation between landowners hip and income, the elasticity of land supply, and the extent to which property taxes are shifted forward to future residents versus borne by current owners.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The operational architecture of urban public finance comprises three interlocking mechanisms: the household location choice problem, the jurisdiction optimization problem, and the spatial equilibrium condition. Understanding how these components interact requires specifying the decision rules, budget constraints, and market-clearing conditions that govern decentralized allocation in a landscape of competing jurisdictions.

The household location choice problem begins with a utility function $U(c, h, g_j, z_j)$ defined over private consumption $c$, housing consumption $h$, local public goods $g_j$ provided by jurisdiction $j$, and an idiosyncratic location preference shock $z_j$ capturing unobserved heterogeneity such as proximity to family or workplace. The household faces a budget constraint $c + p_j(1 + \tau_j)h = y - T_j$, where $y$ denotes income, $p_j$ represents the housing price in jurisdiction $j$, $\tau_j$ is the property tax rate, and $T_j$ captures any non-property taxes such as local income or sales taxes. The tax-inclusive price of housing $p_j(1 + \tau_j)$ reflects the total cost of consuming housing in the jurisdiction.

The household chooses both the quantity of housing $h$ and the jurisdiction $j$ to maximize utility subject to the budget constraint. This two-stage decision decomposes into an inner problem of optimal housing consumption conditional on location, and an outer problem of jurisdiction choice given the indirect utility achievable in each location. The first-order condition for housing consumption yields $\frac{\partial U / \partial h}{\partial U / \partial c} = p_j(1 + \tau_j)$, equating the marginal rate of substitution between housing and consumption to the tax-inclusive housing price. This generates a compensated housing demand function $h^*(p_j(1 + \tau_j), g_j, \bar{u})$ conditional on utility level $\bar{u}$ and public goods $g_j$.

Substituting optimal housing consumption into the utility function yields indirect utility $V_j(y, p_j, \tau_j, g_j, z_j)$ achievable in jurisdiction $j$. The household selects the jurisdiction delivering maximum indirect utility: $j^* = \arg\max_j V_j(y, p_j, \tau_j, g_j, z_j)$. When idiosyncratic preferences $z_j$ are distributed according to an extreme value distribution, the probability that a household with income $y$ chooses jurisdiction $j$ follows a multinomial logit specification: $P_j(y) = \frac{\exp(V_j / \sigma)}{\sum_k \exp(V_k / \sigma)}$, where $\sigma$ parameterizes the variance of idiosyncratic preferences. This probabilistic choice structure generates smooth population distributions across jurisdictions rather than corner solutions where all households concentrate in a single location.

The jurisdiction optimization problem depends on the governance structure and objective function of local government. The Tiebout model assumes jurisdictions passively offer tax-service bundles, with competition and household mobility enforcing efficiency. But in practice, local governments actively choose tax rates and expenditure levels, typically subject to voter preferences, bureaucratic incentives, or developer influence. A median-voter model posits that the decisive voter's preferred tax-service bundle wins majority elections, generating tax rates $\tau_j^*$ and public goods $g_j^*$ satisfying the median household's first-order condition: $\frac{\partial U_m / \partial g_j}{\partial U_m / \partial c} = MC_g / \tau_j p_j h_m$, where $MC_g$ denotes the marginal cost of public goods and $h_m$ is the median voter's housing consumption. This condition equates the median household's marginal willingness-to-pay for public goods to the marginal cost per dollar of property tax revenue.

When the median voter optimizes over the tax-service bundle, stratification by income across jurisdictions emerges endogenously. High-income households with high marginal willingness-to-pay for public goods prefer jurisdictions with high taxes and high public goods, while low-income households prefer low taxes and low public goods. In equilibrium, jurisdictions sort by income, with housing prices adjusting to maintain locational indifference for marginal households. The degree of stratification depends on the income elasticity of demand for public goods: if public goods are normal goods, stratification increases with income inequality, while if public goods are inferior or Giffen goods (empirically implausible for schools and safety), sorting patterns invert.

The spatial equilibrium condition requires that housing markets clear in every jurisdiction and that no household strictly prefers to relocate given equilibrium prices and policies. Market clearing in jurisdiction $j$ imposes $N_j(p_j, \tau_j, g_j) = \bar{H}_j$, where $N_j$ denotes population demand for housing in jurisdiction $j$ given prices and policies, and $\bar{H}_j$ represents the fixed housing stock. With inelastic housing supply in the short run, prices adjust to equate demand with the fixed stock. In the long run, construction responds to price signals, expanding supply in jurisdictions where prices exceed construction costs plus land costs. The long-run spatial equilibrium incorporates a zero-profit condition for developers: $p_j = MC_h + r_j / (r + \delta)$, where $MC_h$ denotes marginal construction cost, $r_j$ is land rent in jurisdiction $j$, $r$ is the interest rate, and $\delta$ is the depreciation rate.

Land rent $r_j$ adjusts to clear the land market, with total land supply in jurisdiction $j$ fixed at $\bar{L}_j$. The land rent function $r_j(p_j, \tau_j, g_j)$ is derived from the zero-profit condition and housing demand, generating a bid-rent curve that describes how much households are willing to pay for land in each jurisdiction. In equilibrium, land is allocated to the highest bidder, with bid-rents capitalized into land values. The capitalization equation links land values to the present value of future rents: $V_j = \int_0^\infty r_j(t) e^{-rt} dt$. Fiscal improvements that increase demand for housing raise bid-rents, increasing land values, while tax increases reduce bid-rents, lowering land values.

The interaction between property taxation and capitalization creates a feedback loop that determines the incidence of fiscal policy changes. Consider a jurisdiction that increases public goods expenditure $g_j$ financed by a higher property tax rate $\tau_j$. If the expenditure increase raises utility more than the tax increase lowers it, demand for housing in the jurisdiction rises, bidding up gross-of-tax housing prices $p_j$. But the net-of-tax return to landowners $p_j - \tau_j p_j$ may rise or fall depending on the elasticity of housing demand and supply. With perfectly inelastic land supply and perfectly elastic housing supply (construction costs constant), the gross housing price adjusts one-for-one with the tax rate, leaving landowners bearing the full incidence through changes in land rent. With inelastic housing supply, prices rise by less than the tax increase, so residents bear part of the burden through higher effective housing costs.

Transportation network congestion introduces an additional equilibrium condition: the assignment of households to routes and departure times must be consistent with congestion-dependent travel times. The classic Wardrop equilibrium posits that in equilibrium, no traveler can reduce travel time by unilaterally switching routes, given the route choices of others. Formally, let $t_k(n_k)$ denote travel time on route $k$ as a function of traffic volume $n_k$, with $\frac{\partial t_k}{\partial n_k} > 0$ capturing congestion. In equilibrium, all used routes between a given origin and destination must have equal travel times, otherwise travelers would switch from slower to faster routes until times equalize. Unused routes must have weakly higher travel times than used routes, otherwise they would attract traffic.

The Wardrop equilibrium travel time $t^*$ satisfies $t_k(n_k^*) = t^*$ for all routes $k$ with positive flow $n_k^* > 0$, and $t_k(n_k^*) \geq t^*$ for unused routes. The equilibrium is generally inefficient because individual travelers ignore the marginal congestion cost they impose on others. The social optimum requires minimizing total travel time $\sum_k n_k t_k(n_k)$, yielding first-order condition $t_k(n_k^{opt}) + n_k^{opt} \frac{\partial t_k}{\partial n_k} = \lambda$ for all routes with positive flow, where $\lambda$ is the shadow cost of travel demand. The wedge $n_k^{opt} \frac{\partial t_k}{\partial n_k}$ represents the marginal external cost of congestion, equal to the optimal Pigouvian toll that decentralizes the social optimum.

The spatial structure of congestion depends on the network topology and the distribution of origins and destinations. In a monocentric city with employment concentrated in a central business district (CBD), commuters travel radially inward during morning peak and outward during evening peak, concentrating congestion on arterial routes near the CBD. The bid-rent function in this setting exhibits a gradient with respect to distance from the CBD, compensating households for longer commutes. Formally, if commute cost per mile is $\tau_c$ including time and fuel costs, and congestion adds $\kappa(n)$ per mile as a function of traffic volume $n$, then the bid-rent gradient satisfies $\frac{dr}{dd} = -[\tau_c + \kappa(n)]$, where $d$ denotes distance from the CBD. In equilibrium, housing prices and land rents decline with distance at a rate equal to marginal commute cost, integrating congestion externalities into the spatial price structure.

Polycentric employment configurations with multiple job centers generate more complex spatial patterns. Households optimize over residential location trading off access to multiple employment centers, while firms optimize over job location trading off land costs against agglomeration economies and labor market access. The equilibrium is characterized by a system of bid-rent surfaces, one for each employment center, with land allocated to the highest bidder at each location. The spatial distribution of rents exhibits peaks near job centers and valleys in low-accessibility areas. Congestion patterns become multi-directional, with traffic flows crossing between employment centers and residential areas, complicating the design of efficient congestion pricing schemes.

The fiscal capitalization mechanism can be quantitatively assessed through hedonic regression of housing prices on jurisdiction characteristics. A hedonic price function $p = f(g_j, \tau_j, X_{ij})$ relates property prices to public goods $g_j$, tax rates $\tau_j$, and housing characteristics $X_{ij}$. The partial derivative $\frac{\partial p}{\partial g_j}$ estimates the marginal willingness-to-pay for public goods, while $\frac{\partial p}{\partial \tau_j}$ estimates the capitalized cost of taxation. If fiscal capitalization is complete, the ratio $\frac{\partial p / \partial g_j}{\partial p / \partial \tau_j}$ should equal the efficiency price of public goods provision. Deviations from this equality indicate either incomplete capitalization due to mobility frictions, information asymmetries, or dynamic adjustment lags, or measurement error in public goods quality.

Empirical estimation faces identification challenges because jurisdiction characteristics are jointly determined in equilibrium. Households sort across jurisdictions based on preferences, creating correlation between public goods provision and unobserved household characteristics that also affect housing demand. Jurisdictions set policies responding to resident preferences, creating reverse causality. And omitted variables such as natural amenities or historical attributes confound the relationship between fiscal variables and prices. Credible identification requires quasi-experimental variation in fiscal policy induced by institutional rules, state mandates, or court-ordered school finance reforms that shift public goods provision independently of local preferences.

A revealing empirical strategy exploits discontinuities at jurisdiction boundaries. If two adjacent neighborhoods differ only in jurisdiction membership, comparing housing prices across the boundary identifies the capitalized value of fiscal differences, controlling for all other location attributes that vary smoothly across space. Boundary fixed effects regressions estimate $p_i = \alpha_b + \beta g_{j(i)} + \gamma \tau_{j(i)} + \varepsilon_i$, where $i$ indexes properties, $b$ indexes boundary segments, and $j(i)$ denotes the jurisdiction containing property $i$. The boundary fixed effects $\alpha_b$ absorb all spatially continuous attributes, isolating the discrete jump in prices attributable to jurisdiction membership. If $\beta > 0$ and $\gamma < 0$, fiscal differences are capitalized, with the ratio $\beta / \gamma$ revealing the implicit price of public goods.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

The efficiency of decentralized urban public goods provision depends on institutional design choices that either facilitate Tiebout sorting and capitalization or introduce distortions that generate misallocation. Optimal local public finance requires aligning incentives for household location, jurisdiction policy, and developer supply responses with social welfare, while addressing market failures that arise from congestion, fiscal externalities, and sorting dynamics.

Property tax assessment and collection mechanisms critically affect both the efficiency and equity of local public finance. Accurate assessment requires frequent revaluation of all properties to reflect current market values, ensuring that the tax base tracks actual property values. Infrequent reassessment or reliance on historical cost basis creates horizontal inequity, taxing identical properties differently based on purchase timing. It also distorts location incentives by creating implicit subsidies for long-term residents whose properties are undervalued relative to recent market transactions. Best-practice assessment systems conduct annual revaluations using hedonic models calibrated to recent sales, supplemented by periodic physical inspections to verify structural characteristics. Jurisdictions that implement such systems observe more stable revenue streams and more accurate capitalization of fiscal differences.

The property tax rate structure interacts with household sorting to determine the progressivity or regressivity of the local fiscal system. A proportional property tax levies the same rate on all properties regardless of value, generating revenue proportional to housing consumption. To the extent that higher-income households consume more housing, proportional property taxes are mildly progressive. But if housing consumption increases less than proportionally with income due to declining marginal propensity to spend on housing, property taxes become regressive relative to income. Progressive property tax structures with graduated rates mitigate regressivity but introduce complexity and potentially distort housing consumption decisions. An alternative mechanism is homestead exemptions that exempt a fixed value of property from taxation, effectively introducing progressivity by reducing the average tax rate on lower-value properties.

Intergovernmental grants and revenue sharing can correct for fiscal externalities and reduce stratification, but their design determines whether they enhance or undermine efficiency. Matching grants that require local jurisdictions to contribute a share of expenditure encourage efficient spending by maintaining marginal incentive alignment, while lump-sum grants fund expenditure without distorting marginal decisions. Equalization grants that transfer revenue from high-tax-base to low-tax-base jurisdictions reduce fiscal disparities but may weaken the link between local preferences and public goods provision, attenuating the incentive for efficient management. The optimal grant structure balances redistribution against incentive preservation, typically requiring matching for expenditures generating spillovers and equalization for base-level services.

Zoning and land use regulation profoundly affect the efficiency of Tiebout sorting by restricting housing supply and segmenting housing markets. Minimum lot size requirements, density restrictions, and exclusionary zoning limit the elasticity of housing supply, amplifying price responses to fiscal differences and potentially excluding lower-income households from high-public-goods jurisdictions. Such regulations can undermine the efficiency of Tiebout sorting by preventing marginal households from accessing preferred jurisdictions, generating excess stratification by income. Conversely, flexible zoning that allows developers to respond to price signals increases the elasticity of housing supply, dampening price capitalization but enabling broader access to high-quality jurisdictions. The welfare tradeoff depends on whether fiscal differences reflect efficient sorting by preferences or exclusionary sorting by income.

The failure mode of exclusionary zoning arises when existing residents use land use regulation to restrict entry, preserving fiscal surpluses and limiting congestion at the expense of potential entrants. A jurisdiction with superior public goods and low tax rates (due to a high property value base) attracts in-migration, bidding up housing prices. Existing residents may respond by tightening zoning restrictions to limit supply, further increasing prices and excluding lower-income households. This dynamic generates a political economy equilibrium where incumbent landowners capture fiscal and congestion rents through regulatory barriers, reducing aggregate welfare by misallocating households across jurisdictions. Empirical evidence suggests that jurisdictions with restrictive zoning exhibit higher price-to-income ratios, lower construction rates, and greater income segregation, consistent with exclusionary dynamics.

Congestion pricing represents the first-best policy for internalizing transportation externalities, but implementation faces political economy and technological challenges. An optimal congestion toll charges each driver the marginal external cost they impose, varying by route, time of day, and traffic volume. In principle, real-time congestion pricing using GPS tracking and dynamic toll calculation can implement the optimal toll schedule, decentralizing the social optimum without requiring centralized route assignment. Singapore's Electronic Road Pricing system and London's Congestion Charge approximate this ideal by charging higher tolls during peak periods and in congested zones. Empirical evaluation finds substantial welfare gains from congestion pricing, reducing travel times by 15-30 percent and generating revenue that can finance infrastructure investment or rebate to residents.

The political feasibility of congestion pricing hinges on the incidence of tolls and the allocation of revenue. If tolls are perceived as regressive, burdening low-income commuters who lack transit alternatives, political opposition intensifies. Revenue recycling mechanisms that rebate toll revenue to affected households or fund transit improvements enhance equity and political support. The optimal revenue use depends on the source of initial misallocation: if congestion arises from underpriced road use, toll revenue should fund capacity expansion or transit alternatives, while if congestion reflects excessive dispersion of jobs and housing, revenue should subsidize densification near employment centers. Transparent communication of welfare gains, distributional impacts, and revenue allocation builds political coalitions for reform.

Public transit provision interacts with road congestion and residential sorting, creating complementarities that affect optimal policy design. High-quality transit substitutes for automobile commuting, reducing congestion on roads and enabling higher residential densities. Jurisdictions with extensive transit networks observe steeper gradients in housing prices with respect to transit access, with price premiums of 10-30 percent within walking distance of stations. This capitalization reflects both direct time savings and the option value of avoiding congestion. Optimal transit investment balances construction and operating costs against congestion reduction, density benefits, and environmental externalities. Cost-benefit analysis typically finds high returns to transit investment in dense corridors with severe road congestion, but low or negative returns in low-density areas where ridership fails to cover operating costs.

The coordination failure in regional transportation planning arises because individual jurisdictions capture only a fraction of the benefits from transit investment or congestion reduction while bearing localized costs. A suburb that invests in park-and-ride facilities or bus rapid transit generates spillover benefits for the entire metropolitan area by reducing automobile commuting, but the fiscal benefits accrue primarily to the CBD jurisdiction where employment concentrates. This fiscal externality leads to under-provision of transit relative to the social optimum unless metropolitan planning authorities internalize regional benefits through cost-sharing or regulatory mandates. Successful regional transit systems such as Washington Metro or Bay Area Rapid Transit rely on dedicated regional funding mechanisms and governance structures that align incentives across jurisdictions.

School finance reform represents a natural experiment for testing fiscal capitalization and the Tiebout hypothesis. Court-mandated equalization of per-pupil spending across districts within a state compresses fiscal differences, reducing the incentive for households to sort by school quality. Empirical studies of school finance reforms find that equalization reduces the correlation between property values and school spending, consistent with reduced capitalization. But the welfare effects are ambiguous: equalization benefits low-income households by improving access to quality schools but may reduce aggregate efficiency if it weakens incentives for school districts to manage resources effectively. The long-run impact depends on whether equalization is implemented through leveling up (increasing spending in low-spending districts) or leveling down (capping spending in high-spending districts), with the former generating Pareto improvements and the latter creating losses for high-spending districts.

The dynamic adjustment path following fiscal policy changes reveals the speed and completeness of capitalization. A jurisdiction that unexpectedly improves public goods experiences an immediate jump in housing prices as households anticipate higher future utility. But if adjustment costs slow household mobility, the full capitalization may unfold over several years as information diffuses and households relocate. Empirical estimates of capitalization dynamics using event study designs around policy reforms find that 50-70 percent of the long-run price adjustment occurs within the first year, with full adjustment achieved within three to five years. The adjustment speed increases with lower moving costs, better information dissemination, and higher population turnover rates, suggesting that capitalization is more rapid in metropolitan areas with liquid housing markets and mobile populations.

The failure mode of fiscal illusion arises when voters systematically misperceive the cost or benefit of local public goods, generating inefficient voting outcomes. If property taxes are not fully salient, households may under-estimate the cost of public goods, voting for excessive expenditure. Conversely, if benefits accrue gradually or diffusely while costs are immediately visible, households may vote for under-provision. Institutional design can mitigate fiscal illusion through transparent budgeting, itemized tax bills that specify expenditure categories, and requirement that tax increases be approved by referenda. Jurisdictions that implement such transparency mechanisms observe closer alignment between voter preferences and expenditure outcomes, suggesting that reducing information asymmetries enhances efficiency.

Gentrification dynamics illustrate the interaction between fiscal capitalization, sorting, and displacement. A neighborhood that experiences improvement in public goods, amenities, or transit access attracts higher-income households, bidding up property values and rents. Existing low-income residents face displacement pressure as rents exceed affordability thresholds. If these residents are renters, displacement is immediate and largely uncompensated. If they are homeowners, they experience windfall capital gains but may face increased property tax liabilities that induce exit. The welfare implications depend on whether displaced households relocate to neighborhoods with comparable or inferior public goods and whether the aggregate benefit from improved neighborhood quality exceeds the cost of displacement. Policies to mitigate displacement include rent stabilization, property tax assessment freezes for long-term residents, and inclusionary zoning mandates that require a fraction of new housing to be affordable.

The equilibrium level of income stratification across jurisdictions depends on the curvature of utility with respect to public goods and the distribution of income. If utility is additively separable and public goods enter linearly, stratification is perfect: each jurisdiction contains a single income level, with complete sorting by ability to pay. But if there are complementarities between private consumption and public goods, or if public goods exhibit peer effects where high-income neighbors increase public goods productivity, stratification is amplified beyond income differences. Conversely, if there are diminishing returns to public goods or congestion costs that increase with jurisdiction wealth, stratification is dampened. Empirical estimates of income stratification find substantial but incomplete sorting, with within-jurisdiction income variance accounting for 60-70 percent of total variance, suggesting that preference heterogeneity and moving costs limit perfect stratification.

The normative evaluation of Tiebout sorting hinges on whether the resulting allocation achieves efficiency and whether the distributional consequences are acceptable. If preferences are truly heterogeneous and mobility is frictionless, sorting generates efficient matching of households to jurisdictions, maximizing aggregate utility subject to resource constraints. But if sorting is driven primarily by income rather than preferences, and if fiscal differences reflect property tax base disparities rather than willingness-to-pay differences, stratification reduces efficiency by misallocating public goods provision. The equity concern is whether low-income households face exclusion from high-quality jurisdictions due to zoning or property values rather than voluntary preference-based sorting. Policy interventions such as inclusionary zoning, school choice programs that sever the link between residential location and school access, or regional tax base sharing can improve equity without sacrificing efficiency, provided they preserve incentives for efficient local management.
