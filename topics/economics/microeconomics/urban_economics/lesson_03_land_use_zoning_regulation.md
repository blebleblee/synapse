# Lesson 3: Land Use Patterns, Zoning, Regulatory Distortions

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Urban land markets present a particularly pure laboratory for studying the spatial allocation of scarce resources under competing uses. Unlike abstract general equilibrium models where goods float in dimensionless commodity space, cities force economic agents to solve a brutal optimization problem: every household and firm must occupy a specific geographic coordinate, and transportation costs create friction that cannot be arbitraged away. The fundamental trade-off—proximity to economic opportunity versus the cost of land—generates predictable spatial patterns that emerge from decentralized decision-making, patterns that persist across centuries and continents until regulatory intervention distorts them.

Johann Heinrich von Thünen formulated the canonical model of agricultural land use in 1826, decades before the marginal revolution formalized price theory. His insight remains the foundation of modern urban economics: when transportation costs are positive and land is homogeneous except for distance from a central market, profit-maximizing farmers will self-organize into concentric rings of land use intensity. The crop with the highest transport cost per unit distance and highest land rent per acre locates nearest the market, while extensive land uses that ship cheaply occupy the periphery. Von Thünen derived this pattern not from central planning but from the differential bid-rent functions of competing agricultural activities, each willing to pay a location-specific premium that exactly offsets its transportation disadvantage.

Translating this logic to modern metropolitan areas, we replace von Thünen's wheat and timber with office towers, residential neighborhoods, and industrial parks, but the core mechanism persists. Commuting costs substitute for agricultural transport costs, and the central business district (CBD) replaces the medieval market town. Households face a canonical urban trade-off: living close to downtown jobs means paying higher housing prices per square foot, while suburban locations offer cheaper land at the expense of longer commutes. Firms face an analogous calculus, with headquarters functions that require face-to-face coordination bidding for downtown proximity while warehouses and manufacturing plants that ship goods via highways locate on cheaper peripheral land.

The equilibrium urban density gradient emerges from this bidding competition. Let $r(d)$ denote the land rent per acre at distance $d$ from the CBD, $t$ the commuting cost per mile, and $u^*$ the utility level that mobile households must achieve in equilibrium (the spatial arbitrage condition). A household's budget constraint at distance $d$ is $y = c + r(d) \cdot h(d) + t \cdot d$, where $y$ is income, $c$ is consumption of the composite good, and $h(d)$ is housing consumption in square feet. The household maximizes utility $U(c, h)$ subject to this constraint. The first-order condition for spatial equilibrium requires that utility remains constant as the household relocates: moving one mile outward must reduce rent by exactly enough to offset the increased commuting cost, holding utility fixed.

Differentiating the spatial equilibrium condition yields the fundamental bid-rent gradient: $\frac{dr}{dd} = -\frac{t}{h(d)}$. Land rent must decline with distance at a rate inversely proportional to housing consumption. This immediately implies that density falls with distance from the CBD—if rent is cheaper further out and households substitute toward housing as it becomes less expensive, then housing consumption per capita rises and population density declines. The canonical empirical specification takes the form $\ln(\text{density}) = \alpha - \beta \cdot d$, where density declines exponentially with distance. This negative exponential gradient has been documented in dozens of cities worldwide, with the steepness parameter $\beta$ varying systematically with transportation technology: cities built in the streetcar era exhibit steeper gradients than automobile-oriented postwar suburbs, exactly as the theory predicts.

The competitive land market generates efficient spatial allocation under standard assumptions: when households and firms face the true social cost of their location decisions, decentralized bidding produces a Pareto-efficient urban form. The first welfare theorem applies to cities as it does to any competitive market. But this efficiency result depends critically on the absence of externalities, complete markets, and perfect information—assumptions that fail dramatically in real urban land markets. Zoning regulations, the dominant institutional reality shaping American cities since the 1920s, represent the largest systematic distortion of urban land use patterns, transforming competitive market outcomes into quantity-constrained equilibria with profound distributional consequences.

Zoning originated as a legal tool to separate incompatible land uses: keeping slaughterhouses away from residential neighborhoods generates clear efficiency gains by internalizing negative externalities. But the institution evolved rapidly into a mechanism for incumbent homeowners to restrict housing supply, driving up property values at the expense of potential residents priced out of opportunity-rich labor markets. The canonical zoning ordinance partitions urban land into discrete use districts (residential, commercial, industrial) and imposes quantity restrictions on development intensity within each zone: minimum lot sizes, maximum floor-area ratios, setback requirements, and parking mandates. These restrictions function as binding quantity constraints in the microeconomic sense, generating wedges between marginal willingness to pay and marginal cost of supply.

The regulatory incidence of zoning—who bears the burden of supply restrictions—follows the standard incidence analysis from public finance, but the spatial dimension introduces crucial subtleties. When a municipality downzones land from multi-family to single-family residential, the immediate effect is a leftward shift in housing supply. In a closed-city model where population is fixed, the entire burden falls on renters through higher housing prices; landowners capture the regulatory scarcity rent. In an open-city model where households are mobile across metropolitan areas, the burden splits between higher housing costs and reduced city population, with the exact division determined by the elasticity of labor demand. Empirical evidence from California coastal cities demonstrates the closed-city case: when San Francisco restricts new housing construction through downzoning and discretionary permitting, rents rise dramatically while population growth stagnates, indicating that the regulatory tax falls almost entirely on incumbent and would-be residents rather than causing net population decline.

The filtering model of housing supply introduces dynamic considerations absent from static bid-rent theory. In competitive markets, new housing construction occurs at the urban fringe where land is cheapest, targeting high-income households who can afford premium prices for new units. As housing ages and depreciates, it filters down the income distribution: middle-class families move into older housing vacated by the rich, and low-income households occupy the most depreciated stock. Filtering provides the primary mechanism by which market-rate construction increases housing supply for all income groups, even though new buildings explicitly target the wealthy. Zoning restrictions that prevent new construction at the top of the market break this filtering chain, forcing middle-income households to compete for older stock and pushing low-income renters into overcrowding or displacement.

Housing supply elasticity—the percentage change in new construction in response to a one-percent price increase—varies by orders of magnitude across metropolitan areas, driven primarily by regulatory constraints rather than geographic features. Atlanta and Houston, with minimal zoning restrictions and abundant developable land, exhibit supply elasticities above two: housing prices remain stable even during demand booms because construction responds rapidly. San Francisco and coastal California cities, with stringent land use controls and slow permitting processes, have supply elasticities near zero: demand shocks translate almost entirely into price increases rather than quantity adjustments. This heterogeneity in supply elasticity generates profound differences in housing affordability, wage pass-through, and the spatial distribution of economic opportunity.

The monocentric city model, while stylized, provides the essential toolkit for analyzing these regulatory distortions. By starting from competitive spatial equilibrium and then introducing zoning as a binding quantity constraint, we can trace through the general equilibrium effects: how restrictions on housing supply near job centers raise commuting costs, how minimum lot sizes reduce density and increase infrastructure costs per capita, and how parking mandates subsidize automobile use and exacerbate congestion externalities. The model's predictions are testable and largely confirmed: cities with restrictive zoning exhibit higher housing price-to-income ratios, steeper rent gradients, longer average commutes, and greater spatial segregation by income. These are not accidental correlations but the predicted consequences of quantity restrictions in a spatial equilibrium framework.

Understanding urban land use patterns requires integrating the competitive spatial logic of von Thünen with the institutional reality of regulatory constraints. The bid-rent framework establishes the efficiency benchmark—what urban form emerges from decentralized optimization when agents face true social costs. Zoning analysis reveals how quantity restrictions distort this efficient allocation, generating scarcity rents, misallocating households across space, and preventing the density gradients that minimize aggregate transportation costs. The welfare losses are first-order: Hsieh and Moretti (2019) estimate that land use regulations in high-productivity cities like New York and San Francisco reduced US GDP by 36 percent between 1964 and 2009 by preventing workers from relocating to opportunity-rich labor markets. These are not minor technical inefficiencies but catastrophic misallocations of labor across space, driven by regulatory restrictions that bind far more tightly than most economists appreciated until comprehensive spatial equilibrium accounting became possible.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The monocentric city model formalizes the spatial equilibrium intuition into a tractable general equilibrium system. Begin with a city built on a featureless plain, with all employment concentrated at a central business district (CBD) of measure zero. Households are identical, each supplying one unit of labor inelastically at the CBD and earning wage $w$. Commuting costs are $t$ per mile per round trip, so a household living at distance $d$ incurs total commuting cost $t \cdot d$. Land is supplied perfectly inelastically by absentee landlords, and housing is produced by competitive firms using land as the sole input with constant returns to scale, so one unit of land produces one unit of housing.

The household maximization problem at distance $d$ is:

$$\max_{c, h} \, U(c, h) \quad \text{subject to} \quad c + r(d) \cdot h = w - t \cdot d$$

where $c$ denotes consumption of the numeraire composite good, $h$ is housing consumption, and $r(d)$ is the land rent per unit at distance $d$. The spatial equilibrium condition imposes that utility must be constant across all occupied locations: $U(c(d), h(d)) = \bar{u}$ for all $d \in [0, \bar{d}]$, where $\bar{d}$ is the city boundary. This no-arbitrage condition ensures that households are indifferent across locations; if utility were higher at some distance, households would bid up rents there until the advantage disappeared.

Differentiating the utility constant condition with respect to distance yields:

$$\frac{dU}{dd} = U_c \frac{dc}{dd} + U_h \frac{dh}{dd} = 0$$

From the budget constraint, $\frac{dc}{dd} = -h \frac{dr}{dd} - r \frac{dh}{dd} - t$. Substituting and applying the first-order condition for housing consumption $\frac{U_h}{U_c} = r$ gives:

$$\frac{dr}{dd} = -\frac{t}{h(d)}$$

This is the fundamental bid-rent equation: land rent declines with distance at a rate inversely proportional to housing consumption. Since housing is a normal good and cheaper further from the CBD (lower $r$ implies higher $h$ via the first-order condition), density must decline with distance. The equilibrium density function $\rho(d) = 1/h(d)$ therefore inherits the shape of the rent gradient.

To close the model, impose the population constraint: total city population $N$ must equal the integral of density over the urban area. For a circular city:

$$N = \int_0^{\bar{d}} 2\pi d \cdot \rho(d) \, dd = \int_0^{\bar{d}} 2\pi d \cdot \frac{1}{h(d)} \, dd$$

The city boundary $\bar{d}$ adjusts endogenously to satisfy this constraint. At the urban fringe, land rent must equal the agricultural rent $r_A$ (the opportunity cost of urban land), which pins down the boundary condition $r(\bar{d}) = r_A$.

For analytical tractability, adopt Cobb-Douglas utility $U(c, h) = c^\alpha h^{1-\alpha}$ with $\alpha \in (0,1)$. The first-order condition for housing consumption gives $h(d) = \frac{(1-\alpha)(w - t \cdot d)}{r(d)}$. Substituting into the bid-rent equation:

$$\frac{dr}{dd} = -\frac{t \cdot r(d)}{(1-\alpha)(w - t \cdot d)}$$

This is a separable differential equation with solution:

$$r(d) = r_A \left( \frac{w - t \cdot d}{w - t \cdot \bar{d}} \right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}$$

Land rent declines monotonically from the CBD to the urban fringe, with the rate of decline determined by the housing expenditure share $(1-\alpha)$ and the ratio of commuting cost to wage income. Cities with higher commuting costs or lower wages exhibit steeper rent gradients. The density gradient inherits this exponential form: $\rho(d) \propto (w - t \cdot d)^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}$, declining more slowly than rent because households substitute toward housing as it becomes cheaper.

Introducing zoning restrictions breaks the competitive equilibrium by imposing binding quantity constraints. Consider a minimum lot size regulation that requires each household to consume at least $\underline{h}$ units of housing. In the inner city where competitive equilibrium housing consumption $h^*(d) < \underline{h}$, the constraint binds and households are forced off their optimal consumption bundles. The shadow price of the zoning constraint—the Lagrange multiplier on $h \geq \underline{h}$—measures the welfare loss per household. Aggregating over all constrained households gives the total deadweight loss from the regulation.

The incidence of this constraint depends on whether we analyze a closed city (fixed population $N$) or an open city (fixed utility $\bar{u}$). In the closed-city case, zoning that forces lower density in the inner city pushes the urban boundary outward to accommodate the same population. This increases average commuting distance and raises aggregate transportation costs. Land rents adjust to clear the market: rents fall in the constrained inner zone (where excess supply of housing per household must be absorbed) and rise in the outer zone (where demand shifts due to the spatial reallocation). Landowners in the constrained zone suffer capital losses, while peripheral landowners gain.

In the open-city case, where households are mobile and utility must equal the national level $\bar{u}$, the city population adjusts endogenously. Zoning that raises housing costs or commuting burdens causes population to decline until utility returns to $\bar{u}$. The incidence now falls on workers through reduced employment and on landowners through lower aggregate land values. Empirical evidence suggests that most large US cities are better approximated by the closed-city model in the short run (housing supply adjusts slowly, so demand shocks affect prices) but the open-city model in the long run (households migrate in response to sustained cost-of-living differentials).

Floor-area ratio (FAR) restrictions provide another canonical zoning intervention. FAR limits the ratio of building floor area to lot area: an FAR of 2.0 allows a developer to build 20,000 square feet of floor space on a 10,000 square foot lot. When FAR binds below the competitive equilibrium building height, developers under-provide floor space relative to the efficient level. The welfare loss decomposes into two components: the reduction in total housing supply (which raises prices) and the distortion in building height per unit of land (which prevents the cost-minimization achieved by tall buildings on expensive downtown land).

The comparative statics of FAR restrictions illuminate the regulatory incidence. Suppose a city imposes an FAR cap of 1.0 in a downtown district where competitive equilibrium FAR would be 4.0. Developers can no longer build four-story apartments on small lots; instead, they must spread the same population across four times as much land (building single-story structures) or reduce the housing supply by 75 percent. If the city has a fixed population, rents rise until demand contracts by 75 percent—an implausibly large adjustment, implying that in practice the city would expand spatially, pushing development to the periphery. If the city is open, population declines by 75 percent (in that zone) as households relocate to other metropolitan areas. The shadow price of land inside the FAR-restricted zone equals the competitive rent plus the regulatory scarcity premium: $r_{\text{regulated}} = r_{\text{competitive}} + \lambda_{\text{FAR}}$, where $\lambda_{\text{FAR}}$ is the Lagrange multiplier on the binding constraint.

Parking mandates constitute a particularly pernicious form of quantity restriction because they force developers to bundle parking spaces with housing units at ratios far exceeding market demand. A typical requirement of two parking spaces per apartment adds $50,000 to $100,000 in construction costs in expensive urban land markets, even though many downtown residents do not own cars. The mandate functions as an in-kind tax: households must purchase parking they do not value, reducing their ability to pay for housing itself. The incidence falls on renters through higher rents and on marginal households who are priced out of the market entirely.

The filtering model adds dynamics to the static spatial equilibrium framework. Let housing quality $q$ depreciate at rate $\delta$ per year: $\dot{q} = -\delta q$. New construction occurs at quality $q_{\text{new}}$ and targets high-income households with willingness to pay $p(q_{\text{new}}, y_{\text{high}})$. As the housing stock ages, quality declines and the units filter down to middle-income households, then eventually to low-income households. In steady state, the flow of new construction must equal the flow of housing exiting the bottom of the quality distribution (through demolition or abandonment): $\dot{H}_{\text{new}} = \delta H_{\text{total}}$.

Zoning restrictions that prevent new construction at the top of the quality distribution break the filtering chain. If high-income households cannot purchase new units, they compete for slightly older housing, bidding up prices and displacing middle-income households downward. This cascade continues through the income distribution, ultimately forcing low-income households into overcrowding or homelessness. The welfare loss is borne disproportionately by the poorest households, who lack the income to win bidding wars for increasingly scarce units.

Empirical estimates of housing supply elasticity quantify the magnitude of regulatory distortion across cities. Saiz (2010) constructs a measure of geographic land supply constraints (mountains, water bodies) and regulatory constraints (Wharton Residential Land Use Regulatory Index) for US metropolitan areas. He estimates a reduced-form supply elasticity:

$$\frac{d \ln Q}{d \ln P} = \beta_{\text{geographic}} \cdot \text{GeographicConstraints} + \beta_{\text{regulatory}} \cdot \text{RegulatoryIndex} + \varepsilon$$

The results show that regulatory constraints reduce supply elasticity far more than geographic features: moving from the 25th to 75th percentile of regulatory restrictiveness reduces elasticity by 0.8, comparable to the effect of being completely surrounded by water (like Manhattan). This implies that high housing costs in expensive cities are primarily policy-driven rather than determined by immutable geographic scarcity.

The general equilibrium incidence of citywide zoning can be traced through a spatial equilibrium accounting. Start from a competitive baseline: measure the urban land area $A_0$, population $N_0$, average rent $\bar{r}_0$, and average commuting distance $\bar{d}_0$. Introduce binding zoning restrictions that reduce density by factor $\theta < 1$. Holding population fixed, the city must expand spatially: $A_1 = A_0 / \theta$. Average commuting distance increases: $\bar{d}_1 > \bar{d}_0$. Total transportation costs rise: $T_1 = t \cdot N_0 \cdot \bar{d}_1 > T_0$. Land rents adjust: inner-city rents fall due to forced lower density, but peripheral rents rise due to increased demand for distant land. The aggregate land value $V = \int_0^{\bar{d}_1} 2\pi d \cdot r(d) \, dd$ can rise or fall depending on the relative magnitudes of the inner rent decline and outer expansion. Household welfare unambiguously declines due to higher transportation costs and distorted housing consumption.

This spatial accounting reveals the fundamental tension in zoning policy: restrictions that raise property values for incumbent homeowners do so precisely by imposing costs on renters and potential residents. The distributional conflict is not a side effect but the core mechanism. When San Francisco downzones land from multi-family to single-family residential, existing homeowners capture a scarcity rent while renters face higher housing costs and longer commutes. The aggregate welfare effect is negative—transportation costs rise and housing consumption is distorted—but the benefits accrue to a politically powerful group (homeowners) while the costs are dispersed across a less organized group (renters and would-be residents).

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Operationalizing the monocentric city model for empirical policy analysis requires confronting several practical challenges: parameterizing the model to match observed data, solving for equilibrium under binding regulatory constraints, and conducting comparative statics to quantify the welfare effects of zoning reforms. The canonical approach estimates structural parameters (commuting cost $t$, utility function parameters $\alpha$, agricultural rent $r_A$) from observed rent gradients and commuting patterns, then uses the calibrated model to simulate counterfactual policies.

The first step is estimating the bid-rent gradient from hedonic regression on housing sales data. The canonical specification regresses log housing price on distance to the CBD, controlling for structural characteristics:

$$\ln(p_{i}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 d_i + \mathbf{X}_i' \gamma + \varepsilon_i$$

where $p_i$ is the sales price of property $i$, $d_i$ is distance to the CBD, and $\mathbf{X}_i$ includes bedrooms, bathrooms, square footage, lot size, and age. The coefficient $\beta_1$ estimates the percentage decline in housing price per mile, which under constant-returns-to-scale housing production equals the land rent gradient $\frac{d \ln r}{dd}$. Standard datasets include Zillow sales records, county assessor files, or proprietary multiple listing service (MLS) data. The primary challenge is defining the CBD: for polycentric cities, use employment-weighted centroids or estimate separate gradients for each employment center.

Commuting cost estimation uses revealed preference from household location choices. The canonical approach compares households at different distances from the CBD, inferring the implicit cost of commuting from the rent-distance trade-off. If two otherwise-identical households locate at distances $d_1$ and $d_2$ with rents $r_1$ and $r_2$, the marginal commuting cost satisfies:

$$t = \frac{(r_1 - r_2) \cdot \bar{h}}{d_1 - d_2}$$

where $\bar{h}$ is average housing consumption. This revealed preference approach captures both the direct time cost of commuting and the psychic cost of longer travel, both of which enter the household's spatial indifference condition. Typical estimates place $t$ between $0.20 and $0.50 per mile for US cities, varying with congestion levels and transit quality.

Solving for spatial equilibrium under binding zoning constraints requires numerical methods because the constrained optimum lacks a closed-form solution. The algorithm iterates between household optimization (given a rent function) and market clearing (given household demands). Start with an initial guess for the rent function $r^{(0)}(d)$. For each distance $d$, solve the household problem:

$$\max_{c, h} \, U(c, h) \quad \text{subject to} \quad c + r^{(0)}(d) \cdot h \leq w - t \cdot d \quad \text{and} \quad h \geq \underline{h}(d)$$

where $\underline{h}(d)$ is the minimum housing consumption imposed by zoning at distance $d$. This yields housing demand $h^{(1)}(d)$ and density $\rho^{(1)}(d) = 1/h^{(1)}(d)$. Integrate density to compute population: $N^{(1)} = \int_0^{\bar{d}} 2\pi d \cdot \rho^{(1)}(d) \, dd$. If $N^{(1)} > N$ (excess supply), reduce rents; if $N^{(1)} < N$ (excess demand), increase rents. Update the rent function: $r^{(1)}(d) = r^{(0)}(d) + \lambda (N - N^{(1)})$, where $\lambda$ is a step size. Iterate until convergence: $|N^{(k)} - N| < \varepsilon$ for some tolerance $\varepsilon$.

For large-scale policy simulations, discretize the city into concentric rings and solve the finite-dimensional market-clearing problem. Divide the urban area into $M$ rings of equal width $\Delta d$, indexed by $m = 1, \ldots, M$. In ring $m$, land area is $A_m = 2\pi m \Delta d \cdot \Delta d \approx 2\pi m (\Delta d)^2$. Zoning restricts density: $\rho_m \leq \bar{\rho}_m$ (maximum density) and $h_m \geq \underline{h}_m$ (minimum lot size). The market-clearing condition for ring $m$ is:

$$\rho_m \cdot A_m = \begin{cases} \frac{A_m}{h_m} & \text{if } h_m \geq \underline{h}_m \\ \frac{A_m}{\underline{h}_m} & \text{if } h_m < \underline{h}_m \end{cases}$$

with population constraint $\sum_{m=1}^M \rho_m A_m = N$. This reduces the infinite-dimensional problem to a system of $M$ nonlinear equations in $M$ unknowns (the rent levels $r_1, \ldots, r_M$), solvable via Newton-Raphson or other root-finding algorithms.

Comparative statics quantify the welfare effects of zoning reforms by computing equilibrium under the baseline policy and under the proposed reform, then measuring the compensating variation. For each household at distance $d$, compute the income transfer $CV(d)$ required to achieve the baseline utility under reform prices:

$$U(c_{\text{baseline}}(d), h_{\text{baseline}}(d)) = U(c_{\text{reform}}(d) + CV(d), h_{\text{reform}}(d))$$

Aggregate across households: $\text{Total Welfare Change} = \int_0^{\bar{d}} 2\pi d \cdot \rho(d) \cdot CV(d) \, dd$. A positive total welfare change indicates the reform is efficiency-improving. Decompose the welfare change into pecuniary effects (rent changes) and real resource effects (transportation cost changes): the former are pure transfers between landlords and tenants, while the latter represent deadweight loss or gain.

Failure modes in spatial equilibrium estimation arise from several sources. First, measurement error in distance: geocoding addresses to obtain CBD distance introduces noise, and polycentric cities lack a well-defined central point. Robustness checks use multiple CBD definitions (historical core, employment-weighted centroid, city hall) and verify that rent gradient estimates are stable. Second, omitted variable bias in hedonic regressions: if unobserved neighborhood quality correlates with distance (e.g., waterfronts are both distant and desirable), the estimated rent gradient confounds location value with amenity value. Instrument for distance using radial highways or historical streetcar lines to isolate the pure transportation-cost effect.

Third, endogenous zoning: municipalities may downzone in response to development pressure, making it difficult to identify the causal effect of regulation on housing supply. Bartik shift-share instruments address this: predict housing demand growth using national industry employment trends interacted with the city's baseline industry composition, then instrument actual demand with predicted demand. The exclusion restriction is that national industry trends affect local housing supply only through their effect on local labor demand, not through direct effects on zoning.

Fourth, dynamic sorting: the cross-sectional rent gradient reflects not only transportation costs but also household sorting by income. High-income households may locate downtown to consume urban amenities, while low-income households sort to the suburbs where land is cheap. The observed rent gradient then overstates the pure transportation cost gradient. Panel data on household moves can separate the sorting effect from the transportation cost effect: track households that move from the suburbs to the city and measure their willingness to pay for shorter commutes, holding amenities constant.

Fifth, supply-side heterogeneity: land is not homogenous, and construction costs vary with topography, soil quality, and existing infrastructure. Ignoring supply-side constraints biases welfare estimates: if developers cannot build densely on steep hillsides, the failure to do so reflects technology constraints rather than regulatory distortion. Saiz (2010) addresses this by constructing a geographic constraint index from topographic maps and water body data, then controlling for it in supply elasticity regressions.

The filtering model's prediction—that market-rate construction increases housing supply for all income groups—has been tested using quasi-experimental variation in new building permits. Mast (2019) uses address-level migration chains from credit bureau data to track where new luxury apartment residents moved from, where their former neighbors moved, and so on through multiple rounds of vacancy chains. He finds that for every 100 new market-rate units built, 70 moves occur within the metropolitan area (rather than from outside), and the vacancy chain reaches low-income neighborhoods within five moves. This confirms that new supply filters rapidly down the income distribution, validating the mechanism assumed in theoretical models.

Policy simulations of large-scale zoning reform require general equilibrium adjustments beyond partial equilibrium supply curves. When San Francisco hypothetically upzones all residential land to allow multi-family construction, the immediate effect is a rightward shift in housing supply. But the increased population also raises labor supply, which depresses wages and increases output. Higher output raises profits, which flow to landowners and capital owners, shifting income distribution. The full welfare accounting must track all these general equilibrium linkages, not merely the direct housing market effects.

The canonical quantitative spatial model (Ahlfeldt, Redding, Sturm, Wolf, 2015) estimates a system of structural equations for floor space supply, residential location choice, firm location choice, and commuting flows. The model parameters (housing supply elasticity, substitution elasticity between floor space and the composite good, productivity spillovers from density) are estimated from observed data using nonlinear GMM. Counterfactual simulations involve solving the spatial equilibrium under alternative zoning regimes, then computing welfare changes from the consumption-equivalent variation. This approach has been applied to quantify the welfare losses from restrictive zoning in London, Berlin, and other European cities, with results confirming that regulatory constraints reduce aggregate welfare by preventing efficient spatial sorting of households and firms.

Optimization strategies for housing policy focus on relaxing the binding constraints: upzoning near transit stations to increase density where transportation costs are lowest, reducing parking mandates to unbundle parking from housing, and streamlining permitting to reduce the time and uncertainty costs of development. The welfare gains are first-order: Hsieh and Moretti (2019) estimate that if New York, San Francisco, and San Jose had housing supply elasticities equal to the median US city, workers would reallocate from low-productivity cities to these high-productivity hubs, raising aggregate US GDP by 36 percent. These gains dwarf the efficiency improvements from traditional tax reforms or trade liberalization, highlighting that spatial misallocation driven by land use regulation is the dominant distortion in the modern US economy.

The key insight is that zoning restrictions are quantity constraints that bind far below competitive equilibrium density, generating massive scarcity rents for incumbent landowners and imposing welfare losses on renters and potential residents. The incidence depends on whether cities are open or closed, but in either case, the aggregate welfare effect is negative due to increased transportation costs and distorted housing consumption. Housing supply elasticity measures the tightness of the regulatory constraint: low-elasticity cities have binding zoning that prevents supply from responding to demand shocks, forcing all adjustment through prices. Filtering models reveal the dynamic mechanism by which market-rate construction benefits all income groups, a crucial insight for policy debates over affordable housing mandates versus supply-side deregulation. The empirical evidence overwhelmingly supports the theoretical prediction: cities with restrictive zoning exhibit higher housing costs, steeper rent gradients, longer commutes, and greater spatial segregation by income, with welfare losses measured in trillions of dollars of foregone GDP.
