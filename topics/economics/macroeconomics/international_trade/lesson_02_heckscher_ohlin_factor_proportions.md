# Lesson 2: Heckscher-Ohlin Theory, Factor Proportions

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The Heckscher-Ohlin model represents a profound departure from the Ricardian framework by grounding comparative advantage not in technological differences across nations but in the deeper structure of factor endowments. Where Ricardo attributed trade patterns to exogenous productivity differentials, Eli Heckscher and Bertil Ohlin constructed a theory in which countries export goods that intensively use their abundant factors of production and import goods that intensively use their scarce factors. This shift from technology to endowments provides a mechanistic explanation for why nations with similar production functions nonetheless specialize differently in international markets.

The fundamental intuition operates through a chain of price relationships linking factor endowments to goods prices through factor prices. A country abundantly endowed with capital relative to labor will exhibit a lower autarky rental rate on capital compared to the wage rate, making capital-intensive production techniques relatively cheaper. When this economy opens to trade, it discovers a comparative advantage in capital-intensive goods, exports those goods, and in doing so bids up the domestic rental rate on capital while depressing the real wage. The model thus embeds a complete general equilibrium structure connecting international goods flows to domestic income distribution, a feature entirely absent from Ricardian analysis.

The canonical Heckscher-Ohlin framework operates under a strict set of neoclassical assumptions designed to isolate the pure effects of factor proportions. Technologies are identical across countries, exhibiting constant returns to scale and diminishing marginal products for each factor. Production functions are such that goods differ in factor intensity: one good is capital-intensive at all factor price ratios, the other labor-intensive. Both goods are produced in both countries under autarky and free trade, ensuring interior solutions. Factors are perfectly mobile between sectors within a country but completely immobile across international borders. Trade in goods thus serves as an indirect mechanism for factor arbitrage, with capital-abundant countries effectively exporting capital services embodied in capital-intensive goods.

Consumer preferences are assumed homothetic and identical across countries, eliminating demand-side sources of comparative advantage. This assumption ensures that differences in consumption patterns at different income levels do not contaminate the supply-side factor endowment predictions. Markets are perfectly competitive, with no transportation costs, tariffs, or other trade frictions in the baseline model. These assumptions, while heroic, construct a theoretical laboratory in which the factor proportions mechanism operates in isolation.

The model's predictive power rests on the factor price equalization theorem, one of the most striking and counterintuitive results in all of trade theory. Under the maintained assumptions, free trade in goods alone suffices to equalize factor prices across countries, despite the complete immobility of factors themselves. The mechanism operates through goods price equalization: integrated world markets force goods prices to converge, and since technologies are identical, the same goods prices imply the same factor prices through the zero-profit conditions in each sector. A capital-abundant country's rental rate rises and wage falls toward the world equilibrium, while a labor-abundant country experiences the opposite adjustments. In the limit, both countries face identical wage-rental ratios, and the factor content of trade exactly offsets the differences in factor endowments.

The Stolper-Samuelson theorem formalizes the distributional consequences embedded in this adjustment process. An increase in the relative price of the capital-intensive good raises the real return to capital unambiguously, measured in terms of both goods, and lowers the real wage unambiguously. The theorem establishes a magnification effect: the percentage change in factor prices exceeds the percentage change in goods prices, creating sharp winners and losers from trade liberalization. Owners of the abundant factor benefit in real terms from trade opening, while owners of the scarce factor lose. This result provides the microeconomic foundations for political economy models of trade policy, explaining why factor owners rather than industry affiliations often predict protectionist coalitions.

The Rybczynski theorem addresses the comparative statics of endowment changes, holding goods prices constant. An increase in the endowment of capital leads to a more-than-proportional expansion of output in the capital-intensive sector and an absolute contraction of output in the labor-intensive sector. This ultra-pro-trade-biased growth effect arises because the expanding capital stock must be absorbed by shifting labor out of the labor-intensive sector, concentrating both factors in capital-intensive production. The Rybczynski theorem explains how endowment growth driven by capital accumulation or immigration reshapes a country's production structure and trade pattern even without technological change.

The mathematical architecture of the two-by-two Heckscher-Ohlin model admits elegant dual formulations linking quantities and prices. Let $w$ denote the wage, $r$ the rental rate, $p_1$ and $p_2$ the prices of goods one and two, and $a_{ij}$ the unit input requirement of factor $i$ in sector $j$. The zero-profit conditions under perfect competition impose:

$$a_{L1} w + a_{K1} r = p_1$$

$$a_{L2} w + a_{K2} r = p_2$$

where good one is capital-intensive such that $\frac{a_{K1}}{a_{L1}} > \frac{a_{K2}}{a_{L2}}$ at all factor price ratios. These two equations in two unknowns determine the wage and rental rate as functions of goods prices, establishing the Stolper-Samuelson mapping. The factor market clearing conditions complete the system:

$$a_{L1} Q_1 + a_{L2} Q_2 = L$$

$$a_{K1} Q_1 + a_{K2} Q_2 = K$$

where $Q_j$ denotes output of good $j$, $L$ the labor endowment, and $K$ the capital endowment. These two equations determine outputs as functions of endowments at given factor prices, establishing the Rybczynski mapping.

The factor content of trade provides an accounting identity linking trade flows to implicit factor service flows. A country's net exports of good $j$ embody factor services equal to the unit input requirements times the trade volume. Summing across goods, the factor content of net exports equals the difference between domestic factor endowments and domestic factor usage under autarky consumption patterns. The Heckscher-Ohlin-Vanek theorem formalizes this for the many-good, many-factor generalization: a country exports the services of its abundant factors and imports the services of its scarce factors, with "abundance" defined relative to the country's share of world GDP.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The operational mechanics of the Heckscher-Ohlin model emerge from the interactions between three distinct equilibrium loci in factor price space, goods space, and endowment space. Tracing these interactions reveals how factor endowments propagate through factor prices to goods prices and finally to trade patterns. The zero-profit conditions in each sector define factor price frontiers, downward-sloping relationships between wages and rental rates for which each sector breaks even at given goods prices. The capital-intensive sector's frontier lies inside the labor-intensive sector's frontier at low wages and outside at high wages, reflecting the differential factor intensities.

When goods prices are set by world markets, the intersection of these two frontiers uniquely determines the domestic factor prices. This intersection represents the point at which both sectors can coexist, earning zero economic profit. If domestic factor endowments are such that both goods are produced in equilibrium, the economy operates at this intersection point, and factor prices are pinned down entirely by goods prices, independent of endowments. This is the core of factor price equalization: so long as both countries produce both goods, they share identical factor prices determined by the common world goods prices.

The diversification cone delineates the set of endowment ratios compatible with incomplete specialization. Visualize the unit input requirement vectors for the two sectors as rays from the origin in labor-capital space. These rays define a cone, and any endowment ratio falling inside this cone permits production of both goods with full employment of both factors. Endowment ratios outside the cone force the economy to specialize completely in one good, as the other sector would require a factor proportion incompatible with available resources. Factor price equalization holds across countries only when their endowment ratios all fall within the same diversification cone at world prices.

The integrated world equilibrium serves as a conceptual benchmark, imagining all factors pooled into a single global economy. World goods prices clear world goods markets, determining a world factor price ratio through the zero-profit conditions. Each country's production and consumption are then determined as if factors could move freely, with the constraint that factors remain physically in their home countries. The factor content of trade emerges as the difference between each country's share of world factor endowments and its share of world factor usage in consumption, the latter determined by its share of world GDP under homothetic preferences.

The Stolper-Samuelson theorem's mechanics operate through the envelope properties of the profit functions and the cost minimization structure of production. Consider an increase in the relative price of the capital-intensive good. At the initial factor prices, producers in that sector now earn positive profits, while producers in the labor-intensive sector continue to break even. To restore zero-profit equilibrium, the rental rate must rise and the wage must fall. But the magnification effect ensures that the rental rate rises by a larger percentage than the goods price increase. This occurs because both sectors must adjust factor proportions in response to the relative factor price change, economizing on now-more-expensive capital and using more labor per unit output. The economy-wide factor market clearing then requires that the capital-intensive sector expand and the labor-intensive sector contract, reinforcing the initial price changes.

Formally, differentiating the zero-profit conditions and applying cost share weights yields the magnification relationships. Let $\theta_{ij}$ denote the cost share of factor $i$ in sector $j$. Then:

$$\theta_{L1} \hat{w} + \theta_{K1} \hat{r} = \hat{p}_1$$

$$\theta_{L2} \hat{w} + \theta_{K2} \hat{r} = \hat{p}_2$$

where the hat notation denotes percentage changes. Since good one is capital-intensive, $\theta_{K1} > \theta_{K2}$ and $\theta_{L1} < \theta_{L2}$. Solving these two equations for $\hat{w}$ and $\hat{r}$ in terms of $\hat{p}_1$ and $\hat{p}_2$ yields:

$$\hat{r} = \frac{\theta_{L2} \hat{p}_1 - \theta_{L1} \hat{p}_2}{\theta_{L2}\theta_{K1} - \theta_{L1}\theta_{K2}} > \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\theta_{K1} - \theta_{K2}} > \hat{p}_1 > \hat{p}_2$$

$$\hat{w} = \frac{\theta_{K1} \hat{p}_2 - \theta_{K2} \hat{p}_1}{\theta_{L2}\theta_{K1} - \theta_{L1}\theta_{K2}} < \hat{p}_2 < \hat{p}_1$$

when $\hat{p}_1 > \hat{p}_2$. The rental rate rises more than the price of the capital-intensive good, and the wage falls in relative terms, potentially even in absolute terms. This magnification property holds regardless of the magnitude of the price change, linking goods price movements to sharper factor price movements.

The Rybczynski theorem's mechanics arise from the dual factor market clearing conditions holding factor prices and hence factor proportions constant. An increase in the capital endowment with fixed factor prices requires absorption of the new capital into production. Since factor proportions in each sector are fixed by the given factor price ratio, the only margin of adjustment is the scale of each sector. The capital-intensive sector must expand to absorb the new capital, but this expansion pulls labor away from the labor-intensive sector. Full employment of labor requires that the labor-intensive sector contract, potentially dramatically, to release enough workers for the capital-intensive sector's expansion.

Formally, differentiating the factor market clearing conditions in percentage change form and holding factor proportions constant yields:

$$\lambda_{L1} \hat{Q}_1 + \lambda_{L2} \hat{Q}_2 = \hat{L}$$

$$\lambda_{K1} \hat{Q}_1 + \lambda_{K2} \hat{Q}_2 = \hat{K}$$

where $\lambda_{ij}$ denotes the share of factor $i$ employed in sector $j$. Solving for $\hat{Q}_1$ and $\hat{Q}_2$ when $\hat{L} = 0$ and $\hat{K} > 0$ produces:

$$\hat{Q}_1 = \frac{\lambda_{L2}}{\lambda_{L2}\lambda_{K1} - \lambda_{L1}\lambda_{K2}} \hat{K} > \hat{K}$$

$$\hat{Q}_2 = -\frac{\lambda_{L1}}{\lambda_{L2}\lambda_{K1} - \lambda_{L1}\lambda_{K2}} \hat{K} < 0$$

The capital-intensive sector output rises by more than the percentage increase in the capital endowment, while the labor-intensive sector output falls absolutely. This magnification effect on quantities mirrors the Stolper-Samuelson magnification effect on prices, both arising from the fixed-coefficients structure of production at given factor prices.

The empirical content of the Heckscher-Ohlin theorem faces immediate measurement challenges. Defining and measuring factor abundance requires specifying the relevant factors beyond simple labor-capital dichotomies. Human capital heterogeneity, natural resource endowments, arable land, and institutional quality all constitute potential factors of production. Factor intensity must be measured at some reference prices, but observed intensities vary with factor prices, creating endogeneity concerns. The theorem's prediction that countries export goods intensive in their abundant factors admits multiple testing strategies, each with distinct data requirements and maintained assumptions.

The factor content approach computes the implied factor services embodied in net exports and compares these to domestic factor endowments. Constructing a direct factor content measure requires input-output tables mapping gross output to factor usage in each sector, then multiplying by net exports to calculate the factor content of trade. The Heckscher-Ohlin-Vanek prediction states that the factor content of net exports should be proportional to the difference between a country's share of world endowments and its share of world consumption expenditure. Leontief's famous 1953 test calculated the capital and labor content of US exports and imports, finding paradoxically that the US exported labor-intensive goods despite being capital-abundant, contradicting the Heckscher-Ohlin prediction.

The Leontief paradox sparked decades of theoretical and empirical investigation. Early explanations invoked factor intensity reversals, demand bias, or tariff distortions. Factor intensity reversals occur if production functions differ sufficiently that the ranking of goods by capital intensity changes with factor prices, violating a key assumption. Demand bias toward capital-intensive goods in capital-abundant countries could offset the supply-side comparative advantage. Tariff protection of labor-intensive import-competing sectors could distort observed trade patterns away from Heckscher-Ohlin predictions.

Modern resolutions emphasize human capital heterogeneity and multi-dimensional factor spaces. Disaggregating labor into skilled and unskilled categories reveals that the US exports goods intensive in skilled labor while importing goods intensive in unskilled labor and physical capital. This finding aligns with an expanded Heckscher-Ohlin framework treating human capital as a distinct factor in which the US is abundantly endowed. Natural resource abundance provides another dimension: resource-rich countries export resource-intensive goods even when capital-scarce. The paradox dissolves when the factor space is sufficiently enriched to capture true endowment heterogeneity.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Empirical implementation of Heckscher-Ohlin tests demands careful data construction and econometric specification to isolate the endowment-based comparative advantage mechanism from confounding factors. The most robust testing strategies employ cross-country panel data with multiple factors and goods, estimating the relationship between factor endowments and net export patterns while controlling for technology differences, trade costs, and demand heterogeneity. The modern workhorse specification regresses the factor content of bilateral trade on relative factor endowments, incorporating country fixed effects to absorb time-invariant technology or preference differences and time fixed effects to control for global shocks.

Constructing factor content measures requires harmonized input-output tables across countries and time. The World Input-Output Database (WIOD) and the OECD's Inter-Country Input-Output (ICIO) tables provide multi-country, multi-sector frameworks tracking gross output, intermediate inputs, and factor payments. Researchers merge these with trade flow data from UN Comtrade or CEPII's BACI database, concorded to match sectoral classifications. The factor content of exports from country $i$ to country $j$ in sector $s$ equals the direct and indirect factor usage per unit of gross output in sector $s$ of country $i$, multiplied by the bilateral export value. Summing across sectors and subtracting imports yields net factor content of bilateral trade.

Measurement of factor endowments extends beyond aggregate labor and capital stocks. Labor must be disaggregated by skill level using educational attainment data from Barro-Lee or the International Labour Organization, constructing full-time equivalent skilled and unskilled labor supplies. Capital stocks are derived from perpetual inventory methods applying depreciation rates to investment flow data, often sourced from the Penn World Table. Natural resource endowments are proxied by production or reserve data for oil, natural gas, minerals, and arable land from the World Bank or US Geological Survey. Institutional quality indices from the World Bank's Worldwide Governance Indicators or Freedom House scores provide measures of intangible endowments affecting production efficiency.

The econometric specification must address several identification challenges. Technology differences across countries violate the identical production function assumption, creating measurement error in factor intensity rankings. Controlling for country-sector fixed effects absorbs time-invariant technology differences, but time-varying productivity shocks require instrumentation or additional controls. Trade costs introduce selection bias, as high-cost sectors may not trade at all, truncating the observed distribution. Gravity model controls for bilateral distance, common language, colonial ties, and regional trade agreements mitigate this concern. Demand-side heterogeneity from non-homothetic preferences requires including importer-sector fixed effects or per capita GDP interactions.

A state-of-the-art regression specification takes the form:

$$\text{FCT}_{ijst} = \beta_1 (K_i - K_j)_{t-1} + \beta_2 (H_i - H_j)_{t-1} + \beta_3 (L_i - L_j)_{t-1} + \gamma X_{ijt} + \alpha_{it} + \delta_{jt} + \mu_{st} + \epsilon_{ijst}$$

where $\text{FCT}_{ijst}$ denotes the factor content of trade from country $i$ to $j$ in sector $s$ at time $t$, $(K_i - K_j)_{t-1}$ is the lagged difference in capital endowments per worker, $(H_i - H_j)_{t-1}$ the difference in human capital per worker, $(L_i - L_j)_{t-1}$ the difference in labor force size, $X_{ijt}$ a vector of bilateral trade cost controls, and $\alpha_{it}$, $\delta_{jt}$, $\mu_{st}$ represent exporter-time, importer-time, and sector-time fixed effects respectively. The coefficients $\beta_1$, $\beta_2$, $\beta_3$ test the Heckscher-Ohlin prediction: capital-abundant countries should export capital services, human-capital-abundant countries should export skilled labor services, and so forth.

Failure modes in Heckscher-Ohlin testing often stem from inadequate factor disaggregation or misspecified trade cost controls. Aggregating all labor into a single homogeneous input obscures the skill-intensity dimension central to modern trade patterns. Using contemporaneous endowments rather than lagged values introduces reverse causality, as trade-induced factor accumulation feeds back into endowments. Omitting intermediate inputs in factor content calculations biases estimates, as global value chains fragment production across borders, embedding foreign factor content in domestic exports. The gross export value overstates domestic factor content; value-added trade measures correct this by subtracting foreign content.

The Stolper-Samuelson theorem's empirical validation requires identifying exogenous goods price shocks and tracing their effects on factor returns. Trade liberalization episodes provide natural experiments, comparing factor price changes in liberalizing countries to control countries. Difference-in-differences specifications estimate the differential effect of tariff reductions on industry wages and profits, testing whether capital-intensive industry liberalization raises capital returns relative to labor returns. The identification assumption requires that liberalization timing is orthogonal to pre-existing trends in factor prices, often questionable for endogenous policy changes.

Instrumental variables strategies exploit multilateral tariff reductions negotiated in GATT/WTO rounds, arguing that global negotiations are exogenous to individual country domestic factor markets. Two-stage least squares regressions instrument domestic goods prices with multilateral tariff changes, then estimate the second-stage effect of instrumented prices on wages by skill level. Studies of US, Brazilian, and Indian trade liberalizations find mixed evidence: skill premiums often rise rather than fall in capital-abundant countries opening to trade, contradicting simple Stolper-Samuelson predictions and motivating skill-biased technological change explanations.

The Rybczynski theorem's empirical content emerges in immigration and capital flow studies. Large immigration waves, such as the Mariel boatlift's Cuban refugee influx to Miami or Syrian refugee inflows to Turkey, provide quasi-experimental variation in local labor endowments. Synthetic control methods construct counterfactual regions with similar pre-treatment characteristics, comparing post-immigration output composition in treated versus synthetic control regions. Rybczynski predicts that immigrant-receiving regions expand labor-intensive sectors and contract capital-intensive sectors at given goods prices. Empirical findings show muted sectoral reallocation, suggesting adjustment through wage changes or technology adoption rather than pure Rybczynski output shifts.

Capital inflows from foreign direct investment or financial liberalization test the capital endowment dimension. Sudden stops in capital flows to emerging markets provide negative shocks to capital endowments, predicting contractions in capital-intensive manufacturing and expansions in labor-intensive services or agriculture. Event study designs compare sectoral output growth in capital-recipient countries before and after flow reversals, controlling for aggregate GDP changes. Evidence supports Rybczynski-type adjustment in manufacturing-heavy economies but less so in service-oriented economies, suggesting sector-specific factors or adjustment frictions matter.

Calibration exercises quantify Heckscher-Ohlin mechanisms in computable general equilibrium (CGE) models. Researchers parameterize multi-country, multi-sector models with CES production functions, inputting observed factor endowments, input-output linkages, and trade costs. Counterfactual simulations remove trade barriers or alter factor endowments, solving for new equilibrium goods prices, factor prices, and trade flows. Comparing simulated outcomes to data reveals how much of observed trade patterns the Heckscher-Ohlin mechanism explains. Modern CGE models incorporating monopolistic competition, fixed costs, and heterogeneous firms find that Heckscher-Ohlin forces account for roughly one-third of trade flow variance, with the remainder attributed to increasing returns, product differentiation, and firm-level productivity differences.

Operational best practices for Heckscher-Ohlin empirical work prioritize granular factor and sector disaggregation, explicit modeling of global value chains through value-added trade measures, and quasi-experimental identification strategies exploiting plausibly exogenous policy or migration shocks. Sensitivity analysis across alternative factor endowment measures, input-output table vintages, and trade cost specifications assesses robustness. Distinguishing short-run adjustment dynamics from long-run equilibrium requires dynamic panel specifications with lagged dependent variables or error correction models, recognizing that factor reallocation and capital accumulation occur gradually rather than instantaneously.

The theory's continued relevance for policy analysis stems from its sharp predictions about distributional consequences and endowment-driven specialization patterns. Trade negotiators employ Heckscher-Ohlin logic to forecast which domestic industries and factor groups gain or lose from liberalization, informing adjustment assistance programs and coalition-building strategies. Development economists apply the framework to industrialization strategies, recognizing that labor-abundant developing countries possess comparative advantage in labor-intensive manufacturing, while premature specialization in capital-intensive sectors risks misallocating scarce resources. Climate policy intersects with Heckscher-Ohlin through carbon intensity as an implicit factor endowment: carbon taxes or cap-and-trade systems alter effective factor costs, reshaping comparative advantage toward low-emission production.

The model's limitations motivate extensions relaxing core assumptions. Increasing returns to scale and imperfect competition generate trade even between countries with identical endowments, as in new trade theory. Heterogeneous firms within industries sort into exporting versus domestic production based on productivity, fragmenting the clean sectoral reallocation predictions. Technology differences persist across countries and evolve endogenously through innovation and diffusion, decoupling goods prices from factor prices. Intermediate input trade and global value chains fragment production stages across borders, embedding foreign factor content in exports and complicating factor content accounting. Each extension enriches the empirical content while preserving the core insight that factor endowments shape trade patterns through factor price linkages.
