# Lesson 4: Market Structures with Strategic Behavior

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The neoclassical edifice rests upon a deceptively simple premise: rational economic agents maximize utility or profit subject to constraints, and market prices equilibrate supply and demand. Yet beneath this elegant abstraction lies a taxonomy of market structures that fundamentally alter the mechanics of price formation, output determination, and welfare distribution. The distinction between perfect competition and imperfect markets—monopoly, oligopoly, and monopolistic competition—represents not merely academic taxonomy but the skeleton key to understanding how real economies generate rents, allocate resources inefficiently, and create opportunities for strategic behavior.

Perfect competition serves as the neoclassical benchmark. In this idealized state, no single firm possesses price-setting power; each producer faces a perfectly elastic demand curve at the market price. The firm is a price-taker, not a price-maker. This condition emerges when four critical assumptions hold simultaneously: (1) many buyers and sellers such that no individual transaction moves the market price, (2) homogeneous products indistinguishable across suppliers, (3) perfect information available costlessly to all participants, and (4) free entry and exit permitting firms to flow toward profitable opportunities without institutional friction. Under these conditions, the competitive equilibrium generates an allocation in which Price equals Marginal Cost at the equilibrium quantity, and the First Fundamental Welfare Theorem holds: no reallocation can make someone better off without making another worse off. This is the theoretical lens through which neoclassicalists view competitive markets as self-correcting engines of efficiency.

Yet the real world rarely satisfies these stringent conditions. When any assumption is relaxed—when products differentiate, information becomes asymmetric, entry barriers crystallize, or participants become few enough to possess market power—the apparatus shifts fundamentally. Firms transition from price-takers to price-makers, capable of restricting output below the competitive quantity to elevate price above marginal cost. This gap between Price and Marginal Cost represents economic rent: a return above the opportunity cost of production, extractable precisely because the firm possesses some degree of monopoly power.

The neoclassical framework decomposes market power into two conceptual tributaries: first, the structural characteristics of the market—the number and size distribution of firms, the ease of entry, the degree of product homogeneity—which determine how much market power *could* exist; second, the strategic behavior of firms within that structure, which determines how market power *actually* manifests in pricing, output, investment, and welfare outcomes. A market with high structural barriers to entry but passive, non-strategic competitors may generate less monopoly rent than a seemingly competitive market where firms engage in aggressive strategic positioning. This integration of structure and strategy represents the modern neoclassical synthesis, bridged by game theory.

Game theory injects behavioral realism into market analysis by modeling firms as strategic actors whose decisions depend not only on their own profit-maximization calculus but on the anticipated responses of competitors. Unlike the perfectly competitive firm, which ignores competitors (because none exist with meaningful scale), the oligopolist must form beliefs about rival behavior: Will competitors match my price cut, or will they hold firm? Will they expand capacity in response to my market entry, or accommodate my presence? These conjectures about rival behavior generate different equilibrium outcomes. The Cournot model assumes firms choose quantities simultaneously, each believing rivals will maintain their current output; the Bertrand model assumes firms set prices simultaneously, with perfect substitutability between products; the Stackelberg model introduces sequential leadership, where the first-mover commits to an output level knowing rivals will respond optimally. Each framework yields distinct predictions about pricing, output, and welfare.

The concept of market power, formally, rests upon the price elasticity of demand facing the individual firm. In perfect competition, the firm faces an infinitely elastic demand curve; even a tiny price increase causes quantity demanded to collapse to zero, as buyers shift to perfect substitutes. The perfectly competitive firm's price-taking behavior emerges not from altruism but from this structural reality: any attempt to raise price above the market level yields zero sales. Conversely, the monopolist faces the downward-sloping market demand curve itself; raising price reduces quantity demanded, but some customers remain inelastic enough that total revenue can increase. The firm's market power can be quantified through the Lerner Index, defined as $L = \frac{P - MC}{P} = -\frac{1}{\epsilon_d}$, where $\epsilon_d$ is the price elasticity of demand facing the firm. This formulation reveals the deep connection: market power is not some mysterious force but simply the inverse of demand elasticity. A firm with market power operates on an inelastic portion of its demand curve; a competitive firm faces infinite elasticity and cannot move along any demand curve.

Price discrimination emerges as a natural extension of market power. When a firm possesses the ability to segment customers into groups with different price elasticities of demand, and can prevent arbitrage between segments, it has incentive to charge higher prices to inelastic customers and lower prices to elastic ones. First-degree (perfect) price discrimination extracts the entire consumer surplus by charging each customer their maximum willingness to pay; second-degree price discrimination uses quantity-based pricing schedules (bulk discounts, tiered pricing) to induce self-selection; third-degree price discrimination directly separates markets and charges different per-unit prices to each segment. Each form requires different prerequisites and generates different welfare implications. The fundamental insight is that price discrimination is not intrinsically irrational or exploitative in the neoclassical sense—it is the rational response of a profit-maximizing firm with market power and information about heterogeneous consumer demands. Whether it harms welfare depends on context: first-degree discrimination eliminates deadweight loss by pricing all customers who value the product above marginal cost, yet transfers all surplus to the firm; third-degree discrimination may reduce total quantity and welfare if it fragments the market inefficiently.

The transition from perfect competition to monopoly, and the intermediate oligopoly landscape, thus represents a continuum of market power rather than discrete categories. Understanding this continuum requires mastery of the structural foundations (entry barriers, product differentiation, information structure), the strategic tools (pricing, output, capacity, R&D investment), and the equilibrium solution concepts (Nash equilibrium, perfect Bayesian equilibrium, subgame perfection) that pin down outcomes. The neoclassical apparatus provides the intellectual machinery for this analysis; the challenge for practitioners is learning to identify which framework applies to a given market empirically, and how to predict the behavioral responses that generate observable market outcomes.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The architecture of market structure analysis bifurcates into two layers: the static, comparative-statics layer that examines how equilibrium changes when exogenous parameters shift, and the dynamic layer that tracks how firms' strategic interactions unfold over time. We begin with the static foundations and progressively integrate strategic complexity.

### Perfect Competition: The Benchmark Case

In perfect competition, equilibrium arises from the intersection of market supply and demand without any single firm exercising discretion over price or output. The individual firm's optimization problem is trivial: given an exogenously fixed price $p$, maximize profit by choosing output $q$ such that Marginal Cost equals price. Formally, the firm solves $\max_q \pi = pq - C(q)$, yielding the first-order condition $p = MC(q)$. This defines the firm's supply curve; since the firm earns zero economic profit in long-run equilibrium (price equals average total cost), entry and exit cease. The market price clears such that aggregate quantity supplied equals aggregate quantity demanded.

The welfare properties are paramount. Consumer surplus (the area under the demand curve above price) plus producer surplus (price times quantity minus variable costs) sum to total surplus. At the competitive equilibrium, this total surplus is maximized: no reallocation of production across firms or consumption across households can increase total welfare. Deadweight loss is zero. This efficiency emerges from the price mechanism's role as an information aggregator: the price simultaneously conveys information about scarcity and incentivizes efficient production without any central authority needing to know consumer preferences or production costs.

The entry and exit mechanism warrants careful exposition. When existing firms earn economic profit (price exceeds average total cost), new entrants perceive opportunity and enter the market. As they do, market supply expands, pushing the market price downward. Entry ceases only when price has fallen to the level of average total cost for the marginal firm, eliminating positive profit. Conversely, if price falls below average total cost, losses trigger exit; as firms depart, market supply contracts, price rises, and the process halts when price reaches the break-even level. This self-equilibrating dynamic requires no external regulator and no firm possessing knowledge of industry-wide costs; individual profit-maximization decentralizes the information discovery process. However, this mechanism critically depends on free entry and exit. When entry barriers exist—whether capital requirements, regulatory licensing, intellectual property protection, or network effects—this self-equilibrating pressure fails.

### Monopoly: The Single-Seller Extreme

Monopoly represents the opposite pole: a single firm faces the market demand curve directly and chooses price and quantity jointly. The monopolist internalizes the demand curve's negative slope; unlike the competitive firm, it recognizes that reducing output elevates price. The optimization problem is $\max_q \pi = p(q) \cdot q - C(q)$, where price depends on quantity sold. The first-order condition is $p + q \frac{dp}{dq} = MC(q)$, or equivalently, $MR(q) = MC(q)$, where Marginal Revenue is $MR = p + q \frac{dp}{dq}$. Since $\frac{dp}{dq} < 0$ for a downward-sloping demand curve, $MR < p$ always; the monopolist's marginal revenue falls below price. Profit-maximization requires equating marginal revenue to marginal cost, not price to marginal cost.

This distinction is mechanically crucial. In perfect competition, $MR = p$, so $p = MC$ in equilibrium. The monopolist, facing $MR < p$, sets $p > MC$; output is restricted below the competitive level, and the firm earns economic profit. The deadweight loss equals the lost surplus from trades that would have been mutually beneficial at competitive prices but are foregone under monopoly: consumers willing to pay above MC but below monopoly price cannot purchase, and these unrealized gains represent welfare destruction.

The Lerner Index quantifies this markup: $L = \frac{p - MC}{p} = -\frac{1}{\epsilon_d}$. For a monopolist facing constant elasticity demand $q = Ap^{-\epsilon}$, equilibrium occurs where $MR = MC$, implying $\frac{p - MC}{p} = \frac{1}{\epsilon}$. If demand elasticity is $\epsilon = 2$ (a unit-elastic increase in the elasticity magnitude), the monopoly markup is 100%; if $\epsilon = 1.5$, the markup rises to 200%. As demand becomes more elastic (larger $|\epsilon|$), the monopolist's pricing power diminishes, and the Lerner Index approaches zero.

Entry barriers are the structural prerequisite for monopoly sustainability. In the absence of barriers, positive monopoly profit attracts entry. Barriers take multiple forms: economies of scale so extreme that a single firm can serve the entire market at lower average cost than any two competitors (natural monopoly); high capital or sunk costs that deter new entrants; exclusive control of essential inputs (OPEC's oil reserves, Alcoa's bauxite mines historically); intellectual property protection (patents, copyrights); or regulatory licensing (telecom franchises, broadcast licenses, professional licensing). The nature of the barrier shapes the monopoly's vulnerability to disruption. A natural monopoly rooted in economies of scale persists because entry itself becomes uneconomic; a patent-protected monopoly faces an expiration date; a regulatory monopoly depends on political sustainability.

### Oligopoly and Strategic Interaction

Oligopoly introduces the essential element of strategic interdependence: a few firms of meaningful size know their decisions affect rivals' incentives and responses. Unlike the competitive firm, which ignores competitors, or the monopolist, which faces no competitors, oligopolists must form expectations about rival behavior. Game theory provides the solution concept: a Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile in which each firm's choice is optimal given rivals' choices, and no firm wishes to unilaterally deviate. The equilibrium is self-sustaining because each player's strategy is a best response to what others are doing.

The Cournot model of simultaneous quantity competition illustrates the mechanics. Suppose two firms (firm 1 and firm 2) each choose output $q_1$ and $q_2$ simultaneously, facing inverse demand $p = a - b(q_1 + q_2)$ and identical marginal costs $c$. Firm 1 solves $\max_{q_1} \pi_1 = [a - b(q_1 + q_2)] q_1 - c q_1$, taking $q_2$ as given. The first-order condition is $a - b(2q_1 + q_2) - c = 0$, yielding the best-response function $q_1^* = \frac{a - c - bq_2}{2b}$. By symmetry, firm 2's best response is $q_2^* = \frac{a - c - bq_1}{2b}$. The Nash equilibrium occurs where both best responses intersect: $q_1 = q_2 = \frac{a - c}{3b}$. Each firm produces one-third of what a monopolist would produce (which is $\frac{a - c}{2b}$), and equilibrium price is $p = c + \frac{a - c}{3}$.

This simple duopoly already illuminates the logic of oligopolistic competition. With $n$ symmetric firms in Cournot competition, each equilibrium quantity is $\frac{a - c}{(n+1)b}$, and price converges to $p = c + \frac{a - c}{n+1}$ as $n$ rises. When $n \to \infty$, price approaches marginal cost and oligopoly collapses toward perfect competition. When $n = 1$, the monopoly pricing emerges. When $n = 2$, Cournot duopoly yields an intermediate outcome. The fundamental logic: as the number of firms increases, each firm's market share shrinks, incentives to restrict output weaken, and competitive pressure intensifies.

Bertrand competition, where firms choose prices rather than quantities, generates a starkly different outcome despite superficial similarity. Suppose two firms set prices $p_1$ and $p_2$ simultaneously, with demand $q_i = a - bp_i + bp_j$ (where $j \neq i$), and identical marginal costs $c$. If the goods are perfect substitutes and firms have capacity to serve all demand at their chosen price, then any price above marginal cost invites the rival to undercut slightly and capture the entire market. The unique Nash equilibrium is $p_1 = p_2 = c$: both firms price at marginal cost despite being only two firms. This is the "Bertrand paradox": duopoly under price competition yields the same outcome as perfect competition with infinitely many firms. The resolution lies in assumptions. If products are differentiated, capacity constraints exist, or firms cannot perfectly monitor and instantly respond to rivals' prices, equilibrium prices exceed marginal cost. The structure of competition (quantity-setting vs. price-setting) and the nature of product differentiation thus matter profoundly for equilibrium outcomes.

Stackelberg competition introduces sequential commitment. Firm 1 (the leader) chooses output $q_1$ first, and firm 2 (the follower) observes this choice and responds optimally. Firm 2 solves $\max_{q_2} \pi_2 = [a - b(q_1 + q_2)] q_2 - c q_2$ given $q_1$, yielding the reaction function $q_2(q_1) = \frac{a - c - bq_1}{2b}$. Firm 1, anticipating this response, solves $\max_{q_1} \pi_1 = [a - b(q_1 + q_2(q_1))] q_1 - c q_1$. Substituting the reaction function: $\max_{q_1} \pi_1 = [a - b(q_1 + \frac{a - c - bq_1}{2b})] q_1 - c q_1 = [\frac{a + c}{2} - \frac{bq_1}{2}] q_1 - c q_1$. The first-order condition yields $q_1^* = \frac{a - c}{2b}$. The follower then produces $q_2^* = \frac{a - c}{4b}$. The leader produces twice the follower's quantity; total output exceeds the Cournot level; price falls between the Cournot price and marginal cost. The first-mover advantage (the ability to commit to output before rivals respond) translates into higher profit, yet this profit advantage is smaller than in monopoly because the follower competes.

### Price Discrimination: Mechanisms and Welfare

Price discrimination exploits market power by charging different prices to different customer segments. First-degree discrimination charges each customer their reservation price—the maximum willingness to pay. This requires perfect information about each buyer's demand and perfect ability to prevent resale (arbitrage). Under first-degree discrimination, every unit trades as long as the buyer's willingness to pay exceeds marginal cost; total quantity equals the perfectly competitive quantity, and deadweight loss is zero. All consumer surplus is converted to producer surplus (firm profit). This is welfare-neutral in aggregate (total surplus unchanged) but highly regressive in distribution (the firm captures all gains).

Second-degree discrimination uses pricing schedules to induce self-selection without direct information about individual willingness to pay. Bulk discounts are the canonical example: the firm offers a low per-unit price for large quantities and a high per-unit price for small quantities. Consumers self-sort according to their demand intensity; high-demand consumers purchase large quantities and enjoy lower per-unit prices; low-demand consumers purchase small quantities at higher per-unit prices. The firm cannot prevent a high-demand consumer from buying small quantities at high per-unit prices (this is always available), but the incentive structure makes large purchases more profitable. The elegance of second-degree discrimination is that it achieves partial separation without requiring direct price discrimination per se; quantity discounts constitute a uniform pricing schedule, not targeted differentiation.

Third-degree discrimination directly separates markets and charges different per-unit prices to each segment. Geographic market segmentation (higher prices in countries with higher willingness to pay), age-based pricing (student discounts, senior discounts), or usage-based pricing (time-of-day electricity pricing, peak-hour tolls) exemplify third-degree discrimination. Each segment faces a different linear price; the firm equates marginal revenue in each segment to (the same) marginal cost. Segments with more inelastic demand pay higher prices. The welfare effects are ambiguous. If third-degree discrimination causes high-elasticity (price-sensitive) segments to reduce consumption below competitive levels, deadweight loss can increase. However, if the alternative to discrimination is uniform monopoly pricing (no discrimination at all), third-degree discrimination may improve welfare by serving additional price-sensitive customers.

Consider the mathematics. Suppose demand in segment $i$ is $q_i = a_i - b_i p_i$, with marginal cost $c$. Without discrimination, the monopolist sets a uniform price $p$ maximizing $\pi = p(Q(p)) - cQ(p)$, where $Q(p) = \sum q_i(p)$. With third-degree discrimination, the firm sets prices $p_1, p_2$ separately, maximizing $\pi = \sum [p_i q_i(p_i) - c q_i(p_i)]$. The first-order conditions are $MR_i = c$ for each segment, where $MR_i = p_i + q_i \frac{dp_i}{dq_i} = p_i(1 - \frac{1}{\epsilon_i})$. Segments with lower elasticity $|\epsilon_i|$ pay higher markups. The distributional effect is regressive (inelastic consumers, often low-income in necessities like healthcare or utilities, pay more), but the quantity effect can be progressive (price-sensitive, lower-income consumers may consume more at lower discriminatory prices than they would under uniform monopoly pricing).

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

### Boundary Conditions and Equilibrium Existence

Game-theoretic solutions hinge critically on assumptions about information, rationality, and strategy spaces. Pure-strategy Nash equilibria do not always exist; Bertrand competition among identical firms with downward-sloping residual demands (due to capacity constraints or product differentiation) may lack a pure-strategy equilibrium and require mixed-strategy analysis. In mixed-strategy equilibrium, firms randomize over prices according to probability distributions such that no competitor can profitably deviate. Existence of Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies is guaranteed under broad conditions (Rosen's 1965 theorem for symmetric games), but the resulting equilibrium often involves positive waste: firms price inefficiently to deter rivals from undercutting.

Capacity constraints interact critically with price competition. If each firm has capacity $\bar{q}$ below which it cannot service demand, a firm that attempts to undercut rivals faces rationing risk. With large capacities, Bertrand undercutting dominates; with tight capacities, firms cannot service entire market demand at lower prices and equilibrium prices rise. The mixed-strategy equilibrium under capacity constraints exhibits discontinuities in pricing; firms use a mixed strategy precisely to make rivals indifferent between pricing high (capturing rationed demand at high margin) and low (capturing full residual demand at low margin).

Firm heterogeneity—differences in costs, capacities, or product quality—breaks the symmetry of standard models and complicates equilibrium. When firms have asymmetric marginal costs, the low-cost firm earns higher profit in competition and has incentive to preempt high-cost rivals through capacity expansion or R&D investment. The resulting equilibrium involves incumbent advantages rooted in structural (cost) differences rather than market power per se. Disentangling these effects empirically requires careful econometric identification.

### Strategic Dynamics: Commitment, Reputation, and Repeated Games

Static game theory assumes a one-shot interaction; firms simultaneously choose strategies knowing the payoff structure, and play ends. Real markets involve repeated interaction. Repeated games unlock cooperation possibilities absent in one-shot games. In infinitely repeated Cournot duopoly, firms can sustain collusive (monopoly) output levels through trigger strategies: each firm produces the monopoly quantity as long as rivals do; if a rival deviates, all firms revert to one-shot Nash equilibrium forever. The threat of reversion is credible if the discounted stream of Nash profits forever exceeds the one-time gain from deviation plus subsequent Nash punishment. Formally, let $\pi^m$ be monopoly profit per period (split equally), $\pi^{dev}$ be the profit from one-shot deviation (when rivals continue monopoly play), and $\pi^c$ be one-shot Nash profit. Cooperation is sustainable if $\frac{\pi^m}{1 - \delta} \geq \pi^{dev} + \frac{\delta \pi^c}{1 - \delta}$, where $\delta$ is the discount factor. Rearranging: $\delta \geq \frac{\pi^{dev} - \pi^m}{\pi^{dev} - \pi^c}$. Cooperation is easier to sustain (lower $\delta$ threshold) when the deviation gain is small relative to the gap between one-shot Nash and monopoly profits.

Reputation effects extend this logic.

Reputation effects extend this logic to entry deterrence and signaling contexts. An incumbent with a long horizon has incentive to build a reputation for toughness—responding aggressively to any challenger even when accommodation would be myopically optimal—because the reputational return across future potential entrants exceeds the short-run cost of fighting. Kreps and Wilson's sequential equilibrium formalization shows that even a small probability that the incumbent is a "tough type" is sufficient to sustain deterrence across many entry episodes, since entrants rationally update beliefs after observing aggressive responses and eventually refrain from entering altogether. Commitment devices, such as irreversible capacity investment, long-term contracts with customers, or publicly announced pricing rules, transform otherwise incredible threats into credible ones by making deviation costly to the firm itself. The logic of strategic commitment therefore unifies entry deterrence, limit pricing, and predatory pricing under a single framework: actions that are costly in isolation become rational when they shift rivals' beliefs and thereby alter equilibrium behavior downstream. For empirical identification, the central challenge is distinguishing genuine commitment from cheap talk, and distinguishing deterrence from efficiency-driven market structure. Structural IO approaches estimate demand, cost, and conduct parameters jointly using firm-level data, allowing researchers to test whether observed markups and quantities are consistent with Nash-Cournot, collusion, or competitive benchmarks, while accounting for unobserved productivity heterogeneity and endogenous market structure.

The synthesis across all market structures examined in this lesson is that strategic behavior adds a layer of equilibrium selection and welfare ambiguity on top of the standard neoclassical price-quantity determination. Perfect competition and monopoly represent polar cases where strategic interaction is either absent or trivial; oligopoly is the empirically relevant intermediate where game-theoretic reasoning is indispensable. The Cournot-Bertrand distinction reveals that the mode of competition, not just market concentration, determines outcomes. Sequential games demonstrate that timing and commitment power create first- or second-mover advantages that partition the gains from trade asymmetrically. Repeated interaction rescues cooperation as an equilibrium possibility but only under sufficient patience and observability, explaining why antitrust regulators monitor communication and transparency practices that facilitate tacit collusion. Taken together, these mechanisms imply that market structure is endogenous: firms invest, signal, and commit precisely to manipulate the equilibrium in which they subsequently compete, so any welfare assessment must account for both the static deadweight loss from market power and the dynamic costs and benefits of the strategic investments that generate or entrench that power.
