# Lesson 3: Strategic Behavior in Games

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Classical game theory, born from the foundational work of von Neumann and Morgenstern and refined through Nash's equilibrium concept, rests on a set of powerful but restrictive assumptions. The canonical rational agent possesses unlimited cognitive capacity, processes information instantaneously, holds common knowledge of rationality among all players, and maximizes expected utility with mechanical precision. Under these assumptions, the Prisoner's Dilemma yields mutual defection, the Ultimatum Game predicts minimal offers accepted with certainty, and Public Goods games collapse to zero contribution through backward induction.

Yet when these games move from the blackboard to the laboratory, a systematic and replicable pattern emerges that shatters the classical predictions. Subjects contribute substantial fractions of their endowments to public goods, even in one-shot anonymous interactions. Responders in Ultimatum Games reject offers below twenty to thirty percent of the stake, sacrificing material payoffs to punish perceived unfairness. Trust Game senders transfer significant amounts to anonymous receivers, and receivers reciprocate at rates far exceeding the subgame perfect equilibrium of zero return. Coordination failures persist in games with Pareto-ranked equilibria, and learning trajectories deviate systematically from Bayesian updating.

Experimental economics has documented these behavioral regularities with sufficient statistical power and cross-cultural replication to establish them as empirical facts requiring theoretical accommodation. The enterprise of behavioral game theory constructs formal models that incorporate bounded rationality, social preferences, and cognitive hierarchy into strategic reasoning. These models do not abandon the optimization framework of neoclassical economics but rather relax specific assumptions to achieve empirical adequacy while preserving predictive discipline.

The experimental method imposes rigorous constraints that distinguish it from both pure theory and observational field data. Laboratory control allows the researcher to induce precise monetary payoffs, implement information structures with certainty, enforce anonymity to isolate social preference channels, and replicate treatments across subject pools to test external validity. The induced value theory of Vernon Smith establishes that if subjects are given monetary rewards monotonically related to abstract outcomes, their behavior reveals preferences over those outcomes under controlled conditions. This methodology transforms strategic interaction from an untestable thought experiment into a measurable phenomenon with quantifiable treatment effects.

The core invariants of experimental strategic behavior crystallize into several robust findings that have survived decades of replication and robustness checks. First, social preferences are non-negligible and heterogeneous. A significant fraction of subjects exhibit inequality aversion, reciprocity, and altruism even in one-shot anonymous interactions where reputation and repeated game incentives are eliminated. Second, strategic sophistication varies systematically across subjects and can be characterized by cognitive hierarchy models, where level-0 players randomize, level-1 players best-respond to level-0, and higher levels iterate this reasoning. Third, learning dynamics in repeated games follow reinforcement patterns rather than Bayesian belief updating, with recent payoffs weighted more heavily than historical data. Fourth, framing effects and default options influence behavior even when the strategic structure remains invariant, violating the procedure invariance assumption of standard theory.

The Public Goods game serves as the canonical laboratory environment for studying cooperation, free-riding incentives, and institutional design. In the linear public goods mechanism, each of $n$ players receives an endowment $w$ and simultaneously chooses a contribution $g_i \in [0, w]$ to a group account. Total contributions are multiplied by a marginal per capita return $m \in (1/n, 1)$ and distributed equally among all players. The individual payoff function takes the form:

$$\pi_i = w - g_i + m \sum_{j=1}^{n} g_j$$

The dominant strategy equilibrium under pure self-interest is $g_i^* = 0$ for all players, since each dollar contributed returns only $m < 1$ dollars to the contributor but generates $mn$ dollars of total group surplus. This creates the social dilemma: individual rationality leads to zero contribution, yet the Pareto optimum occurs at full contribution where each player receives $w(1 + m(n-1))$, which exceeds the defection payoff of $w$ whenever $m > 1/n$.

Experimental evidence systematically rejects the zero-contribution prediction. In one-shot Public Goods games, average contributions typically range from forty to sixty percent of the endowment, with substantial heterogeneity across subjects. The distribution is bimodal, with clusters of free-riders contributing zero and conditional cooperators contributing amounts correlated with their beliefs about others' contributions. When the game is repeated for ten to twenty rounds with fixed groups and no communication, contributions decay toward the Nash prediction but rarely reach complete free-riding. This decay pattern reflects both learning about others' types and strategic adjustment, as conditional cooperators reduce contributions in response to observed free-riding.

The introduction of punishment mechanisms transforms the cooperation landscape. In the Public Goods game with costly peer punishment, after contribution decisions are revealed, each player can assign punishment points to any other player at a personal cost. If player $i$ assigns $p_{ij}$ punishment points to player $j$, player $i$ pays $c \cdot p_{ij}$ and player $j$ loses $k \cdot p_{ij}$ in payoff, where the punishment technology satisfies $k > c$ to create a credible deterrent. This mechanism is strategically paradoxical: in the final period, rational players will not incur costs to punish, and by backward induction, the subgame perfect equilibrium unravels to zero contribution and zero punishment in all periods.

Yet experimental subjects employ punishment aggressively, targeting low contributors even in one-shot and final-period interactions. This peer punishment sustains high contribution levels, often above eighty percent of the endowment in repeated games. The punishment pattern exhibits strong negative reciprocity: the probability and magnitude of punishment increase sharply as the target's contribution falls below the group average. Punishment is not merely error or confusion, as it persists across hundreds of rounds and appears in diverse subject pools spanning multiple continents and cultural contexts. Neuroimaging studies using fMRI reveal that punishing free-riders activates the dorsal striatum, a region associated with reward processing, suggesting that the act of costly punishment itself generates utility through satisfaction of reciprocity preferences.

The Ultimatum Game isolates distributional preferences and rejection behavior in a stark bilateral bargaining environment. The proposer receives an endowment $S$ and offers a division $(S - x, x)$ to the responder, who observes the offer and either accepts, in which case the division is implemented, or rejects, in which case both players receive zero. The subgame perfect equilibrium under self-interested preferences predicts that the proposer offers the smallest monetary unit $\epsilon$ and the responder accepts, since any positive amount exceeds the outside option of zero.

Experimental modal offers cluster around fifty percent of the stake, with the distribution concentrated between forty and sixty percent. Offers below twenty percent face rejection rates exceeding fifty percent, and rejection probability increases sharply as offers decrease. This behavior is stable across stake sizes ranging from ten dollars to three months' salary in low-income countries, suggesting that distributional concerns are not driven by the opportunity cost of small laboratory stakes. The rejection pattern cannot be attributed to incomplete information or reputation building, as one-shot anonymous protocols eliminate these channels.

The responder's rejection decision reveals a willingness to sacrifice material payoff to punish unfair proposals, consistent with models of inequality aversion such as the Fehr-Schmidt utility function:

$$U_i(x_i, x_j) = x_i - \alpha_i \max\{x_j - x_i, 0\} - \beta_i \max\{x_i - x_j, 0\}$$

where $\alpha_i$ represents the disutility from disadvantageous inequality (envy) and $\beta_i$ represents the disutility from advantageous inequality (guilt), with the canonical calibration $\alpha_i > \beta_i \geq 0$ reflecting stronger aversion to being behind than ahead. A responder with $\alpha_i > 1$ will reject offers below $(S \alpha_i)/(1 + \alpha_i)$, rationalizing observed rejection thresholds with plausible parameter values. The proposer, anticipating this rejection behavior, offers amounts sufficient to satisfy the responder's fairness constraint, generating the observed modal offer near fifty percent.

Cross-cultural replication studies by Henrich and colleagues, spanning fifteen small-scale societies from forager groups to market-integrated agriculturalists, reveal substantial variation in modal offers and rejection rates. In some societies, modal offers approach seventy percent with frequent rejections of hyper-fair offers above fifty percent, while others exhibit offers near twenty-five percent with low rejection rates. This variation correlates with the degree of market integration and cooperation in daily economic life, suggesting that fairness norms are not universal cognitive constants but rather equilibrium outcomes of repeated interaction within specific institutional environments.

The Trust Game, introduced by Berg, Dickhaut, and McCabe, measures willingness to extend trust and reciprocate trustworthiness in a sequential investment structure. The sender receives an endowment $E$ and chooses an amount $t \in [0, E]$ to send to the receiver. The experimenter triples the sent amount, so the receiver obtains $3t$. The receiver then chooses a return amount $r \in [0, 3t]$ to send back to the sender. Final payoffs are $(E - t + r)$ for the sender and $(3t - r)$ for the receiver. Backward induction predicts $r^* = 0$, which the sender anticipates, leading to $t^* = 0$ and a collapse of exchange despite the tripling technology that creates substantial surplus.

Experimental senders transfer an average of fifty percent of their endowment, and receivers return approximately thirty percent of the tripled amount, yielding positive net gains for both parties. The return function $r(t)$ exhibits a positive slope: higher transfers elicit higher returns both in absolute terms and as a fraction of the received amount. This reciprocity pattern suggests that the receiver's utility function incorporates a preference for rewarding kind intentions, formalized in models of intention-based reciprocity where utility depends not only on material payoffs but also on beliefs about the sender's motive in choosing $t$.

The Trust Game's tripling technology creates joint surplus, distinguishing it from the Ultimatum Game's zero-sum division problem. This difference matters: the receiver's return decision balances self-interest against the desire to reward the sender's trusting act, and the efficiency gain from trade provides resources to satisfy both motives. When the multiplier is reduced from three to one point five, trust and reciprocity decline but do not vanish, indicating that social preferences interact with strategic surplus rather than operating independently of material incentives.

Coordination games with multiple equilibria expose systematic failures to achieve efficient outcomes even when players' interests are aligned. The Stag Hunt game, attributed to Rousseau's parable of collaborative hunting, features two Pareto-ranked Nash equilibria: mutual stag hunting yields the highest joint payoff but requires coordination, while hare hunting is a safe outside option that guarantees a positive payoff regardless of the partner's choice. Experimental subjects frequently fail to coordinate on the payoff-dominant equilibrium when the risk-dominant safe option is available, particularly when strategic uncertainty is high or communication is prohibited.

The minimum-effort coordination game generalizes this tension to $n$ players. Each player chooses an effort level $e_i \in \{1, 2, \ldots, 7\}$, and payoffs are determined by $\pi_i = a \cdot \min\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\} - c \cdot e_i$, where $a > c > 0$. The efficient equilibrium occurs at $e_i = 7$ for all players, but any profile where all players choose the same effort constitutes a Nash equilibrium. The payoff structure features strategic complementarity: increasing one's own effort is only beneficial if others also increase theirs, since the minimum determines the group outcome.

Experimental play in this game reveals rapid convergence to low-effort equilibria far below the Pareto optimum. Even when subjects begin with high effort levels, a single player choosing low effort drags the minimum down, and subsequent rounds cascade toward the lowest feasible effort. This coordination failure persists across repetitions and resists attempts at re-coordination through public announcements or one-shot bonuses. The phenomenon demonstrates equilibrium selection dynamics and the difficulty of escaping inefficient coordination traps once established.

Bounded rationality models formalize the cognitive constraints that generate systematic deviations from Nash equilibrium. The level-$k$ reasoning model posits a hierarchy of strategic types. Level-0 players employ a non-strategic anchor rule, typically uniform randomization across available actions. Level-1 players best-respond to the belief that all opponents are level-0. Level-2 players best-respond to the belief that all opponents are level-1, and so forth. The population distribution over types is estimated empirically, with typical findings placing the modal type at level-1 or level-2 and negligible mass above level-3.

This cognitive hierarchy generates sharp predictions in games where Nash equilibrium requires higher-order reasoning. In the Beauty Contest game, each player submits a number in $[0, 100]$, and the winner is the player whose submission is closest to two-thirds of the group average. The unique Nash equilibrium is zero, reached by iteratively eliminating dominated strategies. Level-0 players choose uniformly on $[0, 100]$ with mean fifty. Level-1 players compute two-thirds of fifty, yielding approximately thirty-three. Level-2 players compute two-thirds of thirty-three, yielding twenty-two, and the sequence continues. Experimental submissions cluster around these predicted landmarks, with modal entries near thirty to thirty-five in initial rounds and gradual convergence toward lower values with repetition but rarely reaching zero.

The Cognitive Hierarchy model refines level-$k$ reasoning by allowing each player to believe the population contains a distribution of lower types rather than assuming all opponents are exactly one level below. A level-$k$ player best-responds to a belief that opponents are distributed according to a Poisson distribution truncated at $k-1$. This specification smooths the sharp discontinuities of the level-$k$ model and provides better fit to data in games with continuous strategy spaces.

Quantal Response Equilibrium (QRE) offers an alternative bounded rationality framework rooted in probabilistic choice rather than cognitive depth. Players best-respond with error, choosing better strategies with higher probability but retaining positive probability on all actions. The choice probability for action $a_i$ given beliefs about opponents' strategies $\sigma_{-i}$ is:

$$P(a_i | \sigma_{-i}) = \frac{\exp(\lambda \cdot EU_i(a_i, \sigma_{-i}))}{\sum_{a_i'} \exp(\lambda \cdot EU_i(a_i', \sigma_{-i}))}$$

where $EU_i(a_i, \sigma_{-i})$ denotes the expected utility of action $a_i$ and $\lambda \geq 0$ parameterizes the precision of choice. As $\lambda \to \infty$, the model converges to Nash equilibrium, while $\lambda = 0$ yields uniform randomization. Equilibrium obtains when choice probabilities are mutually consistent, forming a fixed point of the quantal response mapping. QRE has successfully explained observed mixing rates in matrix games, overbidding in auctions, and entry patterns in market entry games.

Social preferences extend beyond inequality aversion to incorporate reciprocity, altruism, and conditional cooperation. The distinction between unconditional altruism and reciprocity-based kindness is critical: an altruist derives utility from others' payoffs regardless of their behavior, while a reciprocator conditions their other-regarding preferences on the intentions and actions of interaction partners. Dictator Game evidence provides a clean measure of unconditional altruism, as the dictator unilaterally allocates a pie with no strategic response. Typical allocations give twenty to thirty percent to the passive recipient, with heterogeneity ranging from pure selfishness to equal splits.

Reciprocity preferences, formalized in models by Rabin and Dufwenberg-Kirchsteiger, incorporate psychological game theory where utility depends on beliefs about others' intentions. A player derives positive utility from rewarding kind actions and negative utility from being exploited by unkind actions. This framework rationalizes punishment in the Public Goods game, rejection in the Ultimatum Game, and conditional cooperation patterns where players match the contribution levels of their group members.

The empirical strategy profile preference (SPP) approach, developed by Falk, Fehr, and Fischbacher, measures distributional and reciprocity preferences through a battery of simple games and estimates a structural model linking game behavior to underlying preference parameters. This methodology allows researchers to predict out-of-sample behavior in novel strategic environments using individual-level preference estimates, achieving cross-validation success rates substantially above chance.

Learning dynamics in repeated games deviate systematically from Bayesian belief updating. Reinforcement learning models, which increase the probability of actions that yielded higher payoffs in the past, outperform belief-based learning in fitting experimental time-series data. The Experience-Weighted Attraction (EWA) model by Camerer and Ho nests both reinforcement and belief-based learning as special cases, with a parameter $\phi$ governing the depreciation of past attractions and a parameter $\delta$ controlling the weight on foregone payoffs from unchosen actions. Estimation across hundreds of experimental games finds $\phi < 1$, indicating that recent experience is weighted more heavily than distant history, violating the stationarity assumption of Bayesian updating.

Fictitious play, where players best-respond to the empirical frequency of opponents' past actions, generates convergence to Nash equilibrium in certain classes of games, including zero-sum games and games with strategic complements. However, in games with strategic substitutes or multiple equilibria, fictitious play can cycle or fail to converge. Experimental evidence shows that subjects exhibit directional learning, adjusting toward better responses, but the adjustment is gradual and influenced by payoff salience rather than implementing instantaneous best responses.

The interaction between learning and equilibrium selection matters for long-run outcomes. In coordination games with multiple equilibria, initial play conditions subsequent learning trajectories. If early rounds happen to coordinate on a particular equilibrium, reinforcement learning locks in that outcome even if it is Pareto-dominated. This path dependence explains the persistence of inefficient coordination in minimum-effort games and the sensitivity of outcomes to seemingly irrelevant initial conditions such as the order of action labels or the frame in which payoffs are presented.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The experimental laboratory provides a controlled environment where the data-generating process of strategic behavior is observable with precision unavailable in field settings. The physical architecture of an experimental economics session begins with subject recruitment from a pre-registered pool, typically university students but increasingly diverse populations recruited through online platforms for external validity checks. Subjects receive written instructions explaining the game structure, payoff functions, information conditions, and payment protocols. The instructions avoid loaded terminology: a Public Goods game is described in neutral terms of "group accounts" and "individual accounts" rather than invoking cooperation or free-riding language that might prime social norms.

The software platform implements the game protocol, collects decisions, computes payoffs, and enforces information structures. Standard platforms include z-Tree for laboratory sessions, oTree for online experiments, and custom web interfaces built on JavaScript frameworks for complex dynamic games. The software must enforce simultaneity when required, ensuring that subjects cannot observe others' decisions before committing their own, and must implement sequential decision protocols with precise timing when studying dynamic games. Random matching protocols shuffle subjects across rounds to approximate one-shot interactions in repeated games, breaking reputation channels that would confound the measurement of social preferences.

Payoff implementation follows induced value theory: subjects earn experimental currency units (ECU) through their game decisions, and these ECU are converted to real money at a pre-announced exchange rate. Conversion rates are calibrated to generate meaningful stakes, typically producing hourly earnings above the prevailing wage for comparable non-experimental work. Showup fees are added to decision earnings to ensure a minimum payment floor, preventing negative selection where only risk-seeking or desperate subjects participate. All earnings are paid privately in cash or electronic transfer at the session's conclusion, preserving anonymity and preventing social comparison during the experiment.

The Public Goods game implementation requires careful specification of the marginal per capita return parameter $m$. Setting $m = 0.4$ in a four-player game yields a dominant strategy to free-ride, since each dollar contributed returns forty cents to the contributor but generates $0.4 \times 4 = 1.6$ dollars of total surplus, creating a social dilemma with a Pareto gain of $0.6$ dollars per contributed dollar. The experimenter initializes each subject with an endowment of twenty ECU, displays a contribution screen with a slider or numeric entry field, and collects decisions simultaneously across all group members.

After contribution decisions are submitted, the software computes the sum of contributions, multiplies by the MPCR parameter, divides equally among group members, and displays individual payoffs with a breakdown showing own contribution, group contribution, and resulting earnings. This transparency allows subjects to infer others' average contribution levels, which is essential for conditional cooperation dynamics to operate. In treatment variations with detailed feedback, the software displays each group member's individual contribution, enabling targeted punishment or reputation formation in repeated interaction.

The punishment stage, when implemented, follows the contribution stage. Each subject views the contribution profile of their group and allocates punishment points using a matrix interface where rows represent punishers and columns represent targets. The cost function $c(p)$ is typically linear, $c(p) = p$, but quadratic cost functions $c(p) = p^2 / 2$ are also employed to study how marginal punishment costs affect targeting decisions. The punishment technology parameter $k$ determines the effectiveness of punishment, with $k = 3$ being a common calibration where one punishment point costs the punisher one ECU and reduces the target's payoff by three ECU.

Anonymity protocols are critical for isolating social preference channels from reputation and future interaction incentives. Single-blind anonymity ensures that the experimenter cannot link decisions to individual subjects, while double-blind anonymity prevents even the experimenter from observing the mapping between subjects and choices. Laboratory sessions implement double-blind anonymity through numbered envelopes or private computer terminals with visual barriers, while online experiments achieve anonymity naturally through the lack of face-to-face interaction.

The Ultimatum Game protocol assigns subjects to proposer and responder roles, either randomly or through an initial quiz or task to avoid selection effects. The proposer views an endowment (e.g., ten dollars) and submits an offer amount $x \in [0, 10]$ using a slider or dropdown menu. The software transmits the offer to the matched responder, who views the offer and clicks "Accept" or "Reject." If accepted, payoffs $(10 - x, x)$ are implemented; if rejected, both players receive zero. The strategy method, an alternative protocol, elicits the responder's acceptance decision for all possible offers before the proposer moves, allowing the researcher to observe the full acceptance function rather than only the on-path decision.

The Trust Game implementation follows a sequential structure where timing and information revelation are carefully controlled. The sender observes their endowment and submits a transfer amount $t$. The software multiplies $t$ by three and displays the tripled amount $3t$ to the receiver, who then submits a return amount $r \in [0, 3t]$. The sender observes the final payoffs $(E - t + r)$ and learns the receiver's return decision. Variants manipulate information: in some treatments, the receiver is not informed of the multiplication factor, testing whether reciprocity depends on understanding the sender's sacrifice, while in others, the sender observes the receiver's decision for all possible transfer amounts using the strategy method.

Coordination games require precise specification of payoff matrices and action labels. The minimum-effort game displays seven effort levels labeled numerically or with neutral identifiers, and subjects submit their chosen effort simultaneously. After all decisions are collected, the software computes the minimum effort across the group and applies the payoff formula $\pi_i = a \cdot \min\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\} - c \cdot e_i$ with parameters such as $a = 0.2$ and $c = 0.1$. The feedback screen displays the minimum effort, the subject's own effort, and resulting payoff, but does not reveal the full distribution of efforts unless the treatment explicitly manipulates transparency.

The level-$k$ reasoning framework is elicited through normal-form games designed to separate the predictions of different cognitive levels. The Beauty Contest game interface presents a numeric entry field for choosing a number in $[0, 100]$ and explains that the winner is determined by proximity to two-thirds of the group average. After all submissions are collected, the software computes the average, multiplies by $2/3$, identifies the closest submission, and pays the winner a fixed prize or a prize proportional to their accuracy. Multi-round repetitions allow the researcher to observe learning dynamics and test whether subjects iterate toward equilibrium or plateau at intermediate levels of reasoning.

Quantal Response Equilibrium estimation requires game-level data where multiple subjects play the same game, generating a frequency distribution over action profiles. The econometrician specifies a parametric functional form for the quantal response function, typically the logit specification, and estimates the precision parameter $\lambda$ using maximum likelihood. The likelihood function aggregates over observed action profiles, weighting each profile by its probability under the QRE fixed point. Numerical algorithms solve for the QRE fixed point at each candidate parameter value during the optimization routine, a computationally intensive procedure that requires careful initialization and convergence checks.

Social preference estimation through the SPP approach collects data from a battery of games including Dictator Games, Ultimatum Games, Public Goods games with and without punishment, and Trust Games. Each subject plays multiple games, generating a panel dataset of decisions. The researcher specifies a utility function incorporating distributional preference parameters (inequality aversion $\alpha$, $\beta$) and reciprocity parameters (kindness sensitivity), then estimates these parameters for each subject using maximum likelihood. The estimated parameters predict behavior in holdout games not used for estimation, providing an out-of-sample validation test of the preference model.

Learning model estimation uses time-series data from repeated games where the same subjects play multiple rounds. The Experience-Weighted Attraction model tracks a latent attraction value $A_i^t(a)$ for each action $a$ available to player $i$ in round $t$. The attraction evolves according to:

$$A_i^{t+1}(a) = \phi \cdot A_i^t(a) + \begin{cases} \pi_i^t(a) & \text{if } a \text{ was chosen} \\ \delta \cdot \pi_i^t(a) & \text{if } a \text{ was not chosen} \end{cases}$$

where $\phi \in [0, 1]$ is the depreciation rate, $\delta \in [0, 1]$ is the imagination weight on foregone payoffs, and $\pi_i^t(a)$ is the payoff that action $a$ would have yielded in round $t$. The choice probability in round $t+1$ is a logit function of attractions:

$$P_i^{t+1}(a) = \frac{\exp(\lambda \cdot A_i^{t+1}(a))}{\sum_{a'} \exp(\lambda \cdot A_i^{t+1}(a'))}$$

The parameters $(\phi, \delta, \lambda)$ are estimated via maximum likelihood over the observed sequence of choices, and model fit is evaluated using log-likelihood comparisons against nested alternatives such as pure reinforcement ($\delta = 0$) or Bayesian belief-based learning.

The graphical representation of game structures employs extensive-form trees for sequential games and normal-form matrices for simultaneous games. The Trust Game extensive form is drawn as:

```
Sender
  |
  ├─ Send t
  |    |
  |    Receiver
  |      |
  |      ├─ Return r
  |      |    (E - t + r, 3t - r)
  |      └─ Return 0
  |           (E - t, 3t)
  └─ Send 0
       (E, 0)
```

The Ultimatum Game tree is:

```
Proposer
  |
  ├─ Offer x
  |    |
  |    Responder
  |      |
  |      ├─ Accept
  |      |    (S - x, x)
  |      └─ Reject
  |           (0, 0)
```

Normal-form matrix games are represented as payoff tables. The Prisoner's Dilemma appears as:

|          | Cooperate | Defect |
|----------|-----------|--------|
| Cooperate| (3, 3)    | (0, 5) |
| Defect   | (5, 0)    | (1, 1) |

The Stag Hunt coordination game is:

|      | Stag  | Hare  |
|------|-------|-------|
| Stag | (4, 4)| (0, 3)|
| Hare | (3, 0)| (3, 3)|

The payoff-dominant equilibrium is (Stag, Stag) with payoff $(4, 4)$, while the risk-dominant equilibrium is (Hare, Hare) with payoff $(3, 3)$. Risk dominance is computed by comparing the product of deviation losses: for Stag, the loss from deviating is $4 - 0 = 4$ when the opponent plays Stag and $0 - 0 = 0$ when the opponent plays Hare, yielding a product of zero. For Hare, the loss from deviating is $3 - 3 = 0$ when the opponent plays Stag and $3 - 0 = 3$ when the opponent plays Hare, yielding a product of zero. The risk-dominant equilibrium is the one with the larger product of deviation losses, which in this case is Hare due to the positive payoff in the off-diagonal.

The implementation of framing treatments tests the robustness of strategic behavior to superficial changes in presentation. In a Public Goods game, the "give" frame asks subjects to contribute to a group account from their endowment, while the "take" frame endows the group account initially and asks subjects to withdraw amounts for private use. The strategic structure is identical, but contributions are significantly higher in the give frame than in the take frame, revealing that subjects do not treat economically equivalent frames as strategically equivalent.

The role of communication is tested through cheap talk protocols where subjects send costless messages before making binding decisions. In coordination games, pre-play communication dramatically increases coordination on efficient equilibria. In the minimum-effort game, allowing subjects to announce intended efforts before simultaneous choice raises the minimum effort by two to three levels on average. In the Public Goods game, communication increases contributions but less dramatically, as subjects face incentives to promise high contributions then free-ride on the contributions of others who believed their promises. The effectiveness of communication depends on the alignment of interests: communication achieves near-perfect coordination when interests are aligned, but has limited impact when conflict is severe.

The experimenter's control over information structures allows sharp tests of asymmetric information theories. In Trust Games with incomplete information, the sender may not know the receiver's type (selfish or reciprocal), forcing the sender to form beliefs over types and choose transfers that maximize expected utility given those beliefs. The receiver's return decision reveals their type, and Bayesian updating predicts that senders should learn to discriminate between types over repeated interactions. Experimental evidence shows that senders do learn to trust more in populations with higher reciprocity rates, but learning is slower and noisier than Bayesian benchmarks predict, consistent with reinforcement learning models.

The cross-cultural experimental program extends laboratory findings to diverse populations to test the external validity and universality of behavioral patterns. The Henrich team conducted standardized Ultimatum Game, Dictator Game, and Public Goods game experiments across fifteen small-scale societies, including the Hadza hunter-gatherers of Tanzania, the Tsimane horticulturalists of Bolivia, and the Orma pastoralists of Kenya. Modal offers in the Ultimatum Game varied from twenty-five percent among the Machiguenga to fifty-seven percent among the Lamalera whale hunters, and rejection rates ranged from zero among some groups to substantial frequencies among others. This variation correlates with measures of market integration, payoffs to cooperation in daily life, and the importance of reputation in small-scale communities.

The interpretation of cross-cultural variation divides researchers. One view holds that social preferences are universal cognitive adaptations shaped by evolutionary pressures, with cultural variation reflecting different equilibria or different salience of underlying preferences. An alternative view posits that preferences themselves are culturally transmitted and shaped by the institutions and economic structures of each society. Distinguishing these hypotheses requires experimental designs that manipulate institutional features within a single cultural group, testing whether behavior adjusts to changing incentives while holding culture constant.

The neural and physiological correlates of strategic decision-making provide converging evidence on the mechanisms underlying social preferences and bounded rationality. Functional MRI studies during Trust Game and Ultimatum Game play reveal activation in brain regions associated with reward processing (ventral striatum), emotional regulation (anterior cingulate cortex), and theory of mind (temporo-parietal junction). Costly punishment of unfair Ultimatum Game offers activates the dorsal striatum, suggesting that punishment delivers a hedonic reward independent of material payoffs. Responders with greater activation in the anterior insula, a region associated with negative emotional states like disgust, are more likely to reject low offers.

The integration of neuroscience and experimental economics opens new methodological pathways but also raises interpretive challenges. Brain activation patterns can inform theoretical models by identifying which psychological processes are engaged during strategic choice, but neural data do not directly reveal preference parameters or cognitive depth. A subject exhibiting high anterior insula activation may reject an unfair offer due to inequality aversion, anger at the proposer's intention, or a desire to enforce social norms, and distinguishing these motives requires additional experimental manipulations beyond neuroimaging alone.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

The design of high-powered experiments that generate precise parameter estimates and robust treatment effects requires careful attention to statistical power, sample size determination, and within-subject versus between-subject trade-offs. Power analysis for experimental games begins with specifying the effect size the researcher aims to detect. In Public Goods experiments testing the impact of punishment institutions, treatment effects on average contributions are typically large, often exceeding one standard deviation, allowing adequate power with group sizes of twenty to thirty subjects per treatment. In contrast, testing level-$k$ reasoning models or estimating continuous social preference parameters requires larger samples, often one hundred or more subjects, to achieve narrow confidence intervals on structural parameters.

Within-subject designs, where each subject participates in multiple treatments, increase statistical power by controlling for individual heterogeneity. A subject who is a strong conditional cooperator in one Public Goods treatment will likely remain so in a variant treatment, allowing the researcher to difference out individual fixed effects and isolate the treatment effect with greater precision. However, within-subject designs introduce order effects and learning confounds. If subjects first play a Public Goods game with punishment and then play without punishment, their behavior in the second treatment may be influenced by the experience of punishment possibility in the first. Counterbalancing the order of treatments across subjects mitigates but does not eliminate this concern, and researchers must test for order effects by comparing behavior in the first treatment across counterbalancing arms.

Between-subject designs assign each subject to a single treatment, avoiding order effects at the cost of larger sample size requirements. When the treatment manipulation is subtle (e.g., testing whether a five percent change in the MPCR parameter affects contributions), between-subject comparisons may lack power to detect the effect due to high cross-subject variance. Mixed designs, combining within-subject manipulations for primary contrasts and between-subject assignment for order-sensitive treatments, balance statistical efficiency against internal validity threats.

The choice of statistical test for hypothesis testing in experimental games depends on the level of aggregation and the nature of the dependent variable. Contribution decisions in Public Goods games are continuous variables bounded by the endowment, appropriately analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts for subjects to account for repeated measures. Rejection decisions in Ultimatum Games are binary, requiring logistic regression or probit models. When testing equilibrium predictions, the researcher specifies the null hypothesis as the Nash equilibrium action and employs one-sample tests against that benchmark. When comparing treatments, independent samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests are standard, with non-parametric tests preferred when distributions exhibit heavy tails or outliers.

Clustering standard errors at the session or group level corrects for within-cluster correlation arising from common shocks or peer effects. In Public Goods experiments where subjects interact in fixed groups for multiple rounds, observations within a group are not independent, violating the assumption of ordinary least squares regression. Clustered standard errors or mixed-effects models with random effects for groups yield valid inference in this setting. Failure to account for clustering leads to underestimated standard errors and inflated significance levels, a widespread problem in early experimental economics literature that has been corrected in recent practice.

The replication crisis in psychology and behavioral science has prompted experimental economics to adopt stricter standards for pre-registration, replication, and transparency. Pre-registration involves submitting the experimental protocol, hypotheses, and analysis plan to a registry before data collection begins, preventing researchers from selectively reporting results or adjusting hypotheses post-hoc. Replication studies, both direct replications of published findings and conceptual replications testing the same theory with different implementations, are increasingly valued by journals and funding agencies. The meta-analytic synthesis of replications across labs and populations provides cumulative evidence on the robustness and external validity of behavioral patterns.

The external validity of laboratory experiments, a perennial concern, asks whether behavior in the artificial laboratory environment predicts behavior in natural settings. Critics argue that the stakes are too low, the subject pool is unrepresentative, the environment is too controlled, or the framing is too abstract for laboratory findings to generalize. Proponents counter that the laboratory isolates causal mechanisms that operate in field settings but are confounded by unobserved variables, and that internal validity is a prerequisite for external validity since a finding that is not replicable under controlled conditions cannot be reliably expected to appear in uncontrolled settings.

Empirical tests of external validity compare laboratory behavior with field behavior in parallel settings. Carpenter and Seki (2011) find that Public Goods game contributions in the laboratory correlate positively with real-world cooperation in community forestry management among Vietnamese villagers. Fehr and List (2004) show that Trust Game behavior among CEOs resembles undergraduate behavior in structure but exhibits higher trust and reciprocity levels, consistent with selection of cooperative types into leadership roles. Levitt and List (2007) argue that laboratory environments differ from field settings along dimensions of scrutiny, context, and stakes, and that these differences can generate behavior in the lab that does not generalize. The resolution of this debate requires field experiments that randomize treatments in natural settings and compare outcomes with laboratory experiments using the same strategic structure.

The use of deception in experimental economics is prohibited by long-standing professional norms, contrasting sharply with psychology where deception is common. The no-deception norm rests on the argument that if subjects come to expect deception, they will treat experimenter instructions and payoff claims with skepticism, contaminating the induced value theory foundation that allows experimental control over preferences. However, one-time deception by an individual experimenter imposes a negative externality on the entire experimental community by eroding subject pool trust. This collective action problem is solved through a professional consensus enforced by journal editorial policies and institutional review board protocols that prohibit deception in economics experiments.

The ethical treatment of experimental subjects extends beyond deception to informed consent, privacy, and fair compensation. Informed consent procedures explain the structure of the experiment without revealing hypotheses or strategic details that would confound behavior. Subjects are told they will play games for money, that their decisions affect their earnings, and that anonymity will be preserved, but they are not told that the experiment tests social preferences or that the researcher expects certain equilibrium predictions. Privacy protections ensure that decision data cannot be linked to identifiable individuals outside the research team, and compensation is set to exceed the opportunity cost of participation, typically achieving hourly earnings above minimum wage.

The statistical power to detect heterogeneous treatment effects requires even larger samples than detecting average treatment effects. If the researcher hypothesizes that punishment institutions have larger effects on conditional cooperators than on free-riders, testing this interaction requires sufficient numbers of both types. Pre-experimental surveys or initial game behavior can classify subjects into types, but if one type is rare (e.g., only twenty percent of subjects are strong free-riders), the subgroup analysis will be underpowered unless the total sample is scaled up accordingly. Adaptive designs that oversample rare types or employ Bayesian sequential sampling can improve efficiency.

The econometric challenge of multiple hypothesis testing arises when researchers test many treatment comparisons or subgroup effects within a single experiment. The probability of falsely rejecting at least one null hypothesis increases with the number of tests, inflating the Type I error rate above the nominal significance level. Bonferroni correction, which divides the significance threshold by the number of tests, controls the family-wise error rate but is conservative and reduces power. False discovery rate (FDR) control methods, such as the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, offer a less conservative alternative by controlling the expected proportion of false rejections among all rejections. Researchers should pre-specify which comparisons are primary hypotheses and which are exploratory, applying correction procedures to the set of primary tests.

The identification of causal effects in experimental games with feedback and learning requires careful attention to dynamic confounding. In repeated Public Goods games, a subject's contribution in round $t$ depends on their observation of others' contributions in round $t-1$, creating a feedback loop. If the experimenter introduces a treatment (e.g., punishment) in the middle of the repeated sequence, the treatment effect is confounded with the history-dependent path of play. Clean identification requires between-subject designs where one group plays all rounds with punishment and another plays all rounds without, or random assignment of treatments at the session level rather than the round level.

The interpretation of null results poses particular challenges in experimental economics. Failure to reject the null hypothesis of no treatment effect can arise from low power, noisy data, or a genuinely absent effect. Bayesian methods allow the researcher to compute posterior probabilities over effect sizes given the data, distinguishing "the data are uninformative" from "the data provide evidence for no effect." Equivalence testing, which reverses the null and alternative hypotheses to test whether the effect lies within a region of practical equivalence around zero, provides another framework for interpreting null results.

The stability of social preference estimates across time and context is tested through panel designs where subjects return for multiple sessions spaced weeks or months apart. If social preference parameters are stable traits, estimates from an initial session should predict behavior in subsequent sessions after controlling for learning. Empirical studies find moderate stability: test-retest correlations for inequality aversion parameters are typically in the range of 0.3 to 0.5, indicating that preferences have some stable component but are also influenced by transient factors such as mood, recent experiences, or experimental conditions.

The integration of experimental and structural methods combines the internal validity of experiments with the external applicability of estimated models. A researcher conducts laboratory experiments to estimate preference parameters, then embeds those parameters in a structural model of a field setting such as labor market bargaining or firm entry. The model predicts field outcomes, which are tested against observational data. Discrepancies between model predictions and field data can arise from omitted variables, misspecified preferences, or violations of the common knowledge and rationality assumptions underlying the model. Iterating between experimental refinement and structural estimation narrows the gap and improves predictive accuracy.

The future trajectory of experimental economics points toward tighter integration with neuroscience, genetics, and field experiments. Genetic studies test whether variation in social preferences correlates with polymorphisms in genes regulating oxytocin, serotonin, or dopamine pathways, though results have been mixed and replication concerns remain acute. Field experiments embedded in developing country contexts test whether interventions designed using laboratory-estimated preference parameters achieve predicted effects on cooperation, public goods provision, or market outcomes. The convergence of laboratory control, structural estimation, and field validation offers the prospect of a cumulative empirical science of strategic behavior grounded in causal identification and predictive accuracy.
