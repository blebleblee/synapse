# Lesson 4: Market Institutions

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The market institution constitutes the fundamental substrate upon which economic exchange occurs, yet orthodox economic theory often treats the institution itself as a black box, assuming that competitive equilibrium emerges spontaneously from the rationality of agents and the presence of supply and demand schedules. Experimental economics shatters this assumption by demonstrating empirically that the structure of the trading mechanism—the rules governing bid submission, order matching, information revelation, and transaction execution—profoundly shapes convergence speed, allocative efficiency, price discovery quality, and vulnerability to systematic departures from competitive predictions.

Vernon Smith's seminal double auction experiments in the 1960s established that even with small numbers of human traders possessing induced private values, continuous bilateral trading mechanisms converge rapidly toward competitive equilibrium predictions, often within three or four trading periods. This result astonished theorists who had assumed that textbook market-clearing required infinite agents, complete information, and instantaneous recontracting. The double auction's institutional architecture—simultaneous bid and ask queues, transparent order books, immediate execution upon crossing spreads—creates dynamic incentives for traders to reveal their reservation values incrementally, driving transaction prices toward the intersection of aggregate supply and demand curves even when no individual agent possesses knowledge of these market-level schedules.

The invariant principle governing experimental market design hinges on understanding how information structures, strategic timing, and matching protocols interact to produce allocative outcomes. A sealed-bid first-price auction generates fundamentally different bidding behavior than an ascending English auction, even when valuations remain identical, because the strategic shading required to avoid winner's curse differs across formats. A posted-offer institution, where sellers publicly commit to take-it-or-leave-it prices before buyers arrive, systematically produces higher markups and lower consumer surplus than continuous double auctions with the same trader endowments, because the sequential commitment structure grants sellers first-mover advantage and reduces competitive pressure.

Market power experiments operationalize textbook monopoly and oligopoly theory by endowing specific traders with cost or capacity advantages, then measuring whether observed pricing and quantity choices correspond to Nash-Cournot predictions, Bertrand competitive outcomes, or tacit collusion equilibria. Empirical results consistently reveal that oligopolistic markets with symmetric capacity constraints and repeated interaction exhibit substantial coordination toward joint profit maximization, even without explicit communication, while introducing asymmetry in marginal costs or allowing free entry rapidly erodes supracompetitive pricing. These findings carry direct policy relevance for antitrust merger analysis, spectrum auction design, and electricity market restructuring, where institutional details determine whether theoretical market power translates into actual consumer harm.

Asset market experiments confront perhaps the most vexing puzzle in experimental economics: the persistent emergence of price bubbles and crashes in laboratory markets with transparent fundamental value structures. In Smith, Suchanek, and Williams' canonical design, traders exchange an asset paying a known constant dividend each period with a monotonically declining expected resale value as the finite horizon approaches. Rational expectations equilibrium predicts a flat price trajectory equal to discounted remaining dividends. Instead, laboratory markets reliably generate boom-bust cycles with prices rising far above fundamental value before collapsing in the final periods. This pattern persists across variations in trader experience, dividend structure, and cash-to-asset ratios, suggesting that bubble formation stems not from confused subjects but from the strategic dynamics of heterogeneous beliefs, limited arbitrage capital, and momentum trading incentives endemic to finite-horizon speculation.

The mechanism design revolution transformed economics from a descriptive science analyzing existing institutions into an engineering discipline capable of constructing novel allocation rules optimized for specific objectives. Experimental testing of auction mechanisms—Vickrey second-price, English ascending, Dutch descending, sealed-bid first-price, and their multi-unit generalizations—maps the theoretical landscape of incentive compatibility, revenue equivalence, and efficiency under private values versus common values. Laboratory evidence confirms that Vickrey mechanisms elicit truthful bidding under private values as theory predicts, yet common-value environments with affiliated signals exhibit systematic winner's curse deviations that leave money on the table for auctioneers. These findings guided the FCC's design of simultaneous ascending auctions for spectrum allocation, incorporating activity rules and package bidding to mitigate exposure problems in complementary goods environments.

Matching market experiments extend mechanism design into settings where money cannot or should not determine allocation: school choice assignment, kidney exchange networks, medical residency matching, and two-sided platform clearinghouses. The Gale-Shapley deferred acceptance algorithm produces stable matchings that eliminate blocking pairs, but laboratory tests reveal systematic differences in outcomes when proposers versus receivers enjoy strategic advantage in the protocol sequence. Strategy-proofness emerges as a critical design goal, since even theoretically dominant truth-telling equilibria fail empirically when subjects perceive gains from manipulation or face computational complexity in reasoning through contingent rankings.

Combinatorial complexity pervades institutional design when goods exhibit complementarities or substitutabilities requiring traders to condition bids on package combinations. The combinatorial auction challenge demands mechanisms that allow expressive bidding over exponentially large item bundles while maintaining computational tractability in winner determination. Laboratory experiments with simplified combinatorial structures demonstrate that simultaneous ascending auctions on individual items suffer from exposure problems—traders risk winning subsets of desired packages at prices making the partial bundle unprofitable—while sealed-bid Vickrey-Clarke-Groves mechanisms, though theoretically incentive compatible, impose cognitive burdens leading to frequent bidding errors and budget imbalances. Iterative combinatorial auction formats with bidding languages and preference elicitation protocols represent practical compromises between expressiveness and tractability.

Information aggregation constitutes the highest-order function of market institutions, transforming dispersed private signals held by individual traders into public prices encoding collective forecasts. Prediction markets operationalize Hayek's conception of prices as sufficient statistics aggregating distributed knowledge, creating tradable securities whose payoffs depend on observable future events—election outcomes, project completion dates, macroeconomic releases, disease outbreak trajectories. Experimental prediction markets consistently outperform expert polls and statistical models in forecasting accuracy when traders possess genuine information diversity and face real monetary stakes. The aggregation mechanism relies on the market scoring rule protocol, where the marginal cost of moving probability estimates increases linearly with distance from current consensus, creating incentives for informed traders to reveal signals incrementally while penalizing manipulation attempts.

The theoretical foundation for information aggregation rests on rational expectations equilibrium, where prices fully reveal aggregate information in the limit of large markets. Experimental tests using explicit signal structures—common-value assets with noisy private signals—demonstrate partial aggregation that improves with market size but exhibits systematic biases from herding, failure to account for the informational content of price changes, and overweighting of public information relative to private signals. These deviations motivate bounded rationality models incorporating level-$k$ reasoning, where traders best-respond to forecasts of opponents' strategies truncated at finite depths rather than fixed-point equilibria.

The invariant connecting all experimental market institutions involves recognizing that trading rules function as computational algorithms solving distributed optimization problems under asymmetric information and strategic behavior. The double auction implements a greedy local search procedure, matching highest bids with lowest asks iteratively. Matching algorithms execute constraint satisfaction over stability conditions. Prediction markets perform Bayesian updating aggregated through betting flows. Understanding markets as algorithms enables principled institutional engineering: specifying the objective function—allocative efficiency, revenue maximization, stability, forecast accuracy—then designing trading protocols whose equilibrium or tatonnement dynamics optimize that criterion under realistic behavioral and computational constraints.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The double auction mechanism forms the canonical baseline for experimental market comparison because its institutional structure most closely approximates decentralized continuous trading in organized exchanges. The architecture consists of separate bid and ask order books maintained by a central auctioneer or automated matching engine, with buyers submitting demand schedules represented as price-quantity pairs and sellers submitting supply schedules symmetrically. At each moment, the highest outstanding bid and lowest outstanding ask define the bid-ask spread, the market's instantaneous measure of liquidity and transaction cost. When any buyer raises their bid to meet or exceed the standing ask, or any seller lowers their ask to meet or fall below the standing bid, the orders execute immediately at the crossing price, removing matched volume from the order books and establishing a transaction record.

The information structure of the double auction critically determines its convergence properties. Under the transparent order book protocol, all traders observe the complete bid and ask queues, the full history of transaction prices and quantities, and the evolution of spreads over time. This public information enables sophisticated traders to infer bounds on the distribution of private values, accelerating convergence by revealing aggregate market conditions through price signals. Alternative opacity designs—last-price transparency, where only executed transaction prices appear public while the order book remains hidden, or complete darkness, where traders observe no market information beyond their own order confirmations—slow convergence and widen spreads, as traders must explore price space through costly failed orders rather than observing revealed preferences directly.

The call market variant replaces continuous trading with discrete clearing at scheduled intervals, accumulating all submitted orders during a batching window then executing a market-clearing price that maximizes traded volume. The call auction computes the price $p^*$ solving

$$
\max_{p} \min\left(\sum_{i: b_i \geq p} q_i^{\text{bid}}, \sum_{j: a_j \leq p} q_j^{\text{ask}}\right)
$$

where $b_i$ denotes buyer $i$'s limit price and $a_j$ denotes seller $j$'s limit price. This batch clearing mechanism eliminates high-frequency trading advantages and front-running possibilities inherent in continuous protocols, but sacrifices the rapid information revelation that sequential trading provides. Experimental comparisons demonstrate that call markets achieve higher allocative efficiency in thin markets with few traders, where continuous trading generates wide spreads and failed bargaining opportunities, while double auctions dominate in thick markets where continuous price discovery outweighs batching delays.

The posted-offer institution inverts the double auction's symmetry by assigning sellers the exclusive right to set public prices, with buyers arriving sequentially to accept or reject posted terms. This mechanism models retail environments where price-setting costs and strategic commitment create natural first-mover advantages for suppliers. The experimental protocol specifies a two-stage game: sellers simultaneously post prices in stage one, then buyers observe all posted offers and select purchases in stage two with ties broken randomly. Posted-offer markets systematically produce higher prices than double auctions with identical trader endowments because sellers can coordinate on focal equilibria without explicit communication, exploiting buyers' lack of bargaining power and the absence of competitive undercutting pressure during the commitment phase.

Market power experiments operationalize strategic behavior by constructing asymmetric cost structures or capacity constraints that endow specific traders with pivotal positions. In a four-seller oligopoly experiment, suppose three sellers face constant marginal cost $c_1 = 2$ and unit capacity while the fourth seller possesses cost $c_4 = 1$ and capacity for three units, facing inelastic demand $P(Q) = 10 - Q$. The competitive equilibrium predicts price $p^{\text{CE}} = 2$ with quantity $Q^{\text{CE}} = 8$, while Nash-Cournot oligopoly equilibrium yields

$$
q_i = \frac{a - c_i - \sum_{j \neq i} q_j}{b}
$$

where $a = 10$ and $b = 1$ represent demand intercept and slope. Solving the symmetric capacity-constrained Cournot system produces predicted price $p^{\text{Cournot}} \approx 4.5$. Experimental implementations track whether observed transaction prices approach competitive, Cournot, or collusive joint monopoly predictions as a function of repetition, communication opportunities, and market transparency.

The tracking protocol requires careful induced-value assignment to separate strategic behavior from demand elasticity effects. Each seller receives a marginal cost schedule specifying redemption values for successive units, while buyers receive induced valuations representing willingness-to-pay. Laboratory currency typically denominates costs and values in experimental dollars convertible to real currency at a known exchange rate, ensuring sufficient monetary stakes to motivate strategic optimization. The experimenter records transaction prices, quantities, and timing for each market period, computing empirical surplus distributions across buyers, sellers, and total allocative efficiency relative to competitive benchmark:

$$
\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{Realized Total Surplus}}{\text{Maximum Possible Surplus}} = \frac{\sum_{\text{trades}} (v_i - c_j)}{\sum_{\text{optimal}} (v_i - c_j)}
$$

where $v_i$ denotes buyer valuations and $c_j$ denotes seller costs, with the optimal allocation pairing highest-value buyers with lowest-cost sellers.

Asset market bubble experiments implement finite-horizon trading of a dividend-paying security with known fundamental value declining deterministically toward terminal worthlessness. The canonical Smith-Suchanek-Williams design specifies an asset paying dividend $d \in \{0, 8, 28, 60\}$ cents with equal probability each period, expected value $\mathbb{E}[d] = 24$ cents, over a finite horizon of 15 periods. The fundamental value at period $t$ equals

$$
FV_t = (16 - t) \times 0.24
$$

declining linearly from $FV_1 = 3.60$ to $FV_{15} = 0.24$. Rational expectations equilibrium predicts flat trading prices equal to fundamental value throughout, since all traders observe the dividend structure and horizon publicly.

The experimental protocol endows traders with heterogeneous cash and asset portfolios, then allows continuous double-auction trading each period followed by dividend realization and payout. The institution records transaction prices, bid-ask spreads, trading volume, and portfolio holdings across periods. Bubble formation manifests as sustained price elevation above fundamental value during early periods, typically peaking around period 8-10 at 200-300% of fundamental value, followed by sharp crashes converging toward fundamentals as the terminal period approaches. The bubble-crash pattern emerges robustly across diverse subject pools, though experienced traders who have previously observed crashes exhibit attenuated but not eliminated bubble formation.

The mechanism generating bubbles involves heterogeneous beliefs about resale values, limited arbitrage capital constraining rational traders from fully exploiting mispricing, and momentum effects where rising prices attract extrapolative buyers expecting trends to continue. The computational model underlying observed behavior combines level-$k$ reasoning—where level-0 types believe prices equal fundamentals, level-1 types best-respond to level-0, and higher levels iterate—with evolutionary learning dynamics where successful strategies propagate through imitation or reinforcement. Neither pure rational expectations nor pure bounded rationality alone explains empirical patterns; the interaction between sophisticated arbitrageurs and naive trend-followers generates the characteristic boom-bust cycle.

Auction mechanism experiments isolate strategic bidding behavior by comparing alternative formats holding valuations constant. The four canonical single-unit auction mechanisms partition along two dimensions: sealed versus open bidding, and first-price versus second-price payment rules. The experimental protocol assigns each bidder a private valuation $v_i$ drawn independently from a known distribution, typically uniform on $[0, 100]$, then conducts the auction mechanism and records submitted bids, winner identity, and final payment.

The Vickrey second-price sealed-bid auction instructs bidders to submit sealed bids simultaneously, awards the item to the highest bidder, but charges the second-highest bid. The mechanism implements a dominant strategy equilibrium where truthful bidding $b_i = v_i$ constitutes each bidder's optimal action regardless of opponents' strategies, because raising bids above valuation risks paying more than value when winning, while lowering bids below valuation sacrifices probability of profitable wins without affecting payment. Experimental tests confirm high truth-telling rates around 85-90% under private values, with deviations attributable to confusion rather than strategic reasoning.

The first-price sealed-bid auction charges winners their own bid, creating strategic shading incentives. Symmetric Bayesian-Nash equilibrium with $n$ bidders and valuations uniform on $[0, \bar{v}]$ predicts

$$
b^*(v) = \frac{n-1}{n} v
$$

implying optimal bids shade linearly below valuation, with shading intensity decreasing in competitor count. Laboratory evidence reveals systematic overbidding relative to theoretical predictions, typically $b_{\text{obs}}(v) \approx 0.7v$ when theory predicts $b^*(v) = 0.5v$ with two bidders. This overbidding pattern reflects risk aversion—concave utility functions over monetary payoffs increase willingness to pay for win probability—and anticipated regret from narrowly losing auctions, emotions absent from expected-utility maximization models.

The English ascending-price auction implements an open outcry protocol where the auctioneer raises price continuously from zero, bidders indicate willingness to remain active at current prices, and the auction concludes when only one active bidder remains at price $p^*$, who wins and pays $p^*$. The mechanism theoretically equivalents the Vickrey auction under private values, since optimal strategy dictates remaining active until price reaches valuation then dropping out, yielding winner payment equal to second-highest valuation. Experimental implementations confirm near-theoretical equivalence in allocative efficiency and revenue, though dynamic information revelation in ascending auctions sometimes generates herding behavior absent in sealed formats.

Multi-unit auction extensions confront exposure problems and complementarity complexities. The simultaneous ascending auction for $m$ heterogeneous items runs parallel English auctions with activity rules requiring bidders to maintain active bidding on sufficient quantities to justify final package demands. The exposure problem arises when a bidder desires package $\{A, B\}$ with synergistic value $v(A,B) = 100$ exceeding sum-of-parts $v(A) + v(B) = 60 + 50 = 110$, but faces risk of winning only item $A$ at price 55, generating loss when $A$ alone yields value 60. The experimenter tracks bidder withdrawals, efficiency losses from unallocated items, and revenue shortfalls relative to Vickrey-Clarke-Groves benchmarks.

Matching market experiments implement stable allocation algorithms for assignment problems where monetary transfers are prohibited or constrained. The Gale-Shapley deferred acceptance mechanism for two-sided matching—schools and students, hospitals and residents, workers and firms—operates through iterative proposal and rejection rounds. In the student-proposing version, each round $t$ proceeds as follows:

```text
Initialize: all students and schools unmatched
Round t:
  1. Each unmatched student proposes to their highest-ranked school not yet rejected
  2. Each school tentatively accepts its capacity-best applicants, rejects others
  3. Rejected students remove that school from consideration
  4. Repeat until no rejections occur
Output: tentative acceptances become final matches
```

The algorithm terminates in finite rounds producing a stable matching where no student-school pair mutually prefer each other to their assigned partners. The experimental protocol assigns preference rankings to students and schools, implements the matching algorithm, then measures stability violations, strategic misreporting of preferences, and welfare distribution across sides.

Laboratory tests reveal systematic differences between student-proposing and school-proposing variants despite both producing stable matchings. The proposing side captures strategic advantage, receiving on average higher-ranked partners because they propose to most-preferred choices first while receivers hold multiple options before committing. When preference revelation is strategic rather than enforced, sophisticated students manipulate rankings by truncating lists or reordering middle-ranked options, attempting to game the algorithm's sequential proposal structure. These manipulations generate instabilities and efficiency losses, motivating strategy-proof mechanisms like top-trading cycles for object allocation problems.

Prediction market experiments operationalize information aggregation by creating tradable securities whose payoffs depend on verifiable future events. The binary prediction market design issues a security paying $\$1$ if event $E$ occurs and $\$0$ otherwise, with market price $p$ interpreted as the aggregate probability forecast $\mathbb{P}(E)$. Experimental implementations assign private signals to traders—each observes a noisy indicator correlated with the true state—then run continuous double-auction trading on the event security.

The information structure partitions into common-value and differential-information treatments. Under common-value, the event outcome affects all traders identically, so private signals convey information about a fundamental state. Differential-information designs vary signal precision across traders, creating experts with high-accuracy signals and noise traders with low-accuracy signals. The aggregation efficiency measure compares market price to the Bayesian posterior probability conditional on all private signals:

$$
p^* = \mathbb{P}(E \mid s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_n)
$$

computed via Bayes' rule from signal realizations. Experimental markets typically aggregate 70-85% of available information, with price deviations from Bayesian posterior reflecting overweighting of public information, failure to account for price informativeness, and herding dynamics.

The market scoring rule protocol provides an alternative aggregation mechanism where the institution maintains a probability distribution over event outcomes, and traders pay to revise the distribution incrementally. The logarithmic market scoring rule charges trader $i$ revising the probability from $p_{\text{old}}$ to $p_{\text{new}}$ the cost

$$
C(p_{\text{new}}) - C(p_{\text{old}}) = b[\log(p_{\text{new}}) - \log(p_{\text{old}})]
$$

where $b$ controls market depth. The trader receives payoff $C(p_{\text{final}})$ if the event occurs, zero otherwise, implying expected profit

$$
\pi_i = p_i \cdot C(p_{\text{final}}) - [C(p_{\text{new}}) - C(p_{\text{old}})]
$$

where $p_i$ denotes trader $i$'s subjective probability. Profit maximization incentivizes truthful revelation $p_{\text{new}} = p_i$, since the logarithmic scoring rule's gradient equals the probability ratio at truthful reports.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Experimental market design confronts a fundamental tension between theoretical elegance and practical implementation constraints: mechanisms proven incentive-compatible under complete rationality and common knowledge assumptions often fail catastrophically when deployed with cognitively bounded human subjects facing computational complexity, strategic uncertainty, or dynamically evolving information structures. Optimizing experimental markets for reliable convergence, allocative efficiency, and robust information aggregation requires anticipating systematic behavioral deviations, engineering protocols that remain performant under bounded rationality, and calibrating parameters to prevent institutional collapse.

Double auction convergence speed depends critically on the ratio of traders to competitive equilibrium quantity, termed market thickness. Thin markets with fewer than four buyers and four sellers exhibit slow convergence and persistent efficiency losses because bilateral matching opportunities become sparse, generating long bargaining delays and frequent coordination failures. The optimizer's first intervention point involves strategic replication of traders: rather than conducting a single market with eight total participants, partition into two independent markets of four each, allowing experimental observation of between-market variance while improving within-market convergence. Alternatively, implement multi-unit trading where individual agents can transact multiple units per period, increasing effective thickness without recruiting additional subjects.

The information transparency parameter offers a second optimization lever. While complete order book transparency accelerates price discovery by revealing aggregate supply and demand through bid-ask queues, it simultaneously facilitates tacit collusion in markets with asymmetric power distributions. When a dominant seller observes all standing bids, they gain strategic advantage by undercutting competitor asks only marginally, sustaining elevated prices without explicit communication. The failure mode manifests as persistent markups above competitive predictions despite sufficient trader numbers for theoretical competition. The mitigation strategy introduces adaptive transparency: display the best bid and ask prices plus last transaction price publicly, but obscure the depth of order books beyond the market's immediate inside spread. This partial transparency preserves price discovery benefits while limiting collusion opportunities.

Posted-offer markets suffer from an opposite failure mode: excessive price dispersion and coordination failures when sellers lack common knowledge of competitor actions. In the simultaneous posted-price stage, each seller chooses their price independently without observing competitors' decisions, generating Nash equilibria with symmetric mixed-strategy support over broad price ranges. Experimental implementations frequently observe wide dispersion with sellers choosing prices spanning 30-40% ranges around competitive levels, creating allocative inefficiencies as buyers arbitrarily select among heterogeneous offers. The optimization response introduces sequential pricing with incomplete information: sellers choose prices in random order observing predecessors' choices but uncertain of successors, creating a chain of best-responses that converges toward focal competitive prices while preserving strategic interest.

Market power experiments face calibration challenges balancing saliency of strategic incentives against convergence to theoretical predictions. If cost asymmetries are too modest—say marginal cost differences of only 10% across oligopolists—experimental noise and boundedly rational approximations drown out strategic effects, yielding outcomes statistically indistinguishable from perfect competition. Conversely, excessive asymmetry collapses markets into effective monopoly where the low-cost seller captures all transactions, eliminating interesting strategic interaction. The performance frontier targets intermediate asymmetry inducing Cournot quantity choices distinct from both competitive and monopoly benchmarks by at least one standard error of typical experimental noise, typically requiring dominant sellers to possess 40-60% cost advantages or capacity shares.

Repeated interaction introduces dynamic strategic complexity that either facilitates tacit collusion or destabilizes cooperation through end-game unraveling. In finitely repeated oligopoly experiments, backward induction logic predicts competitive play in the terminal period absent reputation concerns, which unravels cooperation in all preceding periods through subgame perfection. Experimental evidence reveals partial unraveling: cooperation sustains through middle periods but deteriorates sharply in the final three to five periods as terminal defection incentives become salient. The optimization protocol employs random termination with continuation probability $\delta \approx 0.9$, transforming the finitely repeated game into an effectively infinite-horizon environment supporting cooperative equilibria without known endpoint vulnerabilities.

Asset market bubble experiments confront the most vexing optimization problem in experimental economics: how to preserve bubble phenomena as an object of study while preventing complete market collapse and maintaining subject engagement through inevitable crashes. The failure mode termed "confusion-driven bubbles" arises when subjects misunderstand the fundamental value calculation, misperceive the horizon structure, or incorrectly believe the asset pays dividends after terminal period. These confusion bubbles contain no economic content, merely reflecting experimental protocol failures. The diagnostic separates confusion from genuine speculation bubbles by varying experience: running the same subjects through multiple market horizons with feedback. Genuine speculation bubbles attenuate but persist across repetitions as subjects learn, while confusion bubbles vanish completely once subjects comprehend the environment.

The portfolio balance optimization addresses liquidity constraints that limit rational arbitrage against bubble prices. If rational traders recognize that market prices exceed fundamental values but possess insufficient cash reserves to short-sell or exhaust their asset holdings attempting to arbitrage, then momentum traders can sustain bubbles unconstrained. The calibration protocol sets initial cash-to-asset-value ratios between 2:1 and 3:1, providing sufficient leverage for rational arbitrage while preventing complete market freezes. Short-selling constraints offer a second lever: allowing unlimited shorting eliminates most bubble phenomena as arbitrageurs crush mispricing immediately, while prohibiting shorting entirely enables explosive bubbles crashing catastrophically. The performance optimum permits constrained shorting—say up to 50% of holdings—that moderates but does not eliminate bubbles, generating economically meaningful price dynamics.

Auction mechanism experiments optimize across several failure dimensions. The first-price sealed-bid format suffers from winner's curse amplification under common values: when the item's value is identical to all bidders but each observes noisy private signals, winning the auction conveys adverse information that opponents observed lower signals, implying the winner likely overestimated true value. Experimental bidders systematically fail to account for this winner's curse conditioning, generating negative expected profits for winners and auction revenue exceeding rational benchmarks. The optimization intervention either enforces private-value structures where winner's curse cannot arise, or implements extensive training sessions with repeated auctions and detailed feedback calculating conditional expected values given win outcomes.

Multi-unit auction exposure problems generate failures when bidders rationally refuse to bid aggressively on complementary items individually, fearing losses from winning subsets. The pathological outcome observes zero bids on high-value items because no bidder will risk exposure, yielding complete market failure with zero revenue and zero efficiency. The mechanism design solution implements package bidding allowing explicit bids on item bundles: bidder $i$ can submit bid $b_i(\{A,B\}) = 90$ on the package while bidding zero on individual items, eliminating exposure risk. The winner determination problem computes the revenue-maximizing allocation

$$
\max \sum_i x_{i,S} b_i(S)
$$

subject to allocation constraints $\sum_{i,S: j \in S} x_{i,S} \leq 1$ for each item $j$, where $x_{i,S} \in \{0,1\}$ indicates whether bidder $i$ wins package $S$. This integer programming problem becomes NP-hard in problem size, requiring heuristic or approximation algorithms for combinatorial auctions exceeding twenty items.

The experimental implementation scales tractably by restricting bidding languages to structured formats rather than exponential full expressiveness. The OR bidding language allows bids on individual items and predefined packages with free-disposal semantics: winning multiple bids yields payoff equal to the highest-value satisfied bid. The XOR language enforces exclusivity: bidders submit multiple package bids with the constraint that at most one bid can win. Laboratory experiments demonstrate that OR bidding achieves 90-95% of unconstrained efficiency while reducing computational burden by factor of $2^m$ where $m$ denotes item count.

Matching market experiments face manipulation vulnerabilities when agents perceive strategic gains from misreporting preferences. The Gale-Shapley deferred acceptance algorithm is strategy-proof for the proposing side but manipulable by receivers: schools can improve match outcomes by truncating reported preference lists or artificially reducing capacities. Experimental detection of manipulation requires tracking stability violations—situations where a student-school pair mutually prefer each other to assigned matches—which reveal that reported preferences differ from true preferences. The optimization protocol either implements the top-trading-cycles mechanism proven strategy-proof for object allocation problems, or introduces costly misreporting where submitting false preferences incurs explicit fees discouraging manipulation.

Preference elicitation mechanisms optimize cognitive load by reducing the dimensionality of strategy spaces. Rather than soliciting complete rank-order preferences over hundreds of schools, the serial dictatorship with deferrals elicits preferences iteratively: each student in priority order selects their most-preferred available school, which tentatively accepts but can later reject if higher-priority students propose. This dynamic elicitation protocol requires students to reason only about current decisions given available options rather than anticipating full game trees, substantially reducing strategic complexity while preserving stability and efficiency properties.

Prediction market aggregation failures arise from herding dynamics where traders overweight public price signals relative to private information, generating information cascades that lock markets away from Bayesian posteriors. The failure mechanism initializes when early traders with noisy signals happen to move prices in a particular direction, causing subsequent traders to mistrust their own contradictory signals and follow the price trend rather than correcting mispricing. The cascade manifests empirically as rapid price movements followed by persistent stagnation despite later traders receiving informative signals. The optimization intervention introduces market-making subsidies: the institution commits to supplying liquidity by continuously posting bids and asks at narrow spreads around current prices, reducing the impact of individual noise trades and preventing cascade lock-in.

The information structure optimization carefully calibrates signal accuracy distributions. If all traders receive identically informative signals, then aggregation provides minimal value beyond any single trader's assessment. The performance frontier assigns heterogeneous signal precisions with a minority of high-accuracy experts and majority of moderate-accuracy generalists. This structure maximizes aggregation value—the market price substantially outperforms individual forecasts—while maintaining sufficient liquidity from generalist trading to prevent market breakdown. Experimental implementations with precision ratios around 3:1 between expert and generalist signal-to-noise ratios achieve robust aggregation across diverse question domains.

The market scoring rule depth parameter $b$ trades off subsidization costs against prediction accuracy. Larger $b$ values increase market liquidity by reducing the marginal cost of shifting probabilities, encouraging more aggressive information revelation, but expose the institution to larger expected losses subsidizing the market. The optimal calibration sets depth such that informed traders' expected profits from moving prices to true posteriors roughly equals the cognitive and opportunity costs of participating in the mechanism, typically requiring institution subsidies around 5-10% of total prediction payoffs. Automated market-making algorithms maintain continuous liquidity by dynamically adjusting depth parameters based on observed trading volumes and price volatility.

The temporal horizon optimization addresses prediction staleness in long-duration markets. Forecasts made months before event resolution incorporate only information available at submission time, becoming outdated as new signals arrive. The failure mode observes prediction markets that converge rapidly to consensus then stagnate for extended periods despite evolving information environments. The dynamic elicitation solution implements continuous double-auction trading rather than one-shot submissions, allowing traders to revise forecasts costlessly as information arrives. This continuous protocol generates time-series price trajectories encoding information arrival dynamics, providing not just terminal forecasts but complete probabilistic transition paths.

The experimental market design cycle closes with systematic failure analysis comparing observed outcomes against theoretical benchmarks, diagnosing the mechanism by which deviations arise—strategic sophistication, bounded rationality, computational complexity, or information structure—then engineering protocol refinements addressing the diagnosed failure mode. This iterative optimization process transforms market institutions from theoretical curiosities into practical computational infrastructure capable of eliciting preferences, aggregating information, and allocating resources robustly under realistic behavioral and environmental constraints. The experimental laboratory serves as both testing ground for mechanism evaluation and training environment for understanding how institutional architecture shapes economic outcomes, bridging the gap between normative theory and descriptive reality that defines experimental economics as both science and engineering discipline.
