# Lesson 2: Individual Choice Under Risk

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The investigation of how human beings make choices when outcomes are uncertain stands at the heart of experimental economics. Classical economic theory, rooted in the von Neumann-Morgenstern expected utility framework, posits that rational agents evaluate risky prospects by calculating the probability-weighted sum of utilities across all possible states of the world. Under this axiomatization, individuals possess stable, consistent preference orderings that satisfy completeness, transitivity, continuity, and the independence axiom. The independence axiom, in particular, holds profound implications: if an agent prefers lottery $A$ to lottery $B$, then mixing both lotteries with any third lottery $C$ at identical probability weights should preserve that preference ordering. This elegant mathematical structure, formalized in the 1940s, became the cornerstone of decision theory, finance, insurance, and welfare economics.

Yet experimental evidence accumulated over seven decades reveals persistent, systematic deviations from the expected utility predictions. These violations are not merely statistical noise or transient errors; they represent fundamental regularities in human judgment under uncertainty. The Allais paradox, first documented by Maurice Allais in 1953, demonstrates that individuals consistently violate the independence axiom when confronted with particular pairs of lottery choices. Subjects exhibit preference reversals: their stated willingness-to-pay for a gamble differs systematically from the minimum price at which they would sell the same gamble, even when the elicitation mechanism is incentive-compatible. Temporal discounting experiments reveal hyperbolic rather than exponential time preferences, with discount rates declining as delays increase—a pattern incompatible with the constant discounting assumption embedded in standard intertemporal utility models.

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's prospect theory, published in 1979 and refined in 1992 as cumulative prospect theory, emerged as the dominant alternative framework. Prospect theory replaces the expected utility function with a value function defined over gains and losses relative to a reference point, exhibiting diminishing sensitivity and loss aversion. Probability weights are transformed through a non-linear weighting function that overweights small probabilities and underweights moderate and high probabilities. The theory accounts for the fourfold pattern of risk attitudes: risk aversion for gains and risk seeking for losses in the moderate-to-high probability range, with the pattern reversing for low-probability outcomes. This descriptive model has reshaped our understanding of insurance purchases, gambling behavior, financial asset pricing anomalies, and policy choices involving risk.

Experimental economists deploy rigorous elicitation mechanisms to measure these preference structures. The Becker-DeGroot-Marschak (BDM) mechanism induces truthful revelation of reservation prices through a second-price auction structure, ensuring that subjects maximize expected payoffs by reporting their true valuation. Multiple price lists (MPL), pioneered by Holt and Laury, present subjects with ordered sequences of binary choices between safe and risky lotteries, with the switching point revealing the interval containing the subject's risk parameter. These mechanisms must satisfy incentive compatibility: subjects' optimal strategy must be to reveal their true preferences. When mechanisms fail this criterion, measured preferences become contaminated by strategic misrepresentation, rendering the data uninformative about underlying decision processes.

The theoretical edifice of expected utility theory rests on four key axioms. Completeness requires that for any two lotteries $L_1$ and $L_2$, the agent can state either $L_1 \succeq L_2$, $L_2 \succeq L_1$, or both (indifference). Transitivity demands that if $L_1 \succeq L_2$ and $L_2 \succeq L_3$, then $L_1 \succeq L_3$. Continuity ensures that for any three lotteries where $L_1 \succ L_2 \succ L_3$, there exists a probability $p \in [0,1]$ such that the agent is indifferent between $L_2$ and the compound lottery yielding $L_1$ with probability $p$ and $L_3$ with probability $1-p$. The independence axiom states that for any lotteries $L_1, L_2, L_3$ and any $\alpha \in (0,1]$, we have $L_1 \succeq L_2$ if and only if $\alpha L_1 + (1-\alpha)L_3 \succeq \alpha L_2 + (1-\alpha)L_3$. When these axioms hold, there exists a utility function $u: X \to \mathbb{R}$ over outcomes such that lottery $L$ is represented by expected utility $EU(L) = \sum_i p_i u(x_i)$, where $p_i$ denotes the probability of outcome $x_i$.

The profound insight of expected utility theory lies in its separation of beliefs (probabilities) from tastes (utilities). Risk aversion, under this framework, reduces entirely to the concavity of the utility function. An agent with utility function $u$ facing a lottery $L$ with expected value $\mathbb{E}[L]$ is risk-averse if $u(\mathbb{E}[L]) > EU(L)$, which holds precisely when $u$ is strictly concave. The coefficient of absolute risk aversion, defined as $A(x) = -u''(x)/u'(x)$, quantifies local curvature and determines how much wealth an agent would pay to avoid small gambles. The coefficient of relative risk aversion, $R(x) = -xu''(x)/u'(x)$, governs portfolio allocation decisions and remains empirically more stable across wealth levels. Constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) utility functions, taking the form $u(x) = x^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$ for $\gamma \neq 1$ and $u(x) = \ln(x)$ for $\gamma = 1$, dominate empirical applications because they predict wealth-invariant portfolio shares.

Experimental tests of expected utility theory require careful attention to the domain of outcomes, the probability structure, and the monetary stakes involved. Small-stakes gambles present a paradox identified by Matthew Rabin: if an agent rejects a 50-50 gamble of losing \$10 or gaining \$11 at all wealth levels, expected utility theory implies absurdly high levels of risk aversion for large stakes, predicting rejection of 50-50 gambles of losing \$100 or gaining arbitrary amounts exceeding thousands or millions of dollars. This calibration theorem demonstrates that expected utility theory with concave utility functions cannot simultaneously explain both small-stakes risk aversion and reasonable behavior at large stakes. The resolution requires either abandoning expected utility in favor of reference-dependent models like prospect theory, or acknowledging that small-stakes risk aversion stems from decision costs, cognitive effort, or other frictions beyond curvature of the utility function.

The Allais paradox exposes the fragility of the independence axiom through a pair of choice problems. In Problem 1, subjects choose between Lottery A (receiving \$1 million with certainty) and Lottery B (receiving \$5 million with probability 0.10, \$1 million with probability 0.89, and \$0 with probability 0.01). In Problem 2, subjects choose between Lottery C (receiving \$1 million with probability 0.11 and \$0 with probability 0.89) and Lottery D (receiving \$5 million with probability 0.10 and \$0 with probability 0.90). Modal responses exhibit the pattern $(A, D)$: preferring certainty in Problem 1 but the higher-risk option in Problem 2. This pattern violates independence because Lottery A can be written as a mixture of \$1 million with certainty (weight 0.89) and a 11-1 lottery of \$1 million versus \$0 (weight 0.11), while Lottery B mixes the same components with the certainty outcome replaced by a lottery. The $(A, D)$ pattern reveals that subjects weight the certainty component differently when it appears as a sure thing versus when it is embedded in a compound lottery.

Prospect theory resolves the Allais paradox by replacing linear probability weighting with a non-linear probability weighting function $w(p)$. The cumulative prospect theory formulation applies decision weights to cumulative probabilities rather than individual outcome probabilities, ensuring that preferences satisfy first-order stochastic dominance. For a lottery with outcomes $x_{-m}, \ldots, x_0, \ldots, x_n$ arranged in increasing order where $x_0$ represents the reference point (typically the status quo), the prospect theory value function is:

$$V = \sum_{i=-m}^{-1} w^-(F_i) v(x_i) + \sum_{i=1}^{n} w^+(1-F_{i-1}) v(x_i)$$

where $F_i$ denotes the cumulative probability of receiving outcome $x_i$ or worse, $v$ is the value function defined over gains and losses, and $w^+$ and $w^-$ are probability weighting functions for gains and losses respectively. The value function exhibits three key properties: it is defined on deviations from a reference point rather than absolute wealth levels, it is concave in the gain domain and convex in the loss domain (diminishing sensitivity), and it is steeper for losses than for gains of equal magnitude (loss aversion), typically satisfying $v(-x) \approx -\lambda v(x)$ with $\lambda \in [2, 2.5]$.

The probability weighting function captures systematic departures from objective probabilities. Empirical estimates consistently show inverse-S shapes: $w(p) > p$ for small $p$ and $w(p) < p$ for moderate to high $p$, with $w(0) = 0$ and $w(1) = 1$. The one-parameter specification proposed by Tversky and Kahneman takes the form:

$$w(p) = \frac{p^\delta}{(p^\delta + (1-p)^\delta)^{1/\delta}}$$

with typical estimates of $\delta \approx 0.65$ for gains and $\delta \approx 0.70$ for losses. This functional form exhibits the requisite inverse-S shape and implies that a probability of 0.01 receives a decision weight of approximately 0.06, while a probability of 0.50 receives a weight of approximately 0.42. The overweighting of small probabilities explains simultaneous purchases of lottery tickets (overweighting the small probability of a large gain) and insurance (overweighting the small probability of a large loss), a behavioral pattern inexplicable under expected utility theory with any single concave utility function.

Preference reversal phenomena, documented extensively since the 1970s, reveal that choice and pricing elicitation mechanisms yield systematically inconsistent preference orderings. A canonical preference reversal study presents subjects with two gambles: a P-bet (probability bet) offering a high probability of winning a modest amount, and a \$-bet (money bet) offering a low probability of winning a larger amount. When asked to choose between the gambles, subjects typically select the P-bet. Yet when asked to state their minimum selling price for each gamble, subjects assign a higher valuation to the \$-bet. This pattern reverses the preference ordering and cannot be explained by random error or indifference, as the differences are both statistically significant and economically substantial.

The preference reversal phenomenon challenges the fundamental assumption that preferences are a stable primitive of economic models. One explanation invokes scale compatibility: subjects weight attributes more heavily when the response scale matches the attribute scale. Pricing tasks use a monetary response scale, leading subjects to weight the monetary outcome dimension more heavily; choice tasks lack a natural scale, leading to greater weight on probabilities. An alternative explanation emphasizes anchoring: when generating a valuation for the \$-bet, subjects anchor on the large prize amount and make insufficient adjustments for the low probability. The persistence of preference reversals even under financial incentives, market experience, and repeated play suggests that the phenomenon reflects deep features of cognitive processing rather than superficial confusion.

Temporal discounting experiments investigate how individuals trade off payoffs received at different points in time. The exponential discounting model, standard in macroeconomics and finance, represents intertemporal preferences through a discount function $D(t) = e^{-\rho t}$ where $\rho$ denotes the constant instantaneous discount rate. Under exponential discounting, if an agent prefers \$100 today over \$110 in one week, the agent must also prefer \$100 in 52 weeks over \$110 in 53 weeks, maintaining the same time-consistent indifference tradeoff. Experimental evidence overwhelmingly rejects this prediction. Subjects exhibit present bias: they demand substantial premiums to delay immediate rewards but accept much smaller premiums for delays shifted equally far into the future. This hyperbolic discounting pattern is captured by discount functions of the form $D(t) = 1/(1+kt)$ or the quasi-hyperbolic $(\beta, \delta)$ specification where immediate payoffs receive weight 1, all future payoffs are discounted by a present-bias factor $\beta < 1$, and then exponentially discounted at rate $\delta$ per period.

The distinction between risk and ambiguity, clarified by Frank Knight and formalized by Daniel Ellsberg, introduces a further dimension of complexity. Risk refers to situations where outcome probabilities are known; ambiguity (or Knightian uncertainty) refers to situations where probabilities are unknown or imprecisely specified. The Ellsberg paradox demonstrates that individuals exhibit ambiguity aversion: they prefer betting on events with known probabilities over events with unknown probabilities, even when the expected values are equal or the ambiguous option offers a higher worst-case outcome. An urn contains 90 balls: 30 are red, and the remaining 60 are either black or yellow in unknown proportions. Subjects typically prefer betting on red (probability 1/3) over betting on black (probability unknown but bounded in [0, 2/3]), and simultaneously prefer betting on "black or yellow" (probability 2/3) over betting on "red or yellow" (probability between 1/3 and 1), violating the sure-thing principle and revealing a preference for known over unknown probabilities.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

Experimental elicitation of risk preferences requires designing mechanisms that induce truthful revelation while minimizing cognitive load and strategic confounds. The choice of elicitation method profoundly affects the measured preference parameters, and understanding the internal mechanics of each mechanism is essential for proper implementation and interpretation.

The Becker-DeGroot-Marschak mechanism operates as a second-price auction against a random price drawn from a known distribution. The subject states a reservation price $r$ for a lottery $L$. The experimenter then draws a random offer price $p$ from a uniform distribution over an interval $[p_{\min}, p_{\max}]$. If $p < r$, the subject pays $p$ and receives the lottery; if $p \geq r$, the subject receives $p$ and foregoes the lottery. The mechanism achieves incentive compatibility because the subject's optimal strategy is to report the true valuation $v$. If the subject reports $r < v$ and the drawn price falls in the interval $(r, v)$, the subject misses a profitable transaction; if the subject reports $r > v$ and the drawn price falls in $(v, r)$, the subject is forced to purchase at a price exceeding their valuation. Reporting $r = v$ dominates all alternative strategies.

The BDM mechanism admits several implementation variants. The classic version uses a continuous price distribution, but discrete approximations using a finite set of possible prices simplify physical implementation. The binary BDM presents a single random price rather than drawing from a distribution, reducing the subject's computational burden but introducing additional complexity in the probability calculation. Proper implementation requires that subjects understand the second-price structure; experimental instructions typically include multiple examples and comprehension checks. Despite theoretical incentive compatibility, empirical studies document systematic overbidding relative to expected utility predictions, particularly for low-probability high-payoff lotteries. This pattern suggests either misunderstanding of the mechanism or genuine probability weighting effects.

Multiple price lists present subjects with an ordered series of binary choices between a safe option and a risky option, with the relative attractiveness of the safe option increasing monotonically down the list. The canonical Holt-Laury design uses ten rows, each offering a choice between Option A (a safer lottery with lower variance) and Option B (a riskier lottery with higher variance). Both lotteries have the same two monetary outcomes in each row, but the probabilities shift across rows. For example, Row 1 might offer Option A: (0.1 probability of \$2.00, 0.9 probability of \$1.60) versus Option B: (0.1 probability of \$3.85, 0.9 probability of \$0.10). Row 10 offers Option A: (1.0 probability of \$2.00) versus Option B: (1.0 probability of \$3.85). Risk-neutral subjects switch from A to B at Row 5 or 6; risk-averse subjects switch later.

The multiple price list exploits the monotonicity of expected utility in probabilities. As the probability of the high outcome increases, Option B becomes progressively more attractive. A subject with CRRA utility function $u(x) = x^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$ will switch from Option A to Option B when:

$$p \cdot 2.00^{1-\gamma} + (1-p) \cdot 1.60^{1-\gamma} < p \cdot 3.85^{1-\gamma} + (1-p) \cdot 0.10^{1-\gamma}$$

The switching point identifies an interval for the risk aversion parameter $\gamma$. If the subject switches at Row 7 (where $p = 0.7$), the implied risk aversion coefficient falls within a specific range, allowing the experimenter to classify the subject's risk preferences. The mechanism assumes that subjects exhibit a single switch point; multiple switches or inconsistent patterns indicate either indifference, stochastic preferences, or confusion. Experimental protocols typically exclude subjects exhibiting more than one switch or switching back from B to A.

Convex time budgets, introduced by Andreoni and Sprenger, extend the multiple price list approach to intertemporal choice. Subjects allocate tokens between an earlier payment date $t$ and a later payment date $t + k$, with each token allocated to the later date yielding $1 + r$ tokens. The budget constraint is linear, allowing identification of both the discount rate and the curvature of the utility function. Earlier studies using binary choices between dated payments confounded time preference with risk aversion because subjects implicitly faced a risk of payment non-delivery. The convex budget method separates these dimensions by observing the entire budget allocation rather than a single switch point, permitting simultaneous estimation of $\rho$ (the discount rate) and $\gamma$ (the risk aversion parameter).

Experimental testing of the Allais paradox requires careful framing to ensure subjects perceive the choice structure correctly. The original Allais problems involve modest probabilities applied to large monetary outcomes, making them suitable for hypothetical choice studies but expensive to implement with real incentives. Scaled-down versions using smaller amounts or probability equivalents preserve the logical structure while fitting experimental budgets. A common implementation presents Problem 1 as a choice between \$100 for certain versus a lottery yielding \$500 with probability 0.10, \$100 with probability 0.89, and \$0 with probability 0.01. Problem 2 offers \$100 with probability 0.11 (and \$0 with probability 0.89) versus \$500 with probability 0.10 (and \$0 with probability 0.90). The experimenter records the proportion of subjects choosing $(A, D)$ and $(B, C)$, testing whether the independence axiom holds.

A more direct test of independence uses the common consequence effect design. Subjects choose between Lottery X and Lottery Y, which differ only in outcome $x_i$, then choose between Lottery X′ and Lottery Y′, which replace a common outcome $x_j$ in both original lotteries with outcome $x_k$. Independence requires that the preference ordering remain unchanged. Empirical tests consistently find violation rates between 30% and 60%, far exceeding the rate attributable to random error. The magnitude of the violation increases when the common consequence occurs with high probability and the distinctive outcomes differ substantially.

Prospect theory experiments require eliciting both the value function and the probability weighting function. Chained pairwise choice questions can trace out the value function by offering sequences of choices between amounts in the gain domain and amounts in the loss domain, identifying indifference points that reveal the loss aversion parameter $\lambda$. The curvature parameters $\alpha$ (for gains) and $\beta$ (for losses) in the value function $v(x) = x^\alpha$ for $x \geq 0$ and $v(x) = -\lambda(-x)^\beta$ for $x < 0$ emerge from choices between lotteries at varying outcome levels. Median estimates from large-sample studies converge on $\alpha \approx 0.88$, $\beta \approx 0.88$, and $\lambda \approx 2.25$.

Probability weighting function elicitation exploits the certainty equivalent method. Present the subject with a lottery $(x, p; 0, 1-p)$ offering outcome $x$ with probability $p$ and zero otherwise. Elicit the certainty equivalent $CE(p)$, defined as the sure amount that renders the subject indifferent to the lottery. Under prospect theory, $v(CE(p)) = w(p) v(x)$, yielding $w(p) = v(CE(p))/v(x)$. By varying $p$ across a range of probability levels and eliciting certainty equivalents for each, the experimenter traces out the probability weighting function. This method requires prior knowledge of the value function or simultaneous estimation of both functions.

Preference reversal experiments manipulate the elicitation mode while holding the choice objects constant. The standard protocol presents subjects with two gambles displayed simultaneously: the P-bet (for example, \$4 with probability 0.90, \$0 with probability 0.10) and the \$-bet (for example, \$40 with probability 0.10, \$0 with probability 0.90). In the choice phase, subjects indicate which gamble they prefer. In the valuation phase, subjects state their minimum selling price for each gamble using the BDM mechanism. The experimenter calculates the proportion of subjects exhibiting the canonical reversal pattern: choosing P but pricing \$ higher than P. Robust experimental designs randomly assign subjects to choice-first or pricing-first orders, include filler items to obscure the research hypothesis, and employ incentive-compatible payment mechanisms where one randomly selected decision determines earnings.

Temporal discounting experiments use dated monetary rewards to measure intertemporal substitution rates. The standard binary choice format presents pairs such as "\$50 in 30 days versus \$55 in 60 days," varying the front-end delay and the reward magnitudes. The set of indifference points traces out the discount function. Hyperbolic discounting predicts that the indifference premium increases as the front-end delay decreases, with the largest premiums required for immediate rewards. The quasi-hyperbolic $(\beta, \delta)$ model can be estimated by including choices with zero front-end delay (identifying $\beta$) and choices with long front-end delays (identifying $\delta$). Typical estimates yield $\beta \approx 0.7$ and $\delta \approx 0.99$ (daily), implying that immediate rewards receive approximately 30% greater weight than rewards delayed by even a single day, after which standard exponential discounting applies.

Payment protocols in temporal discounting studies must address the credibility of future payments. Subjects may discount ostensibly delayed payments not due to time preference but due to perceived default risk or transaction costs. Experimental designs address this concern by conducting studies in university settings where future payment credibility is high, using automated payment systems that eliminate retrieval costs, or front-loading the experimental session and delaying only the payment disbursement. Paying all subjects by check or bank transfer for both immediate and delayed payments equates transaction costs across time horizons. Studies employing these controls continue to find substantial present bias, confirming that the observed time preference patterns reflect genuine discounting rather than confounded risk perceptions.

The choice of payment mechanism interacts with stake levels and subject populations. Random lottery incentives pay only a subset of subjects based on random draws, allowing higher per-subject stakes within a fixed budget. This method assumes that subjects evaluate individual choices independently, maximizing expected value on each decision as if it were the sole payoff-relevant choice. Violations of the reduction of compound lotteries axiom or portfolio effects could invalidate this assumption, though empirical evidence suggests that random lottery incentives yield results similar to paying all subjects. Flat fees detach earnings from performance, eliminating incentives but avoiding wealth effects and allowing measurement of hypothetical preferences. Incentivized designs dominate the literature when feasible, given the robust finding that real incentives attenuate social desirability bias and increase attention.

Data collection architectures for computerized experiments involve trial sequencing logic, randomization protocols, and attention checks. The experimental software presents stimuli according to a pre-specified randomization scheme, typically balanced across subjects to ensure equal representation of conditions. Within-subject designs expose each subject to multiple conditions, increasing statistical power but introducing order effects and learning. Between-subject designs assign each subject to a single condition, avoiding contamination but requiring larger samples. Mixed designs combine both features, for example by administering a common set of baseline choices followed by condition-specific treatments. Attention checks embedded within instruction comprehension quizzes or as catch trials (choices with dominant options) identify inattentive subjects whose data may introduce noise.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Experimental studies of individual choice under risk face several categories of failure modes that threaten internal validity, external validity, and statistical conclusion validity. Rigorous experimental design anticipates these failure modes and implements procedural safeguards to mitigate their impact.

One pervasive threat involves subject misunderstanding of the elicitation mechanism. The BDM procedure, despite its theoretical incentive compatibility, relies on subjects grasping the second-price auction structure. If subjects misperceive the mechanism as a first-price auction where they pay their stated bid, they will strategically underbid, contaminating the elicited valuations. Experimental protocols address this risk through multi-stage instructions: written descriptions, graphical illustrations showing the mechanism's operation for hypothetical bids, and practice rounds with feedback. Comprehension quizzes with mandatory correct responses before proceeding to the incentivized trials filter out subjects who fail to understand the task. Post-experimental questionnaires asking subjects to explain the mechanism in their own words provide diagnostic information about residual confusion.

Multiple price lists confront the challenge of subjects exhibiting multiple switch points or inconsistent choice patterns. If a subject chooses the safe option in Row 7, then switches to the risky option in Row 8, but reverts to the safe option in Row 9, the data cannot be reconciled with any monotonic utility function. Such patterns may reflect genuine indifference, stochastic preferences, bounded rationality, or simple inattention. Researchers adopt various exclusion criteria: some discard all subjects with any inconsistency, others exclude only subjects with two or more switches, and still others impute the risk parameter using the first or modal switch point. The choice of exclusion rule affects sample composition and estimated preference distributions, introducing a researcher degree of freedom that sensitivity analyses should explore. Recent approaches model stochastic choice explicitly using random utility models, acknowledging that choice probabilities rather than deterministic choices constitute the data-generating process.

Prospect theory experiments face the challenge of identifying a subject's reference point. The theory assumes that value is coded relative to a reference point, but the reference point is not directly observable and may depend on framing, expectations, and prior outcomes. Experimenters typically assume that the reference point coincides with the subject's endowment at the beginning of the experimental session, treating all experimental earnings as gains. This assumption breaks down when subjects integrate experimental earnings with their outside wealth, form expectations about typical experimental earnings, or adopt different reference points for different choices within the session. Designs that explicitly manipulate the reference point through prior losses, endowments, or salient anchors allow testing whether reference point shifts generate the predicted changes in risk attitudes.

Payment credibility concerns loom large in temporal discounting experiments. If subjects doubt that they will receive delayed payments, their stated preferences confound time preference with perceived default risk. University laboratories typically enjoy high credibility, but studies conducted with online subject pools or in field settings must work harder to establish trust. Prepaying subjects for participation while delaying the bonus portion of earnings equalizes credibility across time horizons. Employing established payment intermediaries such as PayPal or university financial offices lends institutional credibility. Researchers report default rates and demonstrate that nearly all subjects who complete the study receive their payments on schedule, providing transparency that builds trust in subsequent studies.

Stake size effects pose both practical and theoretical challenges. Small stakes may fail to engage serious deliberation, leading subjects to choose capriciously or experiment with dominated options out of curiosity. Yet large stakes expand research costs and introduce wealth effects that complicate interpretation: a risk-averse subject who receives a large windfall gain from an early trial may exhibit reduced risk aversion in subsequent trials. Studies varying stake magnitudes within a given paradigm generally find that preference anomalies persist even at substantial stakes, though the magnitude of some effects attenuates. The calibration theorem discussed earlier suggests that small-stakes anomalies cannot be dismissed as trivial, since they imply implausible large-stakes behavior under expected utility theory.

Order effects and anchoring contaminate within-subject designs. A subject who first encounters an Allais pair framed in terms of gains may approach a subsequent pair framed in terms of losses with heightened awareness of the experimenter's hypothesis, inducing experimenter demand effects. Randomizing question order across subjects addresses this concern but increases sample size requirements. Counterbalancing, where half the subjects receive order A-B and half receive order B-A, controls for order effects at the group level. Filler items interspersed between critical trials obscure the research hypothesis, though excessively long experimental sessions induce fatigue and decrease attention.

Response mode effects interact with the structure of the elicitation mechanism. Pricing tasks invoke a different cognitive process than choice tasks: valuation requires constructing a cardinal representation and mapping it onto a monetary scale, while choice permits ordinal comparison without cardinalization. Preference reversals exploit this distinction, suggesting that the preference ordering is not a primitive but rather emerges from the elicitation process itself. This constructive preference interpretation challenges the notion of stable underlying preferences that experiments aim to measure. Experimental economists respond by standardizing elicitation methods within studies, using multiple complementary methods to triangulate underlying preferences, and modeling response mode as a factor in the data-generating process.

Statistical power calculations in preference elicitation studies require specifying the expected effect size, desired significance level, and test structure. Preference reversals typically exhibit large effect sizes, with 30% to 60% of subjects demonstrating the reversal pattern compared to a 10% to 15% baseline rate of inconsistency expected from random error. Detecting such effects requires sample sizes of approximately 50 to 100 subjects per condition. Risk aversion parameter estimation using multiple price lists yields continuous measures, permitting parametric tests with greater statistical efficiency. Testing prospect theory predictions about probability weighting requires sufficient variation in probability levels and stakes, typically necessitating within-subject designs with 20 to 40 lottery choices per subject.

Econometric analysis of experimental data from risk preference elicitation employs maximum likelihood estimation when data take the form of discrete choices and non-linear least squares when data consist of certainty equivalents or reservation prices. The structural approach specifies a functional form for utility or value (such as CRRA or prospect theory), derives predicted choices or valuations, and estimates the parameters that best fit the observed data. The reduced-form approach regresses outcomes on treatment indicators and control variables without imposing a specific utility functional form, testing for treatment effects without committing to a structural model. Both approaches face the challenge that individual-level preference parameters are often estimated imprecisely due to limited within-subject observations, leading researchers to employ hierarchical Bayesian models that pool information across subjects while allowing for individual heterogeneity.

Replication and robustness checks constitute essential components of high-quality experimental research. Exact replications using identical protocols, subject pools, and stake levels test whether the original findings reflect stable behavioral regularities or idiosyncratic sample characteristics. Conceptual replications vary design features—such as stake magnitudes, probability levels, or outcome domains—to assess the generalizability of the phenomenon. Meta-analyses aggregating results across multiple studies provide quantitative estimates of effect sizes and their variability across contexts. The preference reversal phenomenon, for example, has been replicated in dozens of studies across multiple decades, countries, and subject populations, establishing it as one of the most robust anomalies in experimental economics.

Cross-cultural variation in risk preferences raises questions about the universality of experimental findings. Studies comparing subject pools from individualistic versus collectivist cultures, high versus low income countries, and urban versus rural populations document systematic differences in risk aversion, loss aversion, and probability weighting. Chinese subjects exhibit greater loss aversion than American subjects in some studies; subjects from high-trust societies show lower ambiguity aversion. These differences suggest that risk preferences incorporate learned cultural norms and institutional experiences rather than reflecting immutable cognitive architecture. Experimental economists increasingly conduct parallel studies across multiple sites using standardized protocols, enabling direct cross-cultural comparisons that separate universal regularities from context-dependent patterns.

Field experiments testing risk preferences in naturalistic settings confront the tradeoff between experimental control and ecological validity. Laboratory experiments achieve tight control over payoffs, probabilities, and information structures but employ artificial tasks divorced from consequential real-world decisions. Field experiments embed experimental manipulations into authentic economic contexts such as insurance purchases, investment decisions, or occupational choices, gaining realism at the cost of reduced control over confounding factors. Natural experiments exploit exogenous variation in risk exposure—such as natural disasters, policy changes, or market shocks—to study revealed preferences in high-stakes environments. Each approach illuminates different aspects of decision-making under risk, and triangulating across methods builds a comprehensive understanding.

Experimental software platforms such as z-Tree, oTree, and Qualtrics provide standardized tools for implementing risk preference elicitation studies. These platforms handle trial randomization, response recording, payment calculation, and data export, reducing programming burden and minimizing implementation errors. The experimenter defines the choice architecture, specifies the randomization scheme, and configures the payment rule through a graphical interface or scripting language. Real-time data monitoring dashboards allow experimenters to detect technical issues, track completion rates, and identify suspicious response patterns such as straight-lining or excessively rapid responses. Automated data validation checks flag logical inconsistencies such as multiple switch points in MPL designs or out-of-bounds BDM bids.

Ethical considerations specific to risk preference experiments center on informed consent and payment equity. Subjects must understand that the experimental task involves real financial consequences determined partly by chance. Informed consent documents explain the payment mechanism, the role of randomness, and the range of possible earnings. Institutional review boards scrutinize protocols to ensure that no subject can lose money beyond their participation fee and that the expected earnings are reasonable relative to the time commitment and local wage rates. When experimental designs include loss frames, the protocol must clarify that "losses" refer to foregone gains rather than out-of-pocket losses, maintaining the ethical requirement that subjects leave the experiment no worse off than they arrived.

The integration of risk preference experiments into economic research extends beyond testing theoretical models. Elicited risk parameters serve as control variables in studies of labor supply, entrepreneurship, technology adoption, and financial decision-making. Individual differences in risk aversion predict portfolio allocation, insurance purchases, occupational choice, and health behaviors. Prospect theory parameters correlate with consumer responses to price framing, marketing strategies, and contract designs. Temporal discounting measures predict credit card borrowing, retirement savings, and health investment decisions. This predictive validity establishes risk preference elicitation as a valuable tool for applied economic research, justifying the methodological investment required to implement rigorous experimental protocols.
