# Operational Verification Labs: Keynesian

## Objective & Operational Lab Setup

These operational verification labs train you to implement, calibrate, and empirically validate the core transmission mechanisms of Keynesian macroeconomic theory. You will construct working models of aggregate demand deficiency, liquidity preference, the investment-saving equilibrium, and fiscal multiplier effects using authentic macroeconomic datasets, national accounts identity decomposition, and dynamic simulation frameworks. The laboratories prioritize hands-on model construction over abstract theoretical exposition, grounding every exercise in real-world data from national statistical offices, central bank archives, and international financial institutions.

Keynesian economics emerged from John Maynard Keynes's *General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936) as a direct challenge to classical equilibrium assumptions. Where classical theory predicted automatic market clearing through flexible prices and wages, Keynes identified persistent unemployment equilibria arising from deficient aggregate demand, animal spirits, and liquidity traps. The Keynesian framework shifted macroeconomic policy focus from long-run supply-side neutrality to short-run demand management through fiscal intervention and monetary accommodation. These laboratories operationalize the mechanical core of Keynesian theory: the consumption function, the marginal efficiency of capital, the liquidity preference theory of interest rates, and the fiscal multiplier process.

Your analytical environment requires a spreadsheet application with statistical regression capabilities (Excel, Google Sheets, LibreOffice Calc), access to quarterly national accounts data from the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) or equivalent OECD statistical database, and a scripting environment for time-series econometric analysis (Python with pandas, statsmodels, and matplotlib, or R with tidyverse and forecast packages). Download the National Income and Product Accounts (NIPA) Table 1.1.5 (Gross Domestic Product) and Table 2.1 (Personal Income and Its Disposition) covering at minimum the period 1990Q1 through 2024Q4. You will decompose GDP into consumption (C), investment (I), government purchases (G), and net exports (NX), then construct empirical consumption functions, estimate marginal propensities, and simulate fiscal multiplier transmission under varying structural parameters.

The operational diagnostic instruments include regression residual analysis for consumption function specification tests, autocorrelation function (ACF) plots to detect dynamic lag structures in investment response, impulse response functions (IRFs) to trace fiscal shock propagation through the multiplier process, and structural break tests (Chow test, CUSUM statistics) to identify regime shifts in macroeconomic behavioral relationships. Each exercise terminates with a formal diagnostic checklist validating model stationarity, coefficient stability, and out-of-sample forecast accuracy.

## Exercise 1: Foundational Practical Implementation

This exercise constructs the empirical Keynesian consumption function and verifies the stability of the marginal propensity to consume (MPC) across business cycle phases. Keynes posited that aggregate consumption depends primarily on current disposable income, with the MPC lying strictly between zero and unity. The consumption function forms the foundational block of the Keynesian cross diagram and determines the magnitude of the fiscal multiplier. You will estimate the linear consumption function $C_t = \alpha + \beta Y_t^d + \epsilon_t$, where $C_t$ represents real personal consumption expenditures, $Y_t^d$ denotes real disposable personal income, $\alpha$ captures autonomous consumption, $\beta$ measures the MPC, and $\epsilon_t$ represents the stochastic disturbance term.

Begin by acquiring quarterly real personal consumption expenditures (NIPA Table 1.1.6, line 2) and real disposable personal income (NIPA Table 2.1, line 26, deflated by the PCE price index from Table 1.1.4). Load the time series into your spreadsheet with dates in column A, real consumption in column B, and real disposable income in column C. Calculate the year-over-year growth rates to inspect cyclical volatility: create a new column for $\Delta C / C_{t-4}$ and $\Delta Y^d / Y^d_{t-4}$. Construct a scatter plot with disposable income on the horizontal axis and consumption on the vertical axis. The cloud of points should reveal a strong positive linear relationship, confirming the Keynesian consumption hypothesis at the aggregate level.

Execute ordinary least squares (OLS) regression of consumption on disposable income using your spreadsheet's built-in regression tool or a scripting environment. In Excel, navigate to Data > Data Analysis > Regression, set the Y range to consumption and X range to disposable income, and request residual output. Record the estimated intercept $\hat{\alpha}$, slope coefficient $\hat{\beta}$, standard errors, t-statistics, R-squared, and Durbin-Watson statistic. A well-specified consumption function for U.S. data typically yields an MPC between 0.85 and 0.95, an R-squared above 0.98, and a Durbin-Watson statistic near 2.0 indicating minimal autocorrelation. If your Durbin-Watson statistic falls below 1.5, serially correlated residuals suggest dynamic misspecification requiring lagged consumption terms or error correction mechanisms.

Plot the regression residuals against time to inspect for structural breaks. Major economic regime shifts—such as the 2008 financial crisis or the 2020 pandemic—often induce shifts in the consumption function due to precautionary saving behavior and credit market disruptions. Implement a Chow test for structural stability by splitting the sample at 2008Q4, estimating separate regressions for pre-crisis (1990Q1-2008Q3) and post-crisis (2009Q1-2024Q4) periods, and computing the F-statistic for coefficient equality. The test statistic is $F = \frac{(SSR_r - SSR_u)/k}{SSR_u/(n-2k)}$, where $SSR_r$ denotes the restricted (pooled) sum of squared residuals, $SSR_u$ represents the unrestricted (separate) sum of squared residuals, $k$ counts parameters (2 for intercept and slope), and $n$ measures total observations. If the F-statistic exceeds the critical value at the 5% significance level, reject the null hypothesis of parameter stability and estimate regime-specific consumption functions.

Calculate the implied Keynesian fiscal multiplier from your estimated MPC. The simple closed-economy multiplier formula is $\kappa = \frac{1}{1 - \beta}$, where $\beta$ represents the MPC. With an MPC of 0.90, the multiplier equals 10, implying that a one-dollar increase in government purchases raises equilibrium GDP by ten dollars through successive rounds of induced consumption. This mechanical amplification lies at the heart of Keynesian countercyclical fiscal policy. However, the simple multiplier abstracts from taxation, imports, and induced interest rate changes. Extend the formula to incorporate a marginal tax rate $t$ and marginal propensity to import $m$: $\kappa = \frac{1}{1 - \beta(1-t) + m}$. For the U.S. economy with $\beta = 0.90$, $t = 0.25$, and $m = 0.15$, the multiplier contracts to approximately 1.67, reflecting leakages through taxation and import demand.

Construct a dynamic simulation of the Keynesian multiplier process using your spreadsheet. Initialize a column for time periods (0 through 20), a column for the fiscal shock (set to 100 in period 0, zero thereafter), and columns for the cumulative change in consumption, investment (held constant at zero for simplicity), government purchases (the initial shock), and GDP. In each period $t \geq 1$, calculate the induced consumption change as $\Delta C_t = \beta \cdot \Delta Y_{t-1}$, where $\Delta Y_{t-1}$ represents the prior period's GDP change. Sum the components to obtain $\Delta Y_t = \Delta C_t + \Delta I_t + \Delta G_t$. Populate the table iteratively and plot the cumulative GDP change against time. The curve should asymptote toward the theoretical multiplier value, with 90% of the total effect realized within the first ten periods for typical MPC values.

Diagnostic verification proceeds through residual analysis and out-of-sample forecast testing. Calculate the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) for your consumption function by withholding the final eight quarters as a test set, re-estimating the regression on the training sample, generating forecasts for the test period using actual disposable income, and computing $\text{MAPE} = \frac{100}{h} \sum_{t=1}^h \left| \frac{C_t - \hat{C}_t}{C_t} \right|$, where $h = 8$ denotes the test horizon. A well-calibrated consumption function achieves MAPE below 2% for one-year-ahead forecasts. If forecast errors exceed this threshold, investigate omitted variable bias from wealth effects (include household net worth from Federal Reserve Z.1 Financial Accounts), credit constraints (include consumer credit outstanding), or precautionary saving (include unemployment rate or economic policy uncertainty index).

## Exercise 2: Parameter Variation & Empirical Analysis

This exercise operationalizes the Keynesian investment function and liquidity preference theory of interest rate determination. Investment represents the most volatile component of aggregate demand, driven by expectations of future profitability (Keynes's "animal spirits") and the cost of capital determined by the interest rate. The marginal efficiency of capital (MEC) schedule relates the expected return on investment projects to the quantity of capital demanded, while liquidity preference determines the interest rate as the price equilibrating money supply and money demand. You will estimate the interest-elastic investment function, construct the LM curve from money market equilibrium, and simulate the IS-LM model's comparative statics under monetary and fiscal policy shocks.

Acquire quarterly real private nonresidential fixed investment (NIPA Table 1.1.6, line 9) and the effective federal funds rate (FRED series FEDFUNDS, averaged quarterly). Load the series into your spreadsheet alongside real GDP. Compute the investment-to-GDP ratio $I_t / Y_t$ to normalize for scale effects and the real interest rate $r_t = i_t - \pi_t^e$, where $i_t$ denotes the nominal federal funds rate and $\pi_t^e$ represents expected inflation approximated by the four-quarter trailing average of PCE inflation. Keynesian theory predicts an inverse relationship between investment and the real interest rate, with the semi-elasticity measuring the percentage point change in the investment ratio per one-percentage-point increase in the real rate.

Estimate the log-linear investment function $\ln(I_t/Y_t) = \alpha + \beta r_t + \gamma \ln(Y_t) + \epsilon_t$. The coefficient $\beta$ captures the interest semi-elasticity of investment (expected negative), while $\gamma$ measures the accelerator effect of output on investment demand. Execute OLS regression and record the estimated parameters. Empirical studies of U.S. investment typically find interest semi-elasticities between -0.5 and -1.5, implying that a one-percentage-point increase in the real interest rate reduces the investment-to-GDP ratio by 50 to 150 basis points. The accelerator coefficient $\gamma$ generally lies between 0.5 and 1.0, reflecting procyclical investment behavior.

Test for nonlinearity in the investment-interest rate relationship by adding a quadratic term $r_t^2$ to the regression. Financial frictions and credit rationing can generate convexity in the investment function, with interest rate sensitivity increasing at higher rate levels. Compare the adjusted R-squared and Akaike information criterion (AIC) between the linear and quadratic specifications. If the quadratic term carries a statistically significant coefficient (t-statistic above 2 in absolute value) and reduces AIC by at least 2 units, adopt the nonlinear specification for subsequent simulations.

Construct the IS curve from goods market equilibrium by solving the national income identity $Y = C(Y) + I(r) + G + NX$ for the relationship between output $Y$ and the real interest rate $r$. Substitute your estimated consumption function $C = \alpha_C + \beta Y$ and investment function $I = \alpha_I + \beta_I r + \gamma_I Y$ into the identity, hold government purchases and net exports constant at their sample means, and rearrange to obtain $Y = \frac{\alpha_C + \alpha_I + \bar{G} + \overline{NX}}{1 - \beta - \gamma_I} + \frac{\beta_I}{1 - \beta - \gamma_I} r$. The IS curve slope in $(Y, r)$ space equals $\frac{dr}{dY} = \frac{1 - \beta - \gamma_I}{\beta_I}$. With typical parameter values ($\beta = 0.90$, $\gamma_I = 0.5$, $\beta_I = -1.0$), the IS curve exhibits a negative slope of approximately 0.4, indicating that a one-percentage-point increase in the real interest rate reduces equilibrium GDP by 2.5%.

Derive the LM curve from money market equilibrium $M^s / P = L(Y, r)$, where $M^s$ denotes nominal money supply (use M2 from FRED series M2SL), $P$ represents the price level (GDP deflator), and $L(\cdot)$ denotes real money demand. Keynes's liquidity preference theory posits that money demand increases with transaction volume (captured by output $Y$) and decreases with the opportunity cost of holding money (the nominal interest rate $i \approx r + \pi^e$). Estimate the log-linear money demand function $\ln(M_t/P_t) = \alpha + \beta_Y \ln(Y_t) + \beta_r r_t + \epsilon_t$ using quarterly data. The income elasticity $\beta_Y$ typically ranges from 0.5 to 1.0, while the interest semi-elasticity $\beta_r$ lies between -5 and -10, reflecting high sensitivity of money demand to interest rates in modern financial systems with liquid asset substitutes.

Invert the money demand function to derive the LM curve: $r = \frac{\alpha}{\beta_r} + \frac{1}{\beta_r} \ln(M^s/P) - \frac{\beta_Y}{\beta_r} \ln(Y)$. The LM curve slope in $(Y, r)$ space equals $\frac{dr}{dY} = -\frac{\beta_Y}{\beta_r} \cdot \frac{1}{Y}$. With $\beta_Y = 0.8$ and $\beta_r = -8$, the LM curve exhibits a positive slope of approximately 0.1 at the sample mean output level, indicating that a 1% increase in GDP raises the equilibrium real interest rate by 10 basis points due to increased transaction demand for money.

Simulate the IS-LM model equilibrium by solving the system of two equations in two unknowns $(Y, r)$. Create a spreadsheet grid with output $Y$ ranging from 90% to 110% of baseline GDP in 1% increments (column A) and calculate the implied interest rates from the IS curve (column B) and LM curve (column C) for each output level. Plot both curves on the same graph with output on the horizontal axis and the interest rate on the vertical axis. The intersection identifies the model's unique equilibrium $(Y^*, r^*)$. Verify algebraically by equating the IS and LM expressions and solving for $Y^* = \frac{(\alpha_C + \alpha_I + \bar{G} + \overline{NX})(1-\beta-\gamma_I)^{-1} - \beta_I \beta_r^{-1}[\alpha + \ln(M^s/P)]}{1 + \beta_I \beta_Y / [\beta_r (1-\beta-\gamma_I)]}$.

Conduct comparative statics analysis of fiscal policy by increasing government purchases $\bar{G}$ by 100 billion dollars (approximately 0.4% of U.S. GDP). Recalculate the IS curve with the higher government spending level and plot the shifted curve alongside the original. The IS curve shifts horizontally rightward by the fiscal multiplier magnitude $\Delta Y / \Delta G = \frac{1}{1 - \beta - \gamma_I}$. With baseline parameters, a 100 billion dollar spending increase shifts the IS curve right by approximately 250 billion dollars. However, the new equilibrium output increase falls short of the full multiplier due to the induced interest rate rise along the upward-sloping LM curve, which crowds out interest-sensitive investment. Calculate the equilibrium output change by solving the new IS-LM system and compare to the simple multiplier prediction. The crowding-out effect typically reduces the realized multiplier by 30% to 50% in calibrated IS-LM models with empirical interest elasticities.

Simulate monetary policy by increasing the money supply $M^s$ by 5%. Recalculate the LM curve with the expanded money supply and plot the shifted curve. The LM curve shifts downward (rightward in output-interest space) by $\Delta r = \frac{1}{\beta_r} \cdot 0.05$. With $\beta_r = -8$, a 5% money supply increase lowers the equilibrium interest rate by approximately 62.5 basis points before accounting for induced output effects. Solve the new IS-LM equilibrium and record the output and interest rate changes. Monetary expansion stimulates output through lower interest rates that boost investment, with the magnitude depending on the investment interest elasticity $\beta_I$ and the LM curve slope. Compare the output effectiveness of fiscal versus monetary policy by computing the ratio $\frac{\Delta Y / \Delta G}{\Delta Y / \Delta M}$ at your estimated parameters.

Investigate the liquidity trap scenario by imposing a zero lower bound (ZLB) constraint on the nominal interest rate. Set the real interest rate floor at $r_{\min} = -\pi^e$, reflecting the fact that nominal rates cannot fall below zero. Simulate a severe recessionary shock by reducing autonomous consumption $\alpha_C$ by 200 billion dollars, shifting the IS curve leftward. If the new IS-LM equilibrium implies $r^* < r_{\min}$, the economy enters a liquidity trap where conventional monetary policy loses traction. Construct a graph showing the IS curve intersecting the horizontal LM segment at the ZLB. In this regime, the fiscal multiplier attains its maximum value $\frac{1}{1-\beta-\gamma_I}$ because interest rates do not rise to crowd out investment. Quantify the liquidity trap multiplier by calculating the horizontal distance between the initial and shocked IS curves at $r = r_{\min}$. The 2008-2015 U.S. zero-interest-rate period and 1990s-2010s Japanese experience provide historical validation of liquidity trap dynamics.

## Exercise 3: Stress Testing, Boundary Failure & Recovery Protocol

This exercise stress-tests the Keynesian model under structural parameter uncertainty, expectational regime shifts, and supply-side constraints. While Keynesian theory excels at explaining demand-driven fluctuations in the short run, it confronts empirical challenges during stagflation episodes (simultaneous high inflation and unemployment), supply shocks (oil price spikes, pandemic production disruptions), and expectational instabilities (Lucas critique). You will implement econometric specification tests for structural stability, simulate the model under adverse parameter scenarios, and diagnose boundary failures where Keynesian policy prescriptions generate counterproductive outcomes.

Begin with recursive estimation to detect time-varying parameters in the consumption function. Estimate the regression $C_t = \alpha + \beta Y_t^d + \epsilon_t$ using an expanding window: start with 1990Q1-2000Q4 as the initial estimation period, generate one-quarter-ahead forecasts for 2001Q1, expand the sample to 1990Q1-2001Q1, forecast 2001Q2, and continue through 2024Q4. Store the sequence of estimated MPC coefficients $\{\hat{\beta}_t\}$ and construct a time-series plot. Parameter stability manifests as a tight band around the full-sample estimate, while structural breaks appear as persistent level shifts. The 2008 financial crisis typically induces a discrete downward shift in the MPC due to deleveraging and precautionary saving, followed by partial recovery post-2015 as household balance sheets repair.

Quantify parameter uncertainty by constructing confidence intervals for the fiscal multiplier. Bootstrap the consumption function regression by resampling residuals 1,000 times, re-estimating $\hat{\beta}$ for each bootstrap sample, and calculating the implied multiplier $\kappa = \frac{1}{1 - \hat{\beta}(1-t) + m}$ with baseline values $t = 0.25$ and $m = 0.15$. Compute the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap multiplier distribution to obtain a 95% confidence interval. A wide confidence interval (e.g., 1.2 to 2.4) indicates substantial uncertainty in fiscal policy effectiveness, complicating real-time policymaking. If the lower bound falls below unity, the data cannot rule out the possibility that fiscal stimulus generates less than one-for-one output increases due to large confidence regions around behavioral parameters.

Test for expectational regime shifts using the Chow forecast test. Estimate the IS-LM model through 2019Q4 (pre-pandemic sample) and generate dynamic forecasts for 2020Q1-2024Q4 using actual values of exogenous variables (government purchases, money supply). Compare forecasted output to realized GDP and compute the forecast error sequence. Calculate the Chow forecast F-statistic: $F = \frac{(SSE_f - SSE_r)/h}{SSE_r/(T-k)}$, where $SSE_f$ denotes the sum of squared forecast errors, $SSE_r$ represents the in-sample sum of squared residuals, $h = 20$ counts forecast periods, $T$ measures training sample size, and $k$ equals the number of estimated parameters. Critical values at the 5% significance level typically lie near 2.0 for this configuration. The pandemic's unprecedented fiscal transfers, monetary accommodation, and supply chain disruptions almost certainly reject forecast stability, invalidating pre-pandemic parameter estimates for post-2020 policy analysis.

Simulate an adverse supply shock by combining an exogenous oil price increase with wage-price spiral dynamics. Augment the IS-LM framework with a Phillips curve linking inflation to the output gap: $\pi_t - \pi_t^e = \phi (Y_t - Y_t^*) / Y_t^*$, where $\pi_t^e$ denotes expected inflation, $Y_t^*$ represents potential output, and $\phi$ measures the slope of the Phillips curve (calibrate to $\phi = 0.5$). Incorporate adaptive expectations $\pi_t^e = \pi_{t-1}$ to generate inflation inertia. Initialize the model at the baseline IS-LM equilibrium with 2% inflation. In period 1, impose a 50% oil price shock that shifts the aggregate supply curve, reducing potential output $Y^*$ by 2% and raising the inflation rate exogenously by 3 percentage points (stagflation shock). Solve the augmented IS-LM-Phillips system dynamically for periods 1 through 20, allowing inflation expectations to adjust and feeding back into real interest rates $r_t = i_t - \pi_t^e$.

The simulation reveals the Keynesian policy dilemma during stagflation: expansionary fiscal or monetary policy to combat recession exacerbates inflation through the Phillips curve, while contractionary policy to reduce inflation deepens the output contraction. Plot the time paths of output (relative to potential), inflation, and the real interest rate. Quantify the policy tradeoff by computing the sacrifice ratio: the cumulative output loss per percentage point reduction in inflation. If policymakers maintain constant nominal interest rates (passive monetary policy), inflation spirals upward as real rates fall, eventually violating the Taylor principle $\frac{\partial i}{\partial \pi} > 1$ required for determinacy. If instead the central bank aggressively raises nominal rates to stabilize inflation (active monetary policy), output collapses through the investment channel, producing a severe recession. The 1970s stagflation episode and the post-pandemic inflation surge provide historical validation of this adverse scenario.

Implement boundary failure diagnostics for the consumption function. Estimate the regression using first-differenced data $\Delta C_t = \beta \Delta Y_t^d + \epsilon_t$ rather than levels to eliminate spurious regression concerns from nonstationary time series. Compare the differenced MPC estimate $\hat{\beta}_{\Delta}$ to the levels estimate $\hat{\beta}$. Substantial divergence (e.g., $\hat{\beta}_{\Delta} < 0.7$ while $\hat{\beta} > 0.9$) indicates that the levels regression captures a spurious cointegration relationship rather than a structural behavioral parameter. Conduct the Engle-Granger cointegration test by regressing consumption on disposable income in levels, extracting residuals $\hat{\epsilon}_t$, and testing for a unit root in the residuals using the augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test. Reject the null hypothesis of no cointegration if the ADF test statistic exceeds the critical value (approximately -3.5 at 5% significance for this specification). Failure to reject indicates that consumption and income do not share a stable long-run equilibrium relationship, invalidating the Keynesian consumption function.

Diagnose investment function instability during financial crises by incorporating credit spread variables. Augment the baseline investment regression with the BAA-AAA corporate bond spread (FRED series BAA minus AAA), a proxy for financial market stress and borrowing constraints: $\ln(I_t/Y_t) = \alpha + \beta_r r_t + \beta_s \text{Spread}_t + \gamma \ln(Y_t) + \epsilon_t$. Estimate the expanded specification and test whether the spread coefficient $\beta_s$ is statistically significant (expected negative sign). During the 2008 financial crisis, the BAA-AAA spread spiked from 100 basis points to over 350 basis points, inducing a credit crunch that depressed investment beyond what the federal funds rate alone would predict. Calculate the contribution of the spread shock to the 2008-2009 investment collapse by computing $\hat{\beta}_s \cdot \Delta \text{Spread}_{2008Q3 \to 2009Q1}$. If the spread effect explains more than 50% of the investment decline, the conventional Keynesian interest rate channel provides an incomplete account of transmission during financial crises, necessitating credit market frictions (Bernanke-Gertler financial accelerator) or balance sheet constraints (Kiyotaki-Moore).

Conduct a recovery protocol simulation by designing a coordinated fiscal-monetary stimulus package. Initialize the model in a deep recession with output 8% below potential, inflation at 0.5% (near-deflation), and the nominal interest rate at the ZLB. Implement a three-year fiscal expansion increasing government purchases by 2% of GDP annually, combined with forward guidance committing the central bank to hold nominal rates at zero until inflation exceeds 2% for four consecutive quarters. Solve the augmented IS-LM-Phillips system dynamically, verifying that the output gap closes by year three and inflation stabilizes at the 2% target. Calculate the cumulative fiscal cost (sum of discounted deficit increases) and the peak public debt-to-GDP ratio. Compare this coordinated strategy to an alternative scenario where fiscal and monetary authorities act sequentially rather than simultaneously, with monetary policy tightening prematurely once output begins recovering. The sequential strategy typically generates slower recovery and higher sacrifice ratios due to policy conflict.

Validate your Keynesian model predictions against actual historical episodes. Select the 2008-2009 Great Recession or the 2020 pandemic recession as a case study. Extract realized values of exogenous variables (government purchases, money supply, oil prices, credit spreads) for each quarter of the episode and use them as inputs to your calibrated IS-LM-Phillips model. Generate dynamic forecasts for output, inflation, and interest rates, beginning from the quarter immediately preceding the shock. Compute forecast error statistics (RMSE, MAE) and construct fan charts showing the 50% and 90% confidence intervals around the point forecast. Keynesian models typically predict output declines and inflation drops during demand-driven recessions with reasonable accuracy (RMSE below 1.5 percentage points for output gaps) but systematically underpredict inflation during supply-driven episodes (2021-2022 post-pandemic inflation surge). Document the error patterns and infer which structural channels (demand deficiency vs. supply disruption vs. expectational shifts) dominate the episode.

## Operational Reference Guide & Diagnostic Checklist

This reference section consolidates parameter calibration benchmarks, econometric specification tests, and diagnostic thresholds for operational verification of Keynesian macroeconomic models. Refer to this guide when interpreting regression output, evaluating model fit, or troubleshooting anomalous simulation results.

**Consumption Function Diagnostics:** The empirical Keynesian consumption function $C = \alpha + \beta Y^d + \epsilon$ should satisfy: (1) MPC $\beta$ between 0.85 and 0.95 for aggregate U.S. data, (2) R-squared above 0.97 indicating tight fit, (3) Durbin-Watson statistic between 1.5 and 2.5 indicating minimal serial correlation, (4) positive intercept $\alpha$ representing autonomous consumption, (5) residuals exhibiting approximate normality (Jarque-Bera test p-value above 0.05), (6) no evidence of heteroskedasticity (White test p-value above 0.05). Violations signal misspecification: low R-squared suggests omitted wealth effects or credit constraints; extreme Durbin-Watson values indicate dynamic misspecification requiring lagged dependent variables or error correction terms; negative intercept implies specification error (consider log transformation).

**Investment Function Diagnostics:** The interest-elastic investment function $\ln(I/Y) = \alpha + \beta_r r + \gamma \ln(Y) + \epsilon$ should satisfy: (1) interest semi-elasticity $\beta_r$ between -1.5 and -0.5, (2) accelerator coefficient $\gamma$ between 0.4 and 1.0, (3) R-squared above 0.60 (investment exhibits higher idiosyncratic volatility than consumption), (4) statistically significant coefficients (t-statistics above 2 in absolute value), (5) residuals uncorrelated with credit spread shocks during crisis periods. Common failures: insignificant interest rate coefficient indicates weak monetary transmission (investigate credit market frictions or measurement error in real rate proxies); negative accelerator coefficient violates theory (check data alignment or consider nonlinear specifications); large unexplained residuals during financial crises require augmentation with credit spread or uncertainty variables.

**Money Demand Function Diagnostics:** The liquidity preference specification $\ln(M/P) = \alpha + \beta_Y \ln(Y) + \beta_r r + \epsilon$ should satisfy: (1) income elasticity $\beta_Y$ between 0.5 and 1.0, (2) interest semi-elasticity $\beta_r$ between -10 and -5, (3) cointegration between real money balances, output, and interest rates (reject unit root in regression residuals), (4) stable parameters across monetary policy regimes (test for break at Volcker disinflation 1979-1982 or Greenspan era 1987-2006). Diagnostic failures: income elasticity above unity suggests money demand outpacing GDP growth (investigate financial innovation or shift to electronic payments); interest elasticity near zero indicates liquidity trap or return dominance breakdown; structural breaks post-1980 reflect financial deregulation and money market fund introduction invalidating simple M2 aggregates (consider Divisia monetary indices).

**IS-LM Equilibrium Verification:** The solved equilibrium $(Y^*, r^*)$ should satisfy: (1) output within 15% of empirical GDP (model captures level), (2) real interest rate within 200 basis points of actual effective rate (federal funds rate minus trailing inflation), (3) stable equilibrium (IS curve steeper than LM curve in $(r,Y)$ space, equivalent to $\frac{dr}{dY}\big|_{IS} > \frac{dr}{dY}\big|_{LM}$), (4) fiscal multiplier between 1.2 and 2.0 (crowding-out reduces simple multiplier), (5) monetary policy effectiveness positive (money supply increase raises output). Equilibrium failures: multiple solutions or instability indicates misspecified slopes (re-estimate behavioral functions or impose theoretically consistent parameter restrictions); equilibrium real rate negative in normal times suggests measurement error in inflation expectations (use survey-based measures like University of Michigan or SPF forecasts); zero fiscal multiplier implies extreme crowding-out (verify LM curve not vertical, check investment interest elasticity).

**Phillips Curve Diagnostics:** The expectations-augmented Phillips curve $\pi - \pi^e = \phi (Y - Y^*)/Y^*$ should satisfy: (1) slope $\phi$ between 0.3 and 0.7 (flatter post-1990s due to anchored expectations), (2) expectations coefficient near unity under adaptive expectations or survey-consistent measures, (3) residuals uncorrelated with supply shock proxies (oil prices, import prices, productivity shocks), (4) parameter stability across inflation regimes (test for break at Volcker disinflation). Common failures: flat or insignificant slope in recent data reflects anchored inflation expectations and globalization (adjust potential output estimate or use nonlinear specifications); inflation expectations lagging actual inflation by more than four quarters violates adaptive expectations (adopt forward-looking rational expectations or survey measures); large residuals during oil shocks indicate omitted supply-side terms (add oil price growth or import price inflation).

**Structural Stability Tests:** Implement Chow tests at known break dates: 1979Q3 (Volcker appointment), 2001Q1 (dot-com recession), 2008Q3 (Lehman bankruptcy), 2020Q1 (pandemic). Reject stability if F-statistic exceeds 5% critical value (approximately 3.0 for typical sample sizes). Implement CUSUM or CUSUM-of-squares tests for unknown break dates, plotting cumulative recursive residuals against 5% confidence bands; boundary violations indicate instability. Use rolling-window estimation (20-quarter windows) to visualize time-varying parameters; confidence intervals should overlap across adjacent windows under stability. For cointegration relationships (consumption-income, money demand), implement the Gregory-Hansen test allowing for regime shifts in cointegrating vectors; failure to find cointegration after allowing breaks suggests spurious long-run relationship.

**Out-of-Sample Forecast Validation:** Reserve final 8 quarters (2 years) as test set. Train models on remaining data, generate forecasts, and compute: (1) RMSE for output forecasts (target: below 1.5 percentage points for quarterly growth rates), (2) MAE for inflation forecasts (target: below 0.5 percentage points for year-over-year rates), (3) directional accuracy for interest rate movements (target: above 70% correct directional predictions). Compare to naive benchmarks: random walk for output growth, trailing average for inflation, no-change for interest rates. A well-specified Keynesian model should beat naive benchmarks by at least 20% in RMSE terms. Forecast failure modes: systematic over-prediction of output during recessions (insufficient crisis transmission), under-prediction of inflation during supply shocks (missing cost-push channels), premature prediction of interest rate tightening (misspecified policy reaction function or expectational errors).

**Policy Counterfactual Validation:** For historical policy episodes, construct counterfactuals by replacing actual fiscal or monetary variables with alternative paths. Example: simulate 2008-2009 recession under no-stimulus counterfactual by holding government purchases constant at 2007Q4 level. Compare counterfactual output path to actual, attributing the difference to fiscal policy. Validate attribution by comparing to independent estimates (CBO fiscal multiplier reports, Federal Reserve staff projections). Discrepancies above 1% of GDP suggest model misspecification. Alternative validation: compare implied policy multipliers to micro-founded estimates from natural experiments (military spending shocks, state-level tax changes); multipliers differing by more than factor of two indicate structural weaknesses (missing heterogeneity, aggregation bias, expectational effects).

**Keynesian Model Boundary Conditions:** The model loses predictive validity under: (1) supply-constrained expansions where output exceeds potential (inflationary boom, Phillips curve dominates), (2) expectational regime shifts (Lucas critique scenarios, central bank credibility shocks), (3) financial crises with binding credit constraints (investment unresponsive to interest rates alone), (4) open-economy capital flows dominating (Mundell-Fleming trilemma, exchange rate pegs collapse). Diagnostic signals for boundary violations: negative realized multipliers (contractionary expansions from expectational effects), inflation accelerating despite output gaps (supply shocks or expectations unanchoring), investment collapsing despite falling interest rates (credit crunch), trade balance swings explaining more variance than domestic demand. When boundary conditions arise, augment the model with relevant mechanisms (credit frictions, rational expectations, open-economy trade equations) or defer to alternative frameworks (New Keynesian DSGE for expectational stability, financial accelerator models for credit crunches).

**Calibration Crosswalk for Alternative Economies:** The baseline parameter values target U.S. data. For other advanced economies: European Union typically exhibits higher MPC (0.92-0.96) due to stronger automatic stabilizers and lower wealth effects, lower investment interest elasticity (-0.3 to -0.7) due to bank-based finance, flatter Phillips curve (slope 0.2-0.4) due to labor market rigidities. Japan shows near-zero interest elasticities during ZLB periods (1995-2015), income elasticity of money demand near unity (cash-intensive transactions), and negative equilibrium real rates (secular stagnation). Emerging markets display lower MPC (0.70-0.85) due to liquidity constraints, higher interest elasticities (-1.5 to -2.5) due to shallow capital markets, steeper Phillips curves (0.6-1.0) due to weaker nominal anchoring. Adjust parameters to match institutional context and re-estimate structural relationships using local national accounts data.
