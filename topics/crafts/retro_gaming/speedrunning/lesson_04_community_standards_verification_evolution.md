# Lesson 4: Community Standards, Verification Systems, Category Evolution, Competitive Meta

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Speedrunning, at its competitive core, is not merely a performance discipline — it is a distributed epistemic project. Every time a runner posts a time, the community faces a fundamental problem of verification: how do you trust a claim made by a single person, on hardware you cannot inspect, running software that may or may not behave identically to a canonical reference? This question has no clean engineering answer, only progressively refined social and procedural frameworks that approximate certainty without ever fully achieving it. Understanding speedrunning as a craft means understanding that its governance infrastructure is as technically demanding as the execution itself.

The foundational invariant of competitive speedrunning is reproducibility. A legitimate record must be, in principle, achievable again by another runner following the same route under equivalent conditions. This seems obvious until you confront the edge cases: a glitch that only triggers on a cartridge manufactured in a specific factory week, an RNG manipulation that depends on sub-frame timing only achievable on NTSC hardware, a cycle-count exploit that disappears under emulation. The reproducibility standard forces categories to define their scope precisely, because "equivalent conditions" is not a universal concept. It is a category-specific contract.

The second invariant is verifiability. A run must produce evidence sufficient for a community of informed peers to assess its legitimacy. What counts as sufficient evidence has evolved dramatically across the history of the craft. In the early 2010s, a Twitch VOD with audio was often enough. By the mid-2020s, top-tier categories on Speedrun.com routinely demand timer-visible footage, console output verification, and in some cases real-time hardware monitoring via capture cards that frame-lock to the display signal. This escalation is not paranoia — it is the rational response to a history of fraud, tool-assisted submissions disguised as legitimate play, and genuine disputes over what hardware configurations preserve the game's intended behavior.

The third invariant is category coherence. Every speedrunning category makes an implicit philosophical claim about what aspect of a game is worth measuring. Any% says: get to the credits as fast as possible, by any means the ruleset permits. 100% says: achieve a community-defined threshold of completeness before the timer stops. Low% says: minimize a specific resource acquisition metric while still completing the game. These are not neutral framings. Each one selects for a different skill profile, rewards a different type of routing intelligence, and surfaces different technical depths of the game's engine. Category coherence means that the ruleset, the timing method, and the allowed techniques must all serve the same measurement thesis without internal contradiction.

Taken together, these three invariants — reproducibility, verifiability, and category coherence — form the constitutional logic of every leaderboard that earns sustained competitive respect. When a leaderboard drifts from any of these invariants, the community notices, trust erodes, and either the platform corrects course or runners migrate to community-maintained alternatives. The history of Speedrun.com, TASVideos, and game-specific Discord-based leaderboards is largely a history of communities negotiating these invariants against practical constraints of moderation capacity, platform features, and competitive pressure.

What makes this intellectually fascinating from a practitioner's standpoint is that the problem is never fully solved. New strategies reveal ambiguities in rules written before anyone knew the strategy existed. New hardware variants (the GBA SP's backlit screen, the Sharp Twin Famicom's slightly different clock rate, the NTSC-J versus NTSC-U timing differences on the NES) force categories to revisit hardware clauses that were written assuming a homogeneous platform. New runners join with different priors about what constitutes fair play, and their expectations must be absorbed into community consensus. Speedrunning governance is not a solved system. It is a living protocol under constant collaborative revision.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

### Leaderboard Platforms: Structural Design and Operational Logic

Speedrun.com is the dominant public leaderboard infrastructure for most retro games, and understanding its data model is essential for anyone working as a moderator, category manager, or competitive analyst. The platform organizes runs hierarchically: a game entry contains one or more categories, each category contains subcategory variables (such as "major glitches" vs. "glitchless" or "any console" vs. "original hardware"), and each run submission is a tuple of (player identity, time, platform, proof link, moderator decision, timestamp). The platform exposes this data through a public REST API, which allows community tools to aggregate splits, generate statistical leaderboard visualizations, and feed third-party tracking systems.

The moderation queue is the operational chokepoint of this system. Moderators — typically appointed from the established runner community — review submitted proof, check times against timing rules, and either accept, reject, or request additional evidence. For high-traffic games, this queue can back up significantly during record-surge periods, which creates the first major failure mode: stale verification. A run sitting unverified for three weeks is a run that generates community uncertainty, particularly if the runner is active on stream and the community has already seen the performance. Best-practice moderation teams assign primary and backup moderators by timezone, establish SLA targets (typically 48–72 hours for routine submissions), and use the platform's notification system to trigger reviews automatically.

The game-specific Discord server is architecturally parallel to and often more authoritative than the Speedrun.com entry for established communities. Discord hosts the living rulebook: pinned documents in `#resources` or `#rules` channels that predate and postdate any given Speedrun.com rule text. When a routing discovery happens, the Discord thread where it was first demonstrated, analyzed, and named is the primary historical record. The Speedrun.com rule update that follows is secondary documentation. Any analyst trying to reconstruct the history of a category's evolution must work from Discord archives, Twitch VOD timestamps, and forum threads, not just the leaderboard's current ruleset.

The timing infrastructure is a separate layer. LiveSplit is the near-universal split timer for PC-based timing, and its `.lss` split file format is an XML document containing split names, segment best times, and comparison data. The community-maintained LiveSplit.org component ecosystem extends the base timer with game-specific auto-splitters — Lua or C# scripts that read memory addresses from an emulator process or a connected console capture and trigger splits automatically. An auto-splitter for a Super Nintendo game running on SNES9x, for example, reads the game's RAM at addresses corresponding to room transitions, boss death flags, or inventory states, and fires a split event when the target value changes. Writing and maintaining these scripts is skilled technical work that falls squarely in the domain of the advanced practitioner.

```
LiveSplit Data Flow (SNES Example)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  [Game ROM on SNES9x]
        │
        │  Memory-mapped RAM exposed via
        │  SNES9x Named Pipes / Shared Memory
        ▼
  [Auto-Splitter Script (Lua/C#)]
        │  Polls address 0x7E0000 + offset
        │  at ~60 Hz refresh cadence
        │  Compares byte value to split trigger
        ▼
  [LiveSplit Component API]
        │  Fires Split(), StartTimer(), Reset()
        │  events on condition match
        ▼
  [LiveSplit UI]
        │  Renders segment time, delta,
        │  sum of best, pace comparison
        ▼
  [Capture Card OBS Scene]
        Composite: game feed + timer overlay
        → VOD / Submission proof
```

For console runners, the hardware chain is more physically grounded. The canonical setup pairs a region-matched console (e.g., an NTSC-U NES for North American category records) with a capture card feeding into OBS on a sidecar laptop. The timer runs on the laptop display or a secondary monitor, never on the game feed itself, to avoid any perception that the timer could be manipulated independently of gameplay. High-precision categories sometimes use a hardware-synced timer (the original Koop timer concept, now largely superseded by software solutions) to eliminate the inter-frame ambiguity introduced by software timing latency. For games where the winning margin between world records is measured in a handful of frames, this matters.

### Proof Standards and the Evidence Hierarchy

The evidence hierarchy in competitive speedrunning forms a rough pyramid. At the base are the minimum-viable submissions that most categories accept for mid-table times: a Twitch VOD or YouTube video showing continuous gameplay from start to finish, with the timer visible. Moving up the pyramid, top-10 submissions in highly competitive categories typically require a minimum resolution (720p or better), a stable frame rate on the recording, and clear audio that lets a verifier hear game audio cues that correlate with timing events. At the apex, world record and near-WR submissions in the most scrutinized categories often require all of the above plus a secondary camera angle showing the console, the hands, and the television screen simultaneously. This "controller cam + game cam" setup has become a de facto standard for NES and SNES world records following several high-profile fraud incidents that exploited single-angle footage limitations.

Frame counting is the metrology layer beneath all of this. When a run's time is contested or when a record is submitted at a margin of one or two seconds, moderators and analysts pull the VOD into a frame-advance tool. VirtualDub, Avisynth pipelines, or simply VLC with the frame advance keybind (`E` key) allow frame-by-frame inspection against the timing start and stop events defined in the category ruleset. For games where timing begins on the first frame of player control after a title screen input, the verifier must identify that exact frame in the video, which requires knowing what the visual state of the game looks like on that frame. This is not a task a non-runner can perform reliably, which is why moderation teams are drawn from the competitive pool rather than general volunteers.

The TASVideos platform occupies a distinct position in this architecture. Tool-assisted speedruns are submitted as re-encodable input files (`.bk2` for BizHawk, `.fm2` for FCEUX), not video recordings, and are verified by playing back the input file through the same emulator version and verifying the resulting output frame by frame against the submitted encode. This produces a cryptographically stronger proof model than video-only verification, though it comes with its own attack surface: TAS input files have historically been manipulated through emulator version exploits, desync tricks, and undisclosed ROM patches. TASVideos responds to this with a robust emulator certification process and mandatory emulator-version tagging on all submissions.

### Category Ruleset Architecture

A well-designed category ruleset is a formal specification document, not a list of suggestions. It must answer six questions unambiguously: What constitutes the start of the timer? What constitutes the end of the timer? What hardware and software configurations are permitted? What techniques are explicitly allowed? What techniques are explicitly banned? How are disputes adjudicated when a submission falls in an ambiguous case?

The timing definition is the most technically demanding clause to write correctly. Consider the difference between Real Time Attack (RTA) and In-Game Time (IGT). RTA measures elapsed wall-clock time from the moment the runner initiates the run to the moment the game's winning condition is met, with no pauses. IGT reads a timer value maintained by the game's own code, which may exclude load screens, cutscenes, or other non-interactive segments. The choice between RTA and IGT is not cosmetic. It changes the competitive landscape entirely: a game with long load screens on cartridge but fast loads on emulator will have an emulator-advantaged RTA leaderboard and a hardware-neutral IGT leaderboard. Both are valid competitive frameworks, but they measure different things, and running both in parallel creates dual communities with fundamentally different incentive structures.

The hardware clause is where the most contentious ruleset debates occur in retro gaming specifically. For NES categories, the question of whether the original 72-pin cartridge connector's electrical characteristics affect RNG seeding has been debated seriously and productively. For Game Boy categories, the difference between original DMG, GBC, GBA, and GBA SP hardware is not cosmetic: the CPU speed differs between models, the display scanning behavior differs, and some glitches are model-specific. For SNES categories, the 1CHIP versus 2CHIP board revisions produce measurably different output at the analog video stage, and certain graphical glitches that runners exploit trigger differently depending on revision. These are not edge cases. They are central to competitive fairness, and a category ruleset that fails to address them produces persistent disputes.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

### Routing as Collaborative Research

The most important optimization workflow in competitive speedrunning is not execution refinement — it is routing discovery. The world record holder in any mature category typically runs a route that dozens of contributors built incrementally over years, and their personal contribution is execution quality on a highly optimized line, not the route itself. Understanding this is fundamental to working effectively in a speedrunning community as an analyst or contributor. The person who finds the new route is often not the person who first executes it at world-record pace.

Routing research proceeds through a layered stack of tools. At the bottom of the stack is RAM watching in an emulator debugger. BizHawk's Lua console and FCEUX's memory debugger expose the game's working RAM in real time, allowing a researcher to watch how memory addresses change during gameplay. The core technique is to form a hypothesis about what a given address represents — for example, "this two-byte value at 0x0300 seems to track the player's X-position" — then verify it by moving the character and confirming the correlation. Once the researcher has a working memory map, they can identify values that gate progression (door unlock flags, inventory item bits, boss death states) and begin asking: is there any sequence of inputs that sets these flags out of the intended order?

This out-of-order flag manipulation is the engine behind most major speedrunning discoveries. The Legend of Zelda's inventory screen on NES stores item data in a memory region that is adjacent to room-state flags. Manipulating the inventory screen at precise moments causes neighboring bytes to receive unintended writes, which allows rooms to be flagged as visited or items to be flagged as collected without the player completing the intended trigger. Discovering this requires systematic RAM watching, hypothesis testing, and documentation — a research workflow more akin to reverse engineering than to gaming in the traditional sense.

The community amplification layer above individual research is the Discord/Twitch collaborative session. A researcher who finds a promising memory behavior will often stream their investigation live, share the BizHawk Lua script they are using for real-time display, and invite the community to generate input sequences while they watch the RAM. This parallel exploration model accelerates discovery significantly. It also creates attribution challenges: when five people are simultaneously suggesting inputs in a live chat and one of them triggers a novel glitch, the discovery credit is legitimately distributed, and community norms around credit attribution have evolved accordingly. Most established communities now follow a "first documented demonstration" standard, with contributor acknowledgment for anyone whose suggestion directly led to the discovery.

### RNG Manipulation and Frame-Perfect Routing

Random Number Generator manipulation is one of the most technically demanding optimization domains in retro game speedrunning, and it rewards a deep understanding of how late-1980s and early-1990s game developers implemented randomness under severe memory constraints. The NES CPU has no hardware random number generator. Games implement pseudorandom sequences through Linear Feedback Shift Registers (LFSRs), simple increment counters that read from free-running timers, or lookup tables indexed by frame count. The key insight for the speedrunner is that these generators are deterministic from a known initial state, and many retro games seed their RNG from a value that the player can influence: the frame on which a button is pressed during the title screen, the number of frames spent on the character select screen, or the sub-pixel position of the player character at a specific room transition.

Manipulating this initial seed to produce a favorable RNG sequence throughout the run requires the runner to execute a specific input at a specific frame before any randomness is consumed. For Mega Man 2, the classic NES speedrun, the opening input timing determines the pattern of enemy behavior and item drop sequences across the entire run. A runner who seeds the RNG correctly enters Bubble Man's stage knowing that the first gap enemy will drop an energy capsule on the second cycle rather than the first, and they route their energy management strategy accordingly. This is not luck. It is applied knowledge of the game's internal state machine, expressed as a memorized input sequence executed to frame precision at the start of the run.

The failure mode of RNG manipulation is desync: a run where the runner successfully executes the seed timing but an upstream perturbation (a dropped frame in the console's video output sync, a physical vibration that caused the controller's d-pad to register a spurious input, an unexpected lag frame from a cartridge contact issue) shifts the RNG sequence by one or more cycles. The resulting run looks normal to a spectator but produces subtly wrong enemy behavior and forces the runner to either adapt or reset. Experienced runners develop a mental checklist of "RNG checkpoints" — specific in-game moments where the visible enemy behavior or item state confirms that the RNG sequence is on track. Failing a checkpoint triggers a decision: attempt recovery manipulation (possible in some games, impossible in others) or reset immediately before sunk-cost psychology takes over.

### Category Evolution: The Lifecycle of a Competitive Meta

A speedrunning category passes through identifiable phases from initial discovery to mature competitive meta, and understanding these phases helps practitioners know how to contribute value at each stage.

The discovery phase begins when someone finishes the game faster than any previous public attempt and either streams it live or posts a video. At this stage, the route is often incoherent by later standards: the runner completes the game using intuitive strategies that avoid obvious time waste but misses optimizations that require knowledge of the game's engine behavior. The discovery phase is characterized by rapid, large-margin improvements where new runners enter and find obvious routing inefficiencies. A run that set a record in January might be beaten by five minutes in March, not because anyone ran faster, but because someone found an earlier boss exit or realized that a cutscene is skippable with a specific input.

The consolidation phase begins when the major routing decisions are locked and improvements shift from route-finding to execution quality. At this point the community writes its first serious ruleset document, establishes a leaderboard, and begins making hardware/emulator policy decisions. The consolidation phase is where the most important governance work happens: the community must decide what the category is measuring before the competitive field is large enough for disputes to become politically charged. Categories that fail to do this governance work in the consolidation phase often fracture later, spawning parallel "glitched" and "glitchless" leaderboards or hardware-separated divisions that compete for community attention.

The mature meta phase is characterized by diminishing marginal improvements where world records fall by seconds rather than minutes, and the competitive knowledge base is so deep that new runners face a substantial onboarding burden. In this phase the community's primary optimization work shifts from routing to micro-optimization: individual room strategies, movement subpixel management, menu navigation frame counts, and lag reduction on specific console configurations. The Super Mario Bros. NES category exemplifies this phase at an extreme: the game has been run competitively for over a decade, the world record has been shaved to within roughly one second of a theoretical maximum based on lag frame counts, and meaningful improvement requires a detailed command of internal engine timing that takes years to acquire.

The re-discovery phase, which not all categories enter, occurs when a new technique fundamentally invalidates the existing meta. This can be a newly discovered memory corruption glitch that skips a substantial portion of the game, a novel RNG manipulation that removes the most significant luck-dependent segment, or the discovery that a previously ignored hardware configuration produces a measurable timing advantage. Re-discovery events are the most community-stressful moments in a speedgame's history, because they simultaneously invalidate years of competitive development and open the game to new contributors who may not share the community's established norms. The transition from the pre-wrong-warp to the post-wrong-warp meta in The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time is the canonical example, though retro platform games have their own smaller-scale equivalents in virtually every major title.

### Moderation Failure Modes and Structural Mitigations

The most common moderation failure mode in established categories is decision inconsistency: different moderators applying different standards to submissions that are functionally equivalent. This arises from under-specified rulesets, moderator turnover that disrupts institutional knowledge, and the inherent subjectivity of "sufficient evidence" thresholds. The mitigation is a written verification standard document, maintained alongside the ruleset, that specifies exactly what a moderator must check for each submission tier. For a top-10 submission, the document should specify: minimum video resolution, timer visibility requirement, required continuous footage duration, proof of hardware configuration if relevant, and the frame-counting procedure for time verification. This document does not eliminate judgment calls, but it narrows the judgment surface to cases that genuinely require deliberation.

The second major failure mode is moderator capture, where the moderation team becomes dominated by runners who have a competitive interest in verification decisions and use their moderation authority to advantage their own submissions or disadvantage rivals. This is not always deliberate. It manifests as unconscious leniency toward friends' submissions and unconscious strictness toward competitors'. The structural mitigation is a recusal norm: a moderator who has an active submission in a category should not be the approving moderator for that run, and a moderator who is in direct competition for a record should recuse from verifying any submission within a predefined time gap of their own record. TASVideos implements a formal judge system that separates submission authors from the verification chain by policy, a model that competitive RTA communities have been slow to adopt but which increasingly appears in the governance documents of mature game-specific leaderboards.

The third failure mode is technical obsolescence, where the leaderboard's platform infrastructure falls behind the community's proof requirements. Speedrun.com's submission system does not natively support frame count submission, secondary camera angle hosting, or emulator version tagging at the granularity that top-tier competitive categories require. Communities respond by building auxiliary verification infrastructure: Google Sheets-based leaderboards with custom columns for hardware revision and emulator version, Discord bots that tag submissions with metadata parsed from the video description, and community-maintained wikis that document the verification decision history for contested records. These parallel systems are not redundancies. They carry the informational weight that the primary platform cannot, and a practitioner who is serious about contributing to or competing in a mature speedrunning community needs to be fluent in all of them simultaneously.

The practitioner who internalizes the architecture described in this lesson — the platform data model, the proof evidence hierarchy, the category lifecycle, the RNG research workflow, and the governance failure modes — is equipped not merely to compete, but to contribute to the living system that makes competition meaningful. The game is the substrate. The community is the institution. Both require the same rigorous attention to detail, and mastery of the craft demands fluency in both.
