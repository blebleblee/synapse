# Lesson 3: Execution Techniques, Muscle Memory Development, Consistency Protocols

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

There is a persistent myth in speedrunning communities that elite runners are simply faster than everyone else — that their fingers move with superhuman speed, processing visual information and translating it into inputs at a rate ordinary players cannot match. The reality is more interesting and far more instructive. What separates a world-record holder from a skilled enthusiast is not raw reaction time, which varies surprisingly little among adults with gaming experience, but rather the architecture of their decision-making. Expert runners have compressed entire chains of conditional logic into single motor patterns. They do not decide and then act. They have made the decision so many times in practice that it no longer occupies conscious attention at all.

This is the central invariant of speedrun execution: **the goal is to remove decisions from the runtime path.** Every moment a runner must consciously evaluate what to do next is a moment of latency inserted into the execution chain. Cognitive latency is far more expensive than the frame-level latency imposed by hardware, which we will address in detail later. A runner who hesitates for even 200 milliseconds at a routing decision point has introduced the equivalent of twelve frames of delay on a 60Hz system — a cost that dwarfs almost any hardware optimization. The entire structure of a mature practice regimen is therefore an exercise in converting conscious decisions into automatic motor programs.

The psychomotor science underlying this is well-established. The brain stores frequently rehearsed motor sequences in a chunked form within the basal ganglia and cerebellum, structures that operate below the level of conscious prefrontal attention. When a pianist plays a scale, they are not selecting each note; the entire sequence is retrieved as a single motor chunk. Speedrunners exploit this same neurological architecture. A Super Mario Bros. runner executing the 1-1 flagpole clip does not think "hold right, jump at this pixel, adjust with the sub-pixel correction." After sufficient repetition the entire sequence is stored and retrieved atomically. The practical implication for a runner designing a practice regimen is that chunk formation requires not just repetition but *correct* repetition under conditions that approximate the stress of a live run. This is a critical distinction, and one to which we will return when examining consistency protocols.

Retro game systems impose a set of physical constraints that have no analogue in modern software development. The 6502-family processors driving the NES, Atari 2600, and Apple II ran at clock speeds between roughly 1 and 3 MHz, with video output tightly synchronized to a 60Hz (NTSC) or 50Hz (PAL) refresh signal. Gameplay logic, physics, and input sampling all ran inside this vertical blank cycle. The consequence is that **inputs are discrete, not continuous** — the game samples your controller state at a fixed point in each frame, typically at the beginning of the main game loop. Any input registered between samples is invisible to the game logic, unless the system implements a secondary polling mechanism. The runner who understands this does not think in terms of "fast" versus "slow" button presses; they think in terms of *frame windows*, asking which frame within a cycle must carry a given input, and how wide that window is for a given trick.

This frame-discrete model is the bedrock on which every other technical concept in this lesson rests. Input buffering, execution hierarchies, difficulty tiers, and consistency metrics all derive their meaning from the structure of the 60Hz (or 50Hz) sampling loop. Before a runner can make intelligent decisions about which strategies to run in competition, they must have an intuitive and not merely theoretical feel for what a single frame means in physical time. One frame at 60Hz is 16.67 milliseconds. The human average for simple reaction time is approximately 200–250ms, or 12–15 frames. No runner reacts to events within a single frame; instead, they learn to anticipate them, pre-loading inputs before the triggering condition arrives. This is the core intuition behind input buffering, and it is the first execution technique a serious practitioner must internalize.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

### The Input Sampling Pipeline

To execute techniques reliably, a runner needs an accurate mental model of exactly how their physical button press becomes a game event. The signal path is longer and more nuanced than it appears. At the physical layer, pressing a button on a first-party NES controller closes a circuit that the controller's 4021 shift register reads. When the console's CPU pulses the LATCH line (once per frame, synchronized with the VBlank interrupt), the shift register captures the state of all eight buttons simultaneously. The CPU then clocks out each bit in sequence using the CLOCK line. This entire read operation completes in approximately 12 microseconds — well under one frame — and the resulting button state byte is stored in RAM, typically around address $4016/$4017, where game logic can read it throughout the rest of the frame.

The practical upshot is that your physical input must be established *before* the LATCH pulse fires. If you press a button one microsecond after the latch, your input will not register until the following frame. The game is therefore not "reading" your controller in real time; it is reading a snapshot that was taken at the frame boundary. This is why experienced runners speak of "buffering" inputs — they mean pressing a button earlier than the moment of intended effect, ensuring the input is captured in the next available snapshot.

```
Physical Button Press
        │
        ▼
  Controller Shift Register (4021)
        │
        │  (LATCH pulse, ~16.67ms interval)
        ▼
  NES CPU reads $4016/$4017
        │
        ▼
  Game RAM: Button State Byte (8 bits)
        │
        ▼
  Game Logic Loop (runs post-VBlank)
        │
        ▼
  Physics Update → Collision → State Change
        │
        ▼
  PPU Renders Frame to Screen (~1 frame later)
        │
        ▼
  Display (CRT: ~0–1ms, LCD: 10–50ms additional)
```

This pipeline has two important consequences for the runner. First, there is an irreducible one-frame lag between input capture and visual confirmation that the input had its intended effect, because the PPU rendering the result of this frame's logic will not appear on screen until the next VBlank. Second, any additional display latency from an LCD monitor, capture card, or upscaler inserts delay between the visual cue the runner uses to time their next input and the actual game state. A runner playing on original hardware through a CRT experiences roughly 0–1ms of display lag. A runner playing through a cheap LCD capture setup might experience 50–80ms of additional lag, which is 3–5 frames of distortion in the visual feedback loop. This matters enormously for frame-sensitive tricks and is a primary reason the speedrunning community has converged on specific hardware configurations, which we will examine in Section 3.

### Input Buffering: Mechanics and Taxonomy

Input buffering is not a single technique but a family of related practices, and distinguishing between them precisely is important for designing effective practice regimens. The first type, **pre-input buffering**, involves pressing a button before the game state that will be affected by it has fully initialized. The classic example is the "buffered jump" at a spawn point: a runner presses and holds the jump button while a cutscene or screen transition is still playing, so that the first actionable frame of gameplay immediately executes the jump without requiring a reactive input. Many NES and SNES games read the controller during transition frames even when the player has no apparent agency; a held input during these frames will be captured on the first post-transition LATCH cycle, giving the player an effective zero-frame reaction time.

The second type is **window-extension buffering**, relevant when a game provides a grace period around an intended input frame. Certain games check not just the current frame's button state but whether a button was pressed within the last N frames, where N is a small integer typically between 2 and 8. This is common in fighting games and action RPGs designed for accessibility, but it also appears unexpectedly in platformers. When it exists, it effectively widens the execution window for a technique, transforming a strict 1-frame window into a 4- or 8-frame one. Identifying whether a game uses window-extension for a given mechanic is an early analytical step any serious runner should complete, since it fundamentally changes the difficulty classification of a trick.

The third and most demanding type is **frame-perfect reactive input**, where no buffering exists and the runner must physically press the button within a 1-frame (16.67ms) window in response to a visual or audio cue. True frame-perfect inputs are rare in finished commercial games, since most QA processes filter out mechanics requiring that level of precision. When they appear — the original Super Mario Bros. 1-1 small Mario flagpole clip requires a jump input on a specific sub-pixel frame — they are significant barriers to route adoption, and the community's response to them is instructive. Runners typically develop *audio cue synchronization*, identifying a sound event that occurs a fixed number of frames before the required input window and using that sound rather than a visual event as their timing trigger. This is a powerful technique because audio processing in the brain is faster than visual processing by approximately 30–40ms, and because audio cues are often more precisely defined within the game's internal state than the visual cues that accompany them.

### Execution Hierarchies and Trick Classification

A well-constructed speedrun route is not simply a list of time-saving strategies; it is a portfolio of techniques organized by risk and reward. Advanced runners and route analysts use an execution hierarchy to classify tricks along two dimensions: **time save** and **consistency cost**. Time save is straightforward: how many seconds does this trick gain over the safe alternative, measured against a reference time. Consistency cost is more nuanced: it describes how much run-terminating failure risk the trick introduces, expressed as a function of both the inherent difficulty of the trick and the runner's current skill level with it.

A trick with a large time save and low consistency cost is a mandatory route inclusion — skipping it would be irrational. A trick with a small time save and high consistency cost is a liability: its expected time contribution to a run, calculated as (success rate × time save) − (failure rate × time lost to reset), is negative unless the runner's skill with the trick is exceptionally high. The critical insight is that **expected value of a trick is runner-specific and time-varying**. A technique that is net-negative for a runner at 70% consistency may become net-positive at 90%. This means practice decisions must be governed by consistency targets rather than by aesthetic goals. A runner who practices a trick because it looks impressive is making a poor investment; a runner who practices a trick because their current 72% consistency will become net-positive for the route at 85% is managing their training like an engineer.

The standard classification tiers used informally across speedrunning communities map closely onto this framework. "Safe" or "low risk" strategies are those executable at better than 90% consistency under run conditions. "Moderate risk" strategies fall between 70% and 90% and are typically included in routes where the time save exceeds two seconds. "High risk" or "frame-perfect" strategies below 70% are generally routed out in favor of safer alternatives unless the time save is so large that the expected value remains positive even at that failure rate. Any route decision that ignores this calculation is emotionally rather than analytically motivated.

### The Physics of Muscle Memory Formation

Muscle memory is a colloquial term for a neurological process with well-characterized stages. In the *cognitive stage*, the learner consciously attends to every element of the movement, corrects errors explicitly, and performs the task slowly and with high variability. In the *associative stage*, the movement becomes smoother as error-correction begins to happen within the motor system rather than through conscious attention. In the *autonomous stage*, the movement executes automatically with minimal conscious oversight, freeing attention for higher-level tasks like state monitoring and routing decisions.

The transition from cognitive to autonomous typically requires between 300 and 500 quality repetitions for a complex motor sequence. "Quality" here is non-trivial: repetitions performed in a low-stress context, at a pace slower than the target speed, or with an incorrect technique are not necessarily accumulating toward autonomous execution and may in fact reinforce incorrect motor patterns. This is why elite runners structure their practice around *execution-speed repetition* rather than *slow-motion drilling*. A technique that requires a 1-frame window must be practiced at game speed, not slower, because the motor program that fires at 16ms intervals is structurally different from the one that fires at 100ms intervals. The cerebellum learns the specific timing of the trained sequence; changing the tempo requires re-learning.

This principle has a direct implication for how runners should use emulators and tools in their practice. TAS (Tool-Assisted Speedrun) tools like FCEUX's frame advance mode are invaluable for *understanding* what must be done on each frame, but they are counterproductive for *practicing* the motor execution of the technique. A runner who practices a trick exclusively in frame-advance mode and then attempts it at real speed is practicing the wrong motor program. The correct workflow is to use frame advance for analysis — establishing exactly which frame an input must land on and what the visual/audio state looks like at that moment — and then immediately transfer that knowledge into full-speed repetition sets.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

### Hardware Configuration for Minimal-Latency Execution

The hardware environment is not merely a background consideration; it is a first-order variable in execution consistency. A runner optimizing their technique on an LCD with 50ms of display lag and then attempting the same technique at a tournament running through original CRTs will experience their timing as systematically wrong, because the visual feedback loop they trained on no longer matches the feedback loop in front of them. Every serious competitor should characterize the display latency of their practice setup and ensure it matches their competition environment as closely as possible.

For runners on original hardware, the standard practice is a consumer CRT television from the 1990s, which introduces 0–1ms of display lag. The most commonly used models in the NTSC North American community are the Sony Trinitron PVM series (PVM-14M2U, PVM-20M2U) for their precise RGB input and low-noise image, and the more accessible Sony KV-series consumer Trinitrons for runners who cannot source broadcast monitors. PAL runners face the additional consideration that most retro systems output a 50Hz signal on PAL hardware, which affects both game speed (most NES games run roughly 17% slower on PAL) and the frame window calculations for every trick in the game.

For runners on emulators or digital setups, the tool of choice for latency measurement is a lag test cart — a cartridge or ROM that flashes the screen white on the frame an input is registered, allowing the runner to photograph the controller and display simultaneously with a high-speed camera and measure the frame offset. This is not an exotic procedure; lag test ROMs exist for every major retro platform and are freely available. A runner who has not performed this measurement on their practice setup does not actually know their hardware environment and cannot make informed decisions about technique timing.

Controller hardware introduces its own latency and mechanical considerations. The original NES dogbone controller and rectangular controller both use membrane contacts, which have a tactile response that some runners find inconsistent. A common modification is replacing the membrane pads with discrete tactile switches (Omron B3F series are popular for their 0.98N actuation force and sharp tactile break), which provides more consistent actuation depth and a more defined tactile confirmation. The electrical timing difference between a worn membrane and a new tactile switch is negligible — both close the circuit faster than 1ms — but the mechanical consistency of the actuation point matters for trained motor patterns. A runner whose muscle memory is calibrated to a specific actuation depth will experience increased error rates when the controller feel changes.

### Practice Regimens: Structure and Progression

A complete practice regimen for a speedrun operates at three distinct time scales: the single-session micro-structure, the weekly meso-cycle, and the multi-week macro-progression. Neglecting any one of these levels produces runners who plateau — either burning out from monotonous isolated grinding, failing to maintain consistency in areas they have stopped drilling, or never building the run-level mental stamina needed to execute difficult tricks late in a tense run.

At the micro level, a productive session for an advanced practitioner running a 20–45 minute game typically follows a structure of approximately 90 minutes. The first 15–20 minutes are dedicated to technique isolation: selecting the two or three tricks that have the highest consistency cost relative to their route importance and running them in an isolated save state context, targeting 15–20 consecutive attempts per trick. The purpose is not to improve these tricks dramatically in a single session but to maintain and incrementally improve the neural pathways for them. The next 50–60 minutes are full-run attempts, which serve a function distinct from isolated practice: they train run-level pacing, decision-making under accumulated stress, and the execution of late-game tricks after earlier portions of the run have taxed the runner's attention. The final 10–15 minutes should be a brief review session, using in-game timer comparisons or split software (LiveSplit configured with the appropriate game and category definitions) to identify which segments are losing time to execution errors versus which are limited by route ceiling.

At the meso level, a productive weekly structure for a runner targeting a PB within 5% of world record differentiates between high-intensity and maintenance days. Two or three days per week focus on the tricks with the highest consistency cost — the techniques where failure probability exceeds 20%. On these days, the runner may spend as much as 30–40% of session time on isolation drills. Two days per week are dedicated to full-run attempts with less isolation drilling, training run mentality and stamina. One day per week is a deliberate review day: watching back VODs of recent runs, using LiveSplit's history to identify statistically poor-performing splits, and cross-referencing those splits with technique errors noted during the runs. Rest is non-optional. Motor consolidation occurs during sleep, and runners who attempt to maximize improvement by eliminating rest days routinely experience plateaus and increased error rates.

### Error Recovery: The Undervalued Discipline

A complete runner is not simply a runner who executes correctly; they are a runner who minimizes time loss when execution fails. Error recovery is frequently neglected in practice because runners naturally prefer practicing successful execution over practicing failure, but the ability to recover cleanly from common failure states can save as much time over a full season of running as shaving an additional frame off a clean execution window.

Error recovery training begins with failure taxonomy: cataloguing the most common failure modes for each major trick in the route and identifying the fastest recovery path from each. Recovery paths fall into three categories. The first is **abort-and-continue**: the trick failed in a way that leaves the game state intact and the optimal path forward is simply to skip the trick, accept the time loss, and continue at full execution quality. This path requires that the runner recognize the failure quickly and avoid attempting to salvage the trick in a suboptimal state, which typically costs more time than the clean abort. The second is **partial-salvage recovery**: the failure left the game state in a suboptimal but workable position, and a secondary strategy exists that recovers some but not all of the time saved by the primary trick. Many zip tricks and out-of-bounds exploits in games like Super Metroid and A Link to the Past have secondary landing zones that recover 60–70% of the primary trick's time save. Knowing these landing zones and routing them explicitly is a mark of mature run design. The third is **reset-trigger evaluation**: the failure has put the run so far behind pace that the expected value of continuing is lower than the expected cost of a reset. Runners should pre-commit to explicit reset rules — numerical pace thresholds defined against a reference split file — rather than making this decision emotionally mid-run, where loss aversion reliably distorts judgment.

The psychological dimension of error recovery is inseparable from its technical dimension. Research on choking under pressure consistently shows that skill regression during high-stress performance correlates with a return to cognitive-stage processing — the runner begins consciously attending to movements that should be fully automatic, introducing exactly the kind of internal latency we identified at the outset as the primary execution bottleneck. The countermeasure is not to try to suppress this attention but to redirect it. Experienced runners develop specific refocusing protocols: a fixed behavioral sequence executed after a significant error — exhaling, resetting their grip on the controller to a reference position, and stating the next required action aloud or subvocally. This behavioral sequence functions as an external interrupt that resets attentional state. The specific content of the protocol matters less than its consistency; what it must accomplish is breaking the error-rumination loop and reinitializing the runner's attention on forward execution.

### Consistency Measurement and the Plateau Problem

Consistency is not a feeling; it is a statistic, and treating it as anything else is a failure of rigor. A runner who describes a trick as "pretty consistent" and a runner who knows their measured success rate is 83% over the last 240 isolated attempts are not equally equipped to make intelligent routing and practice decisions. The infrastructure for precision consistency measurement is minimal: a simple tally of attempts and successes in a spreadsheet, updated after every session's isolation block. From this data, a runner can track the moving success rate over time and identify whether a technique is improving, stable, or degrading.

Degradation is a real and underappreciated phenomenon. A technique that was trained to 92% consistency six months ago may have drifted to 80% if it has not been maintained in regular practice. The motor program was stored as a chunked sequence in the basal ganglia, but motor memory is not perfectly stable. It undergoes consolidation and reconsolidation processes that can alter the stored sequence, particularly when the runner has acquired other techniques with similar but not identical inputs in the interim — a phenomenon analogous to proactive and retroactive interference in declarative memory research. Runners who maintain a split history and notice a specific segment gradually trending slower over weeks are likely experiencing motor drift in one of that segment's key techniques, not a sudden loss of physical dexterity.

The plateau problem — a period where measured performance stops improving despite continued practice — almost invariably signals one of three failure modes in the practice structure. The first is insufficient challenge: the runner is practicing techniques they have already automated at a rate that provides consolidation but no extension of the ceiling. The second is incorrect practice conditions: the runner is performing techniques at a pace, stress level, or hardware configuration that differs from competition conditions, so the trained motor program does not transfer cleanly. The third is technique ceiling: the runner has reached the limits of what the current routing allows, and further improvement requires a route change, a new trick discovery, or a new hardware optimization rather than more of the same practice. Distinguishing between these three requires honest data: split history, technique success rates, and regular comparison with the current state of the world-record progression for the category. A runner who is practicing diligently but has not looked at a world-record comparison in three months may be grinding toward a ceiling the community has already identified and routed around.

The synthesis of everything in this lesson reduces to a single discipline: **treat execution as an engineering problem, not an athletic one.** Athletes train by feel, by volume, by the vague accumulation of experience. Engineers measure, model, test, and iterate against defined criteria. Speedrunning at its highest level demands the engineer's approach. Know the physical latency of your hardware chain. Know the frame window for every trick in your route. Know your measured success rate for each technique. Know the expected value calculation that justifies or excludes each strategy. Build practice sessions that target specific consistency thresholds rather than generic "getting better." And when you fail — which you will — have a prepared behavioral protocol that returns you to execution quality in under three seconds. The runners who internalize this framework do not merely improve faster; they understand *why* they are improving, which is the only kind of knowledge that reliably transfers across games, categories, and competitive contexts.
