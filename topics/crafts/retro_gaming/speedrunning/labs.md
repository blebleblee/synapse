# Operational Verification Labs: Speedrunning

## Objective & Operational Lab Setup

Speedrunning represents the systematic optimization of video game completion through frame-perfect execution, routing efficiency, and exploitation of deterministic game engine behaviors. These operational verification laboratories ground theoretical route planning in hands-on console manipulation, input timing measurement, and empirical failure-mode analysis. The practitioner will construct verifiable speedrun segments across multiple retro platforms, measure execution variance through frame counting apparatus, and develop muscle-memory sequences through deliberate practice protocols grounded in motor learning science.

The laboratory environment requires authentic hardware or cycle-accurate emulation that preserves frame timing integrity. Original console hardware introduces controller latency characteristics and frame-rate inconsistencies that fundamentally alter input windows; a Nintendo Entertainment System executing at 60.0988 Hz exhibits different sub-frame timing than an emulator locked to precisely 60.00 Hz. For NES verification work, acquire an NES-001 front-loading console or top-loading NES-101 variant, paired with original NES-004 or NES-027 controllers exhibiting minimal membrane wear. Controller membrane degradation increases actuation force by 15–40% and introduces bounce artifacts that corrupt frame-perfect inputs.

For cycle-accurate emulation environments, deploy Mesen version 0.9.9 or later for NES titles, offering instruction-level accuracy and input latency simulation. SNES verification demands bsnes version 115 or higher operating in accuracy profile mode, which replicates SA-1 coprocessor timing and mid-scanline HDMA effects critical to Super Metroid door transition behavior. Sega Genesis work requires BlastEm 0.6.2, faithfully reproducing Z80 synchronization and VDP FIFO characteristics that govern Sonic the Hedgehog 2 zip mechanisms.

Frame counting infrastructure forms the metrology foundation of speedrun verification. Deploy OBS Studio version 28 or newer configured to record at the console's native refresh rate—60 fps for NTSC hardware, 50 fps for PAL systems—using lossless x264 encoding at CRF 0 to eliminate temporal compression artifacts. Install Avidemux 2.8 for frame-by-frame navigation and timestamp extraction. Construct a reference spreadsheet in LibreOffice Calc with columns for segment identifier, predicted frame count, measured frame count, delta variance, and cumulative drift. This audit framework transforms subjective execution assessment into quantifiable performance metrics.

Input display overlays provide real-time feedback on controller state, exposing missed inputs and timing drift. For NES hardware capture, wire an Arduino Uno microcontroller with custom firmware to the controller port's data latch and clock lines, sampling button states at 4 kHz and transmitting over serial to a Python script that renders overlay graphics composited into OBS. The microcontroller approach introduces 2–4 ms measurement latency but eliminates software polling jitter. Emulation environments offer built-in input display—enable Mesen's "Input Display" HUD element or bsnes's controller overlay, configuring visual persistence duration to highlight held inputs across multiple frames.

Construct a physical practice station minimizing environmental variables. Mount the CRT monitor or LCD display at eye level 18–24 inches from seating position, ensuring perpendicular viewing angle to eliminate parallax-induced timing error. For CRT workflows, utilize a Sony Trinitron PVM-14M2U or equivalent broadcast monitor exhibiting 10–15 ms total display latency. Modern LCD alternatives demand sub-10 ms gray-to-gray response times; the ASUS VG248QE and BenQ ZOWIE XL2411K meet this threshold. Lighting conditions must remain constant—install daylight-balanced 5000K overhead illumination at 500 lux to prevent pupil dilation variance that affects reaction time by 8–12 ms across lighting extremes.

Temperature and humidity control influence controller membrane responsiveness and human reaction kinetics. Maintain ambient temperature between 20–22°C, the range minimizing finger perspiration while preserving tactile sensitivity. Relative humidity between 40–50% prevents static discharge events that can corrupt controller data lines while avoiding moisture accumulation on contact surfaces. Document baseline environmental conditions in the practice log; temperature deviations beyond ±3°C correlate with measurable execution variance in frame-window success rates.

## Exercise 1: Foundational Practical Implementation

This foundational exercise establishes empirical measurement discipline through construction and execution of a basic speedrun segment in Super Mario Bros. for NES, specifically World 1-1 from power-on to flagpole touch, targeting sub-380-frame completion. The segment isolates fundamental techniques—jump arc optimization, sub-pixel positioning, and frame rule manipulation—while developing the sensorimotor loop coupling visual recognition to button press timing with 50-millisecond precision.

Power on the NES console and observe the boot sequence. The PPU initialization routine consumes exactly 29,658 CPU cycles before the title screen appears, establishing a deterministic starting state. At the title screen, wait for the demo sequence to begin, then press Start on frame 0 of the demo's second iteration to guarantee consistent PRNG seeding. The NES PRNG state at level entry determines enemy spawn positions and power-up emergence timing; this Start press timing standardization eliminates a 3-frame variance window in Goomba #2 position.

The run timer begins on the frame Mario gains control, identifiable when his sprite transitions from the descent animation to the standing state. Immediately hold Right and tap A on frame 1 to initiate the first jump. Mario's horizontal velocity accumulates from 0.09 pixels/frame to the walking maximum of 1.5 pixels/frame over 23 frames; jumping during this acceleration phase produces a shallow arc. Optimal first-jump timing places the apex 8 pixels above the first Goomba's collision box, clearing the enemy while minimizing airtime that would delay ground-acceleration resumption.

Land from the first jump and maintain Right hold to reach running speed of 2.5 pixels/frame after an additional 16 frames of ground acceleration. The B button initiates running when held simultaneously with directional input, but must be pressed no earlier than frame 28 post-control to avoid triggering the fireball throw animation in powered-up states encountered in full-game runs. Execute the second jump at X-position 1648 (sub-pixel 0x80), targeting the brick block cluster at coordinates (1696, 80). This jump must be held for exactly 18 frames—releasing A earlier truncates the arc via negative jump momentum, while holding longer wastes frames at apex.

The underground pipe at X-position 2976 presents the first critical frame window. Descending into the pipe requires Mario's center point to align within an 8-pixel horizontal window while Down is held and his Y-position exceeds 192. Approach the pipe at running speed and tap Down on frame 179 of the segment; earlier input registers before collision detection and is discarded, while later input misses the single-frame window as Mario's velocity carries him past the detection zone. Missing this window costs 1 frame rule—a 21-frame penalty due to NES global timer quantization.

NES frame rules govern level loading and area transitions, quantizing timing into 21-frame (0.35-second) intervals. Actions completing within the same frame rule interval yield identical real-time progression; saving 8 frames in segment execution provides no benefit if both timings fall within the same frame rule window. Construct a frame rule calculator in LibreOffice Calc: in column A, list cumulative frame counts from 0 to 600 in increments of 1. In column B, apply the formula `=FLOOR(A1/21)*21` to compute the frame rule threshold. In column C, calculate delta to next threshold: `=(FLOOR(A1/21)+1)*21-A1`. This visualizes safety margins—a 2-frame buffer to the next threshold versus a 19-frame surplus indicates vastly different optimization priorities.

After successful pipe entry, navigate the underground bonus room collecting 16 coins within 90 frames. Coin collision boxes extend 8 pixels from visual boundaries; Mario's hitbox center must penetrate this zone. Trace an optimal path minimizing direction reversals: enter at right edge of platform, collect rightmost 6 coins while moving left, reverse at left platform edge, sweep back right collecting remaining coins. Each direction reversal consumes 5 frames decelerating from +2.5 to -2.5 pixels/frame through zero-velocity.

Exit the underground area via the rightmost pipe, emerging at X-position 3552 in the overworld. The flagpole stands at X-position 3168—this discrepancy reflects coordinate space transformation between underground and overworld areas, a common source of route-planning error. From pipe emergence, hold Right to build velocity and execute a jump at X-position 3136, aiming to contact the flagpole at Y-position 36, the highest collision point yielding maximum points and optimal animation length.

Flagpole contact timing determines the final frame rule. Touching before frame 374 achieves the sub-380 target and locks in the second frame rule (frames 357–377). Record the run using OBS at 60 fps lossless, then import the video into Avidemix. Navigate frame-by-frame to the control-gain frame and mark it as Point A using the "A" hotkey. Advance to the flagpole contact frame—identified by the score increment appearing on-screen—and mark Point B. The frame counter displays elapsed frames; log this value in the verification spreadsheet alongside predicted frame count of 374.

Execute ten consecutive attempts, logging measured frame counts and success/failure status for the pipe entry window. Calculate mean completion time and standard deviation. A standard deviation exceeding 8 frames indicates inconsistent execution fundamentals—likely variable jump hold durations or inconsistent B-button timing. Review input display recordings for each attempt, isolating the frame of first deviation from the reference input sequence. This frame-of-divergence analysis pinpoints motor control weaknesses requiring targeted practice.

Construct a deliberate practice drill isolating the pipe entry window. Using Mesen's save state feature, create a state at X-position 2880, 96 pixels before the pipe. Practice the approach sequence in 20-attempt blocks, tracking success rate. Motor learning research indicates that 40–60 repetitions per session, distributed across multiple days, establishes robust procedural memory. Screen 0x76 introduces enemy interference. Octoroks spawn at Y-positions 0x4D, 0x8D, and 0xCD (top, middle, bottom lanes) with spawn positions determined by the frame count modulo 256 at screen entry. These enemies exhibit deterministic movement—Octoroks travel at 0.5 pixels/frame, reversing direction every 128 frames or upon collision with screen boundaries. Model Octorok position as a function of time: `Y_position = Y_spawn ± 0.5 * (frame_count % 256)`, sign alternating every 128 frames. At screen 0x76, intentionally collide with an Octorok moving in Link's direction of travel. The damage knockback imparts 8 pixels of displacement in the direction opposite the enemy's facing, while invulnerability allows passage through additional enemies. Calculate damage boost viability: if enemy density requires ≥32 frames of evasive movement, the damage boost saves time despite the half-heart health cost. Full-game routes must budget health expenditure across all planned damage boosts—exhausting hearts triggers a death that costs 180–220 frames (game over animation, continue screen, respawn sequence).

Execute each experimental matrix cell five times, recording frame counts from Start press to Level-1 door contact. Input the data into the spreadsheet and calculate descriptive statistics for each configuration: mean (`=AVERAGE(range)`), standard deviation (`=STDEV.S(range)`), and 95% confidence interval half-width (`=CONFIDENCE.T(0.05, STDEV.S(range), COUNT(range))`). Plot the means with error bars representing confidence intervals using an XY scatter chart with error bars enabled.

Apply a paired t-test to discriminate meaningful differences between route variants. In LibreOffice Calc, use `=TTEST(array1, array2, 2, 1)` where array1 and array2 contain the frame count samples for two routes, 2 indicates two-tailed test, and 1 specifies paired samples. A p-value below 0.05 indicates statistical significance—the observed difference likely reflects true performance variance rather than random sampling error. Document non-significant differences (p > 0.05) as equivalent routes, guiding route selection toward subjective factors like execution consistency or damage boost risk tolerance.

Construct a decision tree encoding route selection logic based on measured parameters. ## Operational Reference Guide & Diagnostic Checklist

This reference section consolidates measurement protocols, diagnostic procedures, and troubleshooting decision trees for systematic identification and remediation of speedrun execution deficiencies across hardware platforms, input devices, and capture infrastructure.

### Frame Counting and Timing Verification Standards

All timing measurements must reference clearly defined start and end frames with zero ambiguity. For individual-level speedruns, the start frame is the first frame Mario/Link/Samus gains control (identifiable by sprite animation transition from static to player-controlled state). The end frame is the first frame the objective completion trigger fires—flagpole touch in Super Mario Bros., door entry collision in The Legend of Zelda, or fade-out initiation in Super Metroid. Full-game run timing begins at console power-on (first non-black frame displayed) or Start press on title screen, depending on community rules documented at speedrun.com for the specific title.

Construct a master timing sheet in LibreOffice Calc with the following structure: Column A contains segment identifiers (e.g., "SMB 1-1", "SMB 1-2 Pipe Entry", "SMB 1-2 Exit"). Column B lists predicted frame counts derived from tool-assisted speedrun (TAS) analysis or theoretical route planning. Column C records measured frame counts from verification recordings. Column D calculates absolute delta (`=C2-B2`), and Column E calculates cumulative drift (`=SUM(D$2:D2)`). Color-code cells using conditional formatting: green for delta ≤ 5 frames, yellow for 6–15 frames, red for ≥16 frames, providing instant visual feedback on segment execution quality.

Frame rule quantization requires careful accounting. Create a frame rule reference table: Column A lists frame counts 0–1000. Column B calculates frame rule index (`=FLOOR(A1/21,1)` for NES 21-frame rules, adjusting divisor for other systems). Column C shows the ceiling of the current frame rule (`=(FLOOR(A1/21,1)+1)*21`). Column D calculates buffer to next frame rule (`=C1-A1`). When optimizing segments, prioritize improvements on attempts with buffer ≤5 frames; larger buffers indicate the optimization yields no real-time benefit unless sufficient frames are saved to cross the frame rule threshold.

### Input Latency Measurement and Mitigation

Total system latency comprises controller polling latency, emulation/console processing latency, and display latency. Quantify each component independently. For controller polling latency, use a high-speed camera (240 fps minimum) to film simultaneous button press and input display indicator activation. Count frames between thumb contact with button surface and on-screen indicator change; multiply by 4.167 ms (1 frame at 240 fps) to obtain latency. NES controllers exhibit 8–12 ms latency, SNES controllers 10–16 ms, and modern USB adapters add 4–8 ms.

Display latency measurement requires specialized equipment. The Leo Bodnar Input Lag Tester (£75, leogednar.com) generates a photosensor-synchronized video signal, measuring display response time with 0.1 ms precision. For budget verification, use the 240fps.com camera test: film a device with known latency (smartphone displaying a millisecond timer) alongside the target display showing the same timer via HDMI input. Count frame differential and multiply by 4.167 ms. CRT displays exhibit 10–15 ms latency; gaming LCDs range 8–25 ms; standard office LCDs often exceed 40 ms.

Mitigate latency through hardware selection and configuration. For HDMI displays, enable Game Mode to bypass image processing pipelines that introduce 15–30 ms delay. Disable motion smoothing, dynamic contrast, and noise reduction features. On SNES and Genesis, use RGB SCART cables with SCART-to-HDMI converters (RetroTINK-2X Pro, $140) that preserve 240p signals without lag-inducing deinterlacing. For NES, the NESRGB mod kit ($75) bypasses composite video artifacts and enables SCART output.

USB polling rate affects modern controller input. Gaming-focused controllers poll at 1000 Hz (1 ms intervals); standard controllers poll at 125 Hz (8 ms intervals). Verify polling rate using the Device Manager on Windows (Mouse Properties → Hardware → Properties → Details → Polling Rate) or `evhz` utility on Linux. Replace low-polling-rate adapters with 1000 Hz variants such as the Mayflash Magic-NS or 8BitDo Retro Receiver.

### Failure Mode Diagnostics and Remediation

Systematic execution failures cluster into six categories: timing failures (missed frame windows), routing errors (wrong path taken), input errors (incorrect button pressed), state management failures (wrong power-up or health state), environmental factors (controller malfunction, display glitch), and mental errors (concentration lapse, incorrect knowledge). Develop diagnostic protocols for each category.

Timing failures manifest as consistent misses of frame-precise inputs despite correct button selection. Record input display footage of ten consecutive attempts. Use Avidemux to identify the frame of intended input and compare against optimal timing. If deviation shows consistent directionality (e.g., always 2–3 frames early), apply conscious timing offset—delay the input by the measured bias. If deviation exhibits high variance (±5 frames), the visual cue triggering the input is insufficiently precise. Identify an earlier cue or develop a rhythmic input pattern independent of visual feedback.

Routing errors indicate knowledge gaps or attention failures. After each routing error, document the decision point where deviation occurred. If the same routing error recurs across multiple sessions, the route knowledge is insufficiently internalized—create flashcards with screenshots of branch points and correct path overlays. Review flashcards before practice sessions until recall achieves 100% accuracy across 20 consecutive trials.

Input errors (pressing A instead of B, Left instead of Right) suggest motor program encoding failures. These errors spike during high-cognitive-load segments where decision-making competes with motor execution for attentional resources. Isolate high-error segments into dedicated practice drills, executing the segment 30 times per session until error rate drops below 5%. Implement pre-segment verbal cues—subvocalize "A button" immediately before pressing A to strengthen the cognitive-motor linkage.

State management failures occur when segment execution assumes incorrect power-up state or resource count. Construct a run state tracker: a printed checklist with boxes for each key item (Mushroom, Fire Flower, Starman in SMB; Hearts, Bombs, Keys in Zelda). After each segment, physically mark the checklist to maintain external state awareness, reducing dependency on fragile working memory. In emulator-based practice, enable persistent on-screen HUD displays for health, item inventory, and timer.

Environmental factor diagnosis requires component isolation testing. If execution quality degrades suddenly, test controller input integrity using an input test ROM (controller test utilities exist for NES, SNES, Genesis). Verify all buttons register, directional inputs reach full analog range, and no phantom inputs occur. Clean controller contacts with isopropyl alcohol and a microfiber cloth. Replace worn controller membranes—NES membrane replacements cost $8–12, SNES membrane sets $10–15. For wireless controllers, measure battery voltage; voltage below 3.2V introduces input lag spikes.

Mental error mitigation requires metacognitive monitoring. After errors attributable to concentration lapses (e.g., forgetting to press A during a routine jump), rate subjective mental fatigue on a 1–10 scale. Log fatigue ratings alongside error frequency in the practice spreadsheet. If correlation analysis (`=CORREL(fatigue_range, error_range)`) yields r > 0.5, implement mandatory 5-minute breaks every 20 minutes to prevent fatigue accumulation. During breaks, shift visual focus to distant objects (6+ meters) to reduce eye strain.

### Hardware Maintenance Protocols

Console hardware degrades over decades, introducing execution variance. NES 72-pin connectors exhibit contact oxidation causing graphical glitches and inconsistent boot states. Replace the connector with a Blinking Light Win connector ($15) engineered for tighter pin contact and longer lifespan. Clean cartridge contacts with isopropyl alcohol and a cotton swab every 50 hours of operation. Cartridge contact resistance above 0.5 ohms (measured with a multimeter between contact pad and cartridge pin) requires cleaning.

SNES capacitors age, introducing voltage regulation instability that affects APU and CPU timing. If the console exhibits audio distortion or spontaneous resets, measure the +5V rail voltage at the cartridge slot using a multimeter (test between pins 27 and 35 on the cartridge connector). Voltage deviation beyond ±0.25V mandates capacitor replacement—replace all electrolytic capacitors with low-ESR equivalents (parts list and tutorial at console5.com).

Controller membranes harden with age, increasing actuation force from the original 60–80 grams to 120–150 grams. Measure actuation force using a digital gram scale: position the scale probe on the button surface and slowly increase pressure until actuation occurs. Replace membranes exhibiting >100 gram actuation force. For competitive-level play, consider aftermarket tactile switch mods (SNES controller mechanical button mod kits available for $25–35) providing 50-gram actuation with crisp tactile feedback.

CRT monitors require periodic degaussing to eliminate magnetic field accumulation that distorts geometry. Press the built-in degauss button (if present) or power-cycle the monitor—automatic degaussing occurs during power-on. For severe distortion, use an external degaussing wand (Tekdesk Professional Demagnetizer, $35) swept across the screen surface in expanding circular patterns. Degauss every 200 hours of operation or when geometric distortion exceeds 2% (measured by photographing a grid test pattern and calculating pixel displacement).

### Performance Plateau Diagnosis

When improvement stagnates despite consistent practice, systematic diagnosis identifies the bottleneck. Plot personal best times across 30-day rolling windows. If the trend line (calculated via `=TREND(time_range, attempt_number_range)`) shows slope ≈0 for three consecutive 30-day windows, a performance plateau exists. Identify the limiting factor through component analysis: isolate each segment's execution time and compare against theoretical optimal (TAS time + 30 frames human execution buffer). Segments within 50 frames of theoretical optimal are execution-limited; further improvement requires enhanced motor precision. Segments exceeding 80 frames indicate knowledge gaps—better routing or technique exists.
