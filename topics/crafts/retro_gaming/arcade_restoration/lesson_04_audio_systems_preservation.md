# Lesson 4: Audio Systems Preservation

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The audio system in a golden-age arcade cabinet is not a high-fidelity reproduction chain. It never was. The designers of these machines worked under brutal cost constraints, packing sound generation hardware, mixing circuitry, and power amplification onto a single PCB sitting inches away from switching power supplies, fluorescent light ballasts, and unshielded video deflection coils. The audio subsystem was expected to produce recognizable explosions, synthesized music, and voice samples through a four- or eight-inch paper-cone speaker mounted in a wooden or particle-board enclosure that doubled as the cabinet's structural spine. The bar was functional, not transparent. Understanding this original engineering context is the foundation of every restoration decision you will make, because the goal is not to improve upon what the designers intended but to return the system to the operating envelope they originally specified.

The arcade audio chain follows a predictable topology across most manufacturers, though the implementation details vary considerably. A dedicated sound CPU, typically a Z80 or a MOS 6502, reads a program stored in ROM and feeds commands to a sound synthesis chip. That synthesis chip, whether a Yamaha FM generator like the YM2151, a General Instrument AY-3-8910, a Texas Instruments SN76477, or an OKI M6295 ADPCM sample player, produces a raw analog output at a level measured in hundreds of millivolts. This signal enters a mixing network where multiple chip outputs are summed through passive resistor networks, shaped by RC filter stages that roll off high-frequency quantization artifacts, and routed to a volume control element, almost always a logarithmic-taper potentiometer. From there, the signal reaches a power amplifier IC, frequently an LM386, a TDA2003, or a discrete transistor push-pull stage, which drives the cabinet speaker at line-level-isolated power. Every link in this chain is a potential failure point, and the failure modes are not random. They follow the physics of aged components.

The dominant invariant in arcade audio degradation is electrolytic capacitor death. Every coupling capacitor in the signal path, every bypass capacitor on the amplifier supply rail, every filter capacitor in the power supply section uses aluminum electrolytic chemistry. These components have a finite service life measured in thousands of hours at rated temperature, and arcade cabinets ran hot. A capacitor manufactured in 1985 and operated in a cabinet for ten years in an arcade environment has exceeded its design life by a wide margin. The electrolyte dries, the equivalent series resistance rises, the capacitance drops, and the effect on audio is immediate and audible. Coupling capacitors that have lost capacitance introduce a high-pass characteristic that thins out bass response and attenuates low-frequency sound effects. Power supply bypass capacitors that have gone high-ESR allow switching noise and digital hash from the logic board to ride the amplifier supply rail directly into the audio output. This is the single most common cause of the buzzing, hissing, and digital noise bleed that plagues unrestored arcade sound boards.

The second invariant is potentiometer wear. Arcade volume controls were not gentle home-audio controls. They were mounted on circuit boards behind coin doors or inside service panels, adjusted with screwdrivers by operators who treated them roughly, and exposed to decades of ambient humidity and temperature cycling. The carbon resistive element degrades through mechanical abrasion and oxidation, producing scratchy, intermittent, or dead spots in the rotation. The wiper contact loses spring tension. In some cases, the resistive track cracks entirely. Restoration of these components is possible in many cases through chemical cleaning and mechanical reconditioning, but replacement requires matching not just the resistance value but the taper characteristic, which in arcade applications is almost always logarithmic, sometimes marked as "A-taper" on the component body.

The third invariant is ground topology. Arcade cabinets are notorious for ground-loop problems because the JAMMA standard, and many pre-JAMMA wiring schemes, use a shared ground reference for logic, audio, and the cabinet's mechanical components. When the logic ground and audio ground are not properly isolated at the board level, digital switching noise propagates through the shared impedance of the ground path and appears as audible hash in the speaker output. Williams boards from the early 1980s are particularly susceptible because their sound boards were physically separate from the CPU boards, connected by ribbon cables, and powered from supply rails shared with the video deflection circuitry. The restoration technician must understand the ground architecture of each specific board to diagnose noise problems that are not caused by failed components but by wiring and grounding topology.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The signal path from sound generation to speaker output can be traced as a linear chain with well-defined stages, each of which has characteristic failure modes and restoration procedures. Consider the Capcom CPS1 architecture as a representative example, since it appears in dozens of cabinets and its service documentation is thorough. The audio subsystem on a CPS1 board consists of a Z80 sound CPU running at 3.58 MHz, a YM2151 FM synthesis chip for music and effects, an OKI M6295 for ADPCM voice samples, and a TL074 quad JFET-input op-amp for active mixing and filtering. The combined output passes through a 10kΩ logarithmic volume potentiometer mounted on the PCB edge, then into a BA5406 dual-channel power amplifier that drives the speaker at up to 2.5 watts per channel.

```
CAPCOM CPS1 AUDIO SIGNAL PATH
=============================

  Z80 CPU ──(data bus)──→ YM2151 (FM synth) ──(analog out)──→ [MIXING NET]
          │                                                      │
          └──(data bus)──→ OKI M6295 (ADPCM) ──(analog out)──→  [MIXING NET]
                                                                     │
                                                              [RC LP FILTER]
                                                          (cutoff ~12 kHz)
                                                                     │
                                                            [10kΩ LOG POT]
                                                           (volume control)
                                                                     │
                                                        [BA5406 PWR AMP]
                                                        (gain ~40 dB)
                                                                     │
                                                              [SPEAKER]
                                                           (4Ω or 8Ω)

  SUPPLY RAIL: +12V ──[100µF bypass]──→ BA5406 Vcc
  COUPLING:   1µF electrolytic between YM2151 output and mixing network
  COUPLING:   1µF electrolytic between OKI6295 output and mixing network
  COUPLING:   470µF electrolytic between BA5406 output and speaker
```

The mixing network on the CPS1 is a passive summing junction built from discrete resistors. The YM2151 output, which is already a current-to-voltage converted signal from the chip's internal DAC, passes through a 1µF coupling capacitor to strip any DC offset, then through a series resistor of approximately 4.7kΩ into a summing node. The OKI M6295 output takes a parallel path through its own 1µF coupling cap and a series resistor. At the summing node, a small capacitor to ground, typically 1000pF, forms a low-pass filter with the series resistors that rolls off response above approximately 12 kHz, removing the stair-step quantization noise inherent in the 10-bit DACs. This filter is not designed for audiophile quality. It is designed to prevent the aliasing artifacts of the sample-rate converters from reaching the amplifier and producing harsh, metallic distortion that operators would recognize as a defect.

The volume control sits after the mixing and filtering stage but before the power amplifier. This placement is critical because it means the potentiometer operates at line level, not at speaker level. The signal at the wiper is a few hundred millivolts, and the potentiometer forms a voltage divider with the input impedance of the BA5406 amplifier. A 10kΩ logarithmic potentiometer wired as a variable divider, with one end to the signal, the other end to ground, and the wiper to the amplifier input, provides the standard volume control. The logarithmic taper is essential because human hearing response follows an approximately logarithmic curve; a linear potentiometer would cluster all useful volume adjustment in the last quarter of the rotation, making fine control impossible. When replacing this component, a linear-taper pot of the same resistance will physically fit and electrically function, but the volume curve will be wrong, and the operator will perceive the control as broken.

The power amplifier stage is where the most consequential restoration work happens. The BA5406 on the CPS1, the LM386 on older boards like those from Atari and Centuri, and the TDA2003 on many Konami boards all share a common weakness: they are capacitor-coupled on both input and output. The input coupling capacitor blocks DC from the preceding stage from reaching the amplifier's internal bias network. The output coupling capacitor blocks the amplifier's DC bias, typically at half the supply voltage, from reaching the speaker voice coil. When these capacitors fail, the symptoms are distinct and diagnostic. A failed input coupling cap produces a DC offset at the amplifier input that the amplifier dutifully amplifies, resulting in a constant tone or severe distortion at all volume levels. A failed output coupling cap passes DC to the speaker, which produces a noticeable thump at power-on and power-off, can displace the speaker cone from its centered position, and in extreme cases burns the voice coil. Measuring DC voltage across the speaker terminals with the cabinet powered and no audio playing should read near zero; anything above 100 mV indicates a coupling capacitor failure or a shorted amplifier output.

The JAMMA pinout standardizes audio as a differential pair on pins L (positive) and M (negative), but the negative leg is not a true differential signal on most boards. It is simply the audio ground reference, and the speaker is driven single-ended. This means the speaker wiring in the cabinet forms a loop antenna that can pick up electromagnetic interference from the video deflection yoke, the switching power supply, and the fluorescent light fixture. The cabinet wiring from the JAMMA harness to the speaker is typically unshielded, and this is a common source of 60 Hz hum and switching-supply whine. In restoration, replacing this run with shielded two-conductor cable, with the shield grounded at the JAMMA edge connector end only to avoid creating a ground loop, eliminates a class of noise problems that no amount of board-level work will address.

Williams sound boards, common in Defender, Robotron, Joust, and Sinistar cabinets, present a fundamentally different architecture. These boards use a dedicated 6802 or 6808 CPU with its own ROM and RAM, communicating with the main CPU board through a parallel interface. The sound generation is handled by a combination of custom Williams sound chips and discrete analog circuitry, including voltage-controlled oscillators, analog noise generators, and active filter networks built around LM324 or LM2902 quad op-amps. The signal path is longer, with more active stages, and the boards use a higher component count than the integrated Capcom approach. This means more coupling capacitors, more solder joints, and more potential failure points. The Williams sound board also derives its power from the main power supply through a separate connector, and the ground reference is established through the ribbon cable to the CPU board, which makes ground integrity at the ribbon connector a critical diagnostic point.

```

```

The discrete push-pull amplifier used on early Williams boards is a complementary symmetry stage using an NPN transistor (BD139 or similar) and a PNP transistor (BD140 or similar) in a class-AB configuration. The bias network establishes a small quiescent current to prevent crossover distortion, and the output is capacitor-coupled to the speaker. These transistors are rated for 1.5 A collector current and 12.5 W dissipation with a heat sink, but many Williams cabinets ran without adequate heat sinking on these devices, leading to thermal failure. A failed output transistor is often the result of a shorted speaker or a shorted output coupling capacitor, and replacing the transistor without diagnosing the root cause will produce a second failure. When rebuilding a Williams discrete amplifier stage, check the speaker voice coil resistance, verify the output coupling capacitor, and confirm the bias resistor values before installing replacement transistors.

The impedance matching between amplifier output and speaker is less critical in arcade applications than in home audio because the amplifiers are low-power and the speakers are insensitive, but it still matters. A 4Ω speaker driven by an amplifier designed for an 8Ω load will draw twice the current, potentially exceeding the amplifier's output stage current limit and causing thermal shutdown or destruction. Conversely, an 8Ω speaker driven by an amplifier designed for 4Ω will produce less power and lower volume, which may be acceptable but changes the character of the sound. The original speaker impedance is specified in the cabinet service manual and is often printed on the speaker frame magnet. If the original speaker is missing, the replacement must match the original impedance within 10 percent. Wire-wound resistors can be used as a stopgap impedance matching measure, but they waste amplifier power and should be considered a temporary fix, not a permanent repair.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Signal path troubleshooting follows a strict divide-and-conquer methodology that isolates the failure to a specific stage before any component replacement begins. The first instrument in the diagnostic chain is not an oscilloscope but a simple audio probe: a 0.1µF ceramic capacitor in series with a 1N4148 diode and a pair of headphones, connected ground-to-ground and signal-to-test-point. This passive probe draws no current from the circuit, loads the signal path negligibly, and lets you listen to the audio at any point in the chain. Beginning at the sound synthesis chip output and working toward the speaker, you touch the probe to each successive node in the signal path. If you hear clean audio at the chip output but noise or silence at the mixing network output, the fault lies between those two points. If the mixing network is clean but the amplifier output is distorted, the amplifier stage is the culprit. This method, used by arcade service technicians since the 1970s, is faster and more diagnostic than reading waveforms on an oscilloscope for most audio problems, because the human ear is a remarkably sensitive pattern-matching instrument for the artifacts produced by failing analog components.

When the audio probe identifies a stage, the oscilloscope confirms the diagnosis. A Tektronix 2235, a Rigol DS1054Z, or any analog or digital scope with at least 5 MHz bandwidth is more than sufficient for arcade audio work. Probe the input and output of the suspected stage with the scope set to AC coupling, 1V per division vertical, and 1 ms per division horizontal. A coupling capacitor that has lost 80 percent of its capacitance will show a waveform at its input but an attenuated and high-pass-filtered version at its output, with low-frequency content visibly reduced. A shorted coupling capacitor will pass DC, visible as a shifted baseline on the output side. An amplifier IC with internal failure will show a waveform at its input but a flat DC rail or a distorted clipped waveform at its output. The oscilloscope distinguishes between a passive component failure and an active component failure in seconds.

Potentiometer restoration begins with physical inspection. Remove the potentiometer from the board if possible, or at least gain access to its body. Most arcade potentiometers are enclosed types with a slot or opening in the body. The first treatment is Caig DeoxIT D5 contact cleaner, applied through the opening in the body or through the shaft bushing if the body is sealed. Rotate the shaft through its full range ten to twenty times after application to work the cleaner across the entire resistive track. The DeoxIT dissolves oxidation on the carbon element and on the wiper contact, and the mechanical action of rotation physically removes the dissolved contamination. If the potentiometer is still scratchy after this treatment, apply DeoxIT FaderLube, which is specifically formulated for carbon-track potentiometers and leaves a protective film that reduces future oxidation. If the potentiometer is still noisy or has dead spots after both treatments, the carbon element is physically worn through, and the component must be replaced.

Replacement potentiometer selection requires matching three parameters: resistance value, taper, and physical form factor. The resistance value is printed on the side of the body or can be measured with a multimeter across the two end terminals with the wiper disconnected. The taper is indicated by a letter prefix on the component: "A" denotes logarithmic (audio) taper, "B" denotes linear taper, and "C" denotes reverse-logarithmic taper. Arcade volume controls almost universally use A-taper. The physical form factor must match the PCB footprint: the pin spacing, the bushing diameter for panel mounting, and the shaft length and type (knurled, flatted, or round). Common arcade potentiometer values include 10kΩ, 20kΩ, 50kΩ, and 100kΩ, with 10kΩ and 50kΩ being the most prevalent. Panasonic EVH5 and ALPS RK097 series potentiometers are reliable modern replacements that match many arcade PCB footprints, though the bushing and shaft dimensions must be verified against the original before ordering.

Noise isolation requires a systematic approach that addresses each noise source independently. The first source is the switching power supply, which generates broadband noise in the 20 kHz to 1 MHz range through its switching frequency and harmonics. This noise enters the audio chain through the supply rail if the bypass capacitors on the amplifier are degraded. Replace the 100µF or 470µF electrolytic bypass capacitor on the amplifier supply pin with a fresh part, and add a 0.1µF ceramic capacitor in parallel to handle high-frequency bypassing that the electrolytic cannot address due to its parasitic inductance. The electrolytic handles low-frequency ripple; the ceramic handles high-frequency switching noise. Both are necessary.

The second noise source is ground-loop coupling between the logic ground and audio ground. On JAMMA boards, the audio ground is typically connected to the logic ground at a single point on the PCB, often through a small resistor or a ferrite bead. If this connection is corroded or if a service modification has bridged it with solder, the ground isolation is lost. Inspect the PCB for the ground isolation component and verify its integrity with a multimeter continuity check. A 0Ω jumper in place of the intended 10Ω resistor or ferrite bead indicates a previous repair that bypassed the isolation, and restoring the correct component will reduce logic noise coupling into the audio path.

The third noise source is electromagnetic pickup by the speaker wiring, addressed earlier through shielded cable replacement. The fourth is capacitive coupling between high-frequency logic traces and adjacent audio traces on the PCB itself, which is a design issue that cannot be corrected without board modification. This is the source of the subtle digital hash that is audible during silent passages on some boards, and it represents the noise floor of the original design. Attempting to eliminate it is futile; it is the sound of the board working as designed. Recognizing this distinction between a defect and a design characteristic is part of the judgment that separates a competent restorer from someone who replaces every component and still hears noise.

Capacitor replacement in the audio signal path demands attention to the specific requirements of each position. Coupling capacitors pass audio frequencies and block DC, so their value determines the low-frequency cutoff of the coupling network. The cutoff frequency is given by f_c = 1 / (2π R C), where R is the input impedance of the following stage and C is the coupling capacitance. A 1µF coupling capacitor feeding a 10kΩ input impedance produces a cutoff at 16 Hz, well below the audio band. Replacing it with a 0.1µF capacitor raises the cutoff to 160 Hz, which attenuates bass response and changes the character of the sound. Always replace coupling capacitors with the same value, and use the same or higher voltage rating. Film capacitors are electrically superior to electrolytics for coupling applications and will not degrade over time, but they are physically larger. In tight PCB layouts, high-quality electrolytics from Nichicon, Panasonic, or Rubycon are acceptable replacements. For coupling positions where space allows, WIMA MKS2 or MKP2 film capacitors provide a service life measured in decades rather than years.

Filter network components, the resistors and small capacitors that define the active and passive filter characteristics, rarely fail but sometimes drift in value. Carbon composition resistors, common on boards from the early 1980s, can drift 20 percent or more from their marked value over decades of thermal cycling. This shifts the filter cutoff frequencies and changes the tonal balance of the audio. Measuring the resistors in the filter network with a multimeter and comparing to the schematic values identifies drifted components. Metal film replacements at 1 percent tolerance restore the original filter characteristics and are thermally stable. The small capacitors in the filter networks, typically 100pF to 0.01µF, are usually ceramic or film types that do not degrade with age, but ceramic discs from the 1980s can develop microcracks that change their value. A capacitance meter confirms whether these components are still within tolerance.

The final validation of any audio restoration is a listening test with the cabinet fully assembled and powered, using game audio as the test material. Inject a test tone using a function generator connected to the audio input if the board supports external input, or simply run the game's attract mode and listen for the full range of sound effects. The audio should be free of hum, buzz, and digital hash. Volume control should be smooth across its entire range with no scratchiness or dropouts. Low-frequency effects should have body and impact, not the thin, reedy quality of high-passed coupling. Voice samples should be intelligible, not garbled by amplifier clipping or filter distortion. When all of these conditions are met, the audio system has been returned to its original operating envelope, and the restoration is complete.

| Troubleshooting Symptom | Probable Stage | Diagnostic Method | Corrective Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| No audio at all | Power amp or output coupling | Audio probe at amp input; scope at amp output | Check amp Vcc; replace output coupling cap; test amp IC |
| Constant hum, no game audio | Amp input stage or ground loop | Lift audio ground; check for DC offset at speaker | Repair ground isolation; replace input coupling cap |
| Scratchy volume control | Potentiometer wear | Rotate pot through full range | DeoxIT D5 treatment; replace if worn through |
| Distorted audio at all levels | Coupling cap failure or amp bias | Scope for clipping at amp output | Replace input coupling cap; check amp bias resistors |
| Digital hash in background | Supply bypass or ground coupling | Probe amp supply rail with scope | Replace bypass caps; verify ground isolation component |
| Audio present but thin/bright | Coupling cap value loss | Measure coupling cap value | Replace with same-value cap, film preferred |
| Thump at power on/off | Output coupling cap failure | Measure DC at speaker terminals | Replace output coupling cap; check amp for DC offset |
| Intermittent audio | Cold solder joint in signal path | Physical inspection; flex board while powered | Reflow solder joints in signal path; check ribbon connectors |

The discipline of arcade audio restoration rewards the technician who treats the signal path as a narrative: a story told in voltages and frequencies, where each chapter builds on the last and a single broken link silences the entire tale. The components are not exotic, the circuits are not complex by modern standards, and the diagnostic tools are accessible. What distinguishes the master restorer is the patience to trace the signal through every stage, the discipline to verify each component against its specification before replacing it, and the judgment to distinguish between a component that has failed and a design that was always imperfect. The goal is fidelity to the original sound, not improvement upon it, and that distinction defines the boundary between restoration and modification.
