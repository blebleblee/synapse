# Lesson 3: PCB Diagnostics & Repair

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

There is a particular kind of silence that greets you when you power up a classic arcade board and nothing happens — no raster, no sound, no coin-mech chime. That silence is not random. Every fault in a digital circuit is deterministic, the product of a physical cause that existed before you arrived and will continue to exist until you find and correct it. The discipline of PCB diagnostics is, at its core, the practice of reasoning backward from symptom to cause through a principled understanding of how digital logic, analog power supplies, and electromechanical systems interact on a single laminated board.

The golden era of arcade hardware, spanning roughly 1978 through 1994, was built almost exclusively on bipolar TTL (Transistor-Transistor Logic) and CMOS logic families, with Z80, 6502, 68000, and their derivatives serving as central processors. What makes these machines tractable to repair — far more so than a modern system-on-chip — is precisely their discreteness. Every gate, every latch, every bus driver is a physical package you can probe with a meter or a logic probe. The laws governing their behavior are not hidden in firmware abstractions; they are the laws of voltage thresholds and switching times, expressed in the datasheets of well-documented parts that have been in continuous production for decades.

Understanding TTL logic states is the invariant from which all diagnostic work flows. In the standard 74LS family, a logic HIGH is any voltage between 2.0 V and 5.0 V at an output, and a logic LOW is anything between 0 V and 0.8 V. The region between 0.8 V and 2.0 V is undefined — a floating or contested state that will cause erratic behavior anywhere it appears on a bus. When you measure 1.4 V on a data line, you are not seeing a "half-on" signal; you are seeing a bus conflict, a failed driver, or a floating input that happens to be biased by leakage current. The measurement itself is diagnostic. CMOS logic, used heavily in later boards and in memory mapping glue logic, has even wider HIGH/LOW thresholds relative to supply voltage but is far more sensitive to electrostatic discharge and to the slow voltage transitions that occur when a driving TTL output is not strong enough to fully swing the rail.

The JAMMA standard, ratified by the Japan Amusement Machine and Marketing Association in 1985, is the mechanical and electrical contract that makes this lesson possible. Before JAMMA, every manufacturer wired their cabinet differently, and swapping boards required custom harnesses. JAMMA defined a 56-pin edge connector — 28 fingers per side — that standardized power distribution, video output, audio, coin inputs, and a two-player button matrix across virtually the entire industry. Understanding this pinout is not merely useful for installation; it is your first diagnostic instrument. When a board arrives on your bench showing no video, your first measurement is not on the board itself — it is at the JAMMA connector, confirming that +5 V, +12 V, and ground are present, correctly positioned, and within tolerance before the board ever receives power. Applying 12 V to a +5 V rail, a mistake that becomes easy when harness wires migrate or corrosion has bridged adjacent pins, will destroy TTL logic instantaneously.

The specific pin assignments every practitioner must commit to memory are as follows. On the component side (side A), pin 1 carries the frame ground connection to the cabinet chassis. Pins 3 and 4 carry +5 V; pins 5 and 6 are additional +5 V. Pin 7 is +12 V. Pin 2 is the video ground reference. On the solder side (side B), the mirror layout repeats the power distribution. Video signals — RED, GREEN, BLUE, and CSYNC — appear on pins A/B 10 through 13. Coin switches, start buttons, and player controls occupy pins 15 through 28. The standard specifies that player one's joystick and buttons appear on side A and player two's on side B, though many manufacturers took liberties with the button count extensions.

Electrolytic capacitors are the single most common failure point on boards of this era, and the physics of that failure are worth internalizing. An aluminum electrolytic capacitor stores charge in a thin aluminum oxide dielectric layer that is anodized onto the anode foil. The electrolyte — a conductive fluid that serves as the true cathode contact — slowly evaporates and chemically degrades over decades, particularly when the capacitor is subjected to elevated temperatures. As capacitance falls and equivalent series resistance (ESR) rises, the capacitor's ability to decouple power supply noise becomes impaired. On a digital board, this manifests as rail voltage ripple that falls outside TTL supply tolerance (the 74LS family requires +5 V ± 5%, meaning the rail must stay between 4.75 V and 5.25 V under load). When ripple exceeds this window on a 10 MHz clock cycle, the first failures appear as intermittent resets, corrupted sprite data, or audio that buzzes and pops. In the worst cases, an electrolytic that has developed internal gas pressure ruptures through its vent seal and deposits caustic electrolyte across adjacent PCB traces — turning a capacitor replacement job into a trace repair and corrosion remediation exercise simultaneously.

The professional mindset here is one of directed skepticism. You do not assume the schematic is correct, you do not assume the previous technician's work was competent, and you do not assume a component is good simply because it tests within spec at DC when the fault only manifests at speed. What you can assume is that the laws of physics are consistent and that every symptom is a clue.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

### The JAMMA Signal Path

To diagnose a board systematically, you need a clear mental model of how signals flow from the power supply, through the logic, and out to the monitor and speaker. The following diagram represents a generalized JAMMA-era arcade PCB architecture at the signal-path level.

```text
                        ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
                        │                   ARCADE PCB                            │
                        │                                                         │
   JAMMA EDGE           │   ┌──────────┐    ┌──────────┐    ┌──────────────────┐ │
   CONNECTOR            │   │  Power   │    │   CPU    │    │  Program ROMs    │ │
                        │   │  Filter  │───▶│  Z80 /   │───▶│  (27C256 etc.)   │ │
   +5V ───────────────▶ │   │  Caps    │    │  68000   │    └──────────────────┘ │
   +12V ──────────────▶ │   └──────────┘    │          │                         │

                        │                   │          │───▶│  Work RAM        │ │
```

Each stage in this chain is a potential fault domain. Power filter capacitors failing raises rail noise before the CPU ever executes an instruction. A bad ROM means the CPU fetches corrupt opcodes and runs into undefined behavior or halt states. A failed custom sprite IC produces garbled graphics even when the CPU is running correctly. This layered architecture means that disciplined fault isolation always proceeds from the power supply inward toward the logic, and from the CPU outward toward the peripherals.

### TTL Logic State Measurement in Practice

Your primary instruments for in-circuit logic analysis are a quality digital multimeter (Fluke 87V or equivalent true-RMS instrument), a dedicated logic probe, and, for more complex work, a 100 MHz+ digital oscilloscope with at least two channels. The logic probe deserves particular attention because it displays the dynamic state of a signal — HIGH, LOW, PULSE, or FLOAT — in real time without loading the circuit the way a scope probe can at higher frequencies.

When you bring a logic probe to a Z80 data bus during normal operation, you should see the PULSE indicator active on all eight data lines (D0–D7). A line showing perpetually HIGH or perpetually LOW is either driven to that state by a bus conflict or is stuck due to an internal short in a RAM chip, a tri-state buffer that has failed non-conducting, or a physical trace break. The procedure for isolating a stuck bus line begins with identifying which device is asserting the line. With power off, an ohmmeter between the stuck data line and VCC or GND will reveal a resistive path under 100 Ω if a chip has a failed output stage. Work through each device connected to that line, lifting its pins or placing a logic clip to monitor individual outputs in isolation.

A logic clip — the spring-loaded 16-pin or 20-pin DIP clip with individual LED indicators for each pin — is one of the most underused tools in arcade repair. Clipped onto a 74LS374 octal latch, it displays the state of all sixteen pins simultaneously, letting you watch in real time whether input data is reaching the chip and whether the output is following it after the clock edge. A chip that shows active, changing inputs on D0–D7 but static outputs on Q0–Q7 has either lost its CLK signal, its OE (output enable) is held inactive, or the chip has failed internally.

### Capacitor ESR Testing and Replacement Protocols

Modern in-circuit ESR testers — the MESR-100 and similar instruments — allow you to measure the equivalent series resistance of electrolytic capacitors without desoldering them, by injecting a low-amplitude 100 kHz AC signal that bypasses the DC charge stored in the capacitor. A healthy 47 µF electrolytic in the power filter section of a +5 V rail should read below 1 Ω ESR; anything above 5–10 Ω in a cap of that value is functionally failed for decoupling purposes even if capacitance reads close to rated value on a standard LCR meter. This distinction matters: a standard capacitance measurement at 1 kHz will often show a degraded cap as "in spec" while its ESR at switching frequencies has tripled, making it invisible to casual multimeter measurement but invisible in a different sense — invisible as a cause while its effects destroy game stability.

For through-hole electrolytic replacement on boards from this era, the standard practice is to use 105°C-rated capacitors (versus the original 85°C parts) with equal or slightly higher capacitance and equal or higher voltage rating. Never substitute a lower voltage rating, and be scrupulous about polarity. The negative stripe on the capacitor body must align with the square pad on the PCB, which marks the negative terminal on virtually all Japanese and American boards from this period. When a cap has leaked electrolyte, do not simply replace it and move on. The electrolyte is conductive and mildly corrosive; it must be fully neutralized before a new cap goes in. Apply 99% isopropyl alcohol with a stiff-bristle toothbrush, working the contamination away from the board center, and allow complete evaporation. Inspect adjacent traces under 10× magnification — a loupe or USB microscope — before soldering anything.

### Trace Continuity and Repair

A broken PCB trace is physically tiny but electrically absolute — it is an open circuit that interrupts a signal path as completely as a severed cable. Identifying broken traces requires a combination of visual inspection under raking light (a fiber-optic illuminator directed at a low angle makes lifted or cracked traces reflect differently than the surrounding laminate) and electrical continuity measurement with the board powered down. Set your meter to its lowest resistance range or its continuity/beep mode and test between known connected points: a chip pin and its via, a via and the next pad it feeds, a pad and the edge connector finger it should reach.

Trace repair materials depend on trace width and location. For traces wider than 0.5 mm on the board surface, 30 AWG Kynar wire-wrap wire soldered along the break with rosin-flux solder paste is both mechanically durable and electrically adequate. For fine traces under 0.3 mm, silver-loaded conductive epoxy (MG Chemicals 8331) is preferable when the epoxy joint can be fully covered with a conformal coating dot afterward to prevent the relatively brittle epoxy from cracking under thermal cycling. Never use conductive silver paint as a permanent repair on signal traces that carry clocked data — its resistance is too variable and it migrates under humidity.

For multilayer boards, internal trace breaks are substantially more difficult. If the break is within a via, re-drilling the via at a slightly larger diameter and re-plating with a copper rivet (available from Circuitworks as pre-formed rivets for PCB repair) is the professional solution. Drilling blind and bridging with a surface wire only addresses the symptom if the via itself is the break point, as opposed to an internal plane delamination.

### ROM Verification

A bad ROM is one of the most deceptive failures in arcade diagnostics because it corrupts behavior in ways that mimic CPU faults, RAM failures, or even timing problems. The CPU is faithfully executing instructions — just the wrong ones. The standard for ROM verification in professional arcade restoration is to dump the ROM contents with a device programmer and compare the resulting binary against a known-good reference.

The TL866II Plus (or its successor, the T48) handles the full range of JEDEC-standard UV EPROMs used in arcade hardware: 2716, 2732, 2764, 27128, 27256, 27512, and their CMOS variants. Procedure begins by marking the ROM's physical location on the board before removal — a small piece of masking tape with the chip identifier and orientation notch direction is sufficient. After extraction with a DIP extraction tool (never a flathead screwdriver, which bends pins), place the chip in the programmer's ZIF socket, ensuring pin 1 aligns with the socket's lever end, and use the programmer's read function to dump the full contents. The resulting binary is then compared against MAME's ROM set reference or the manufacturer's published checksum. MAME's romset for the specific PCB revision provides individual CRC-32 and SHA-1 checksums for each ROM file; your programmer software should compute both during read. A mismatch on any bit in the binary indicates a failed cell in the EPROM array, and the chip must be replaced.

Replacement EPROMs require an erasure step if reusing salvaged chips. UV EPROMs — identifiable by the quartz window on top of the IC package — are erased by exposure to shortwave UV (254 nm) for 15–20 minutes at 2–3 cm distance using a dedicated UV eraser unit. After erasure, verify the chip is fully blank (all bytes read as 0xFF) before programming. Write the verified binary at the programmer's recommended programming voltage for that device (21 V for 2716, 12.5 V for most 27C256 variants). Verify the write immediately after programming by re-reading and comparing. Only then install the chip, with a turned-pin IC socket permanently installed in the original pad — you should never again solder a ROM directly to the board once you have established that it is a failure-prone position.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

### Custom IC Substitution Constraints

The most difficult category of fault in arcade restoration is a failed custom integrated circuit. These are the proprietary ASICs, PALs (Programmable Array Logic devices), and gate arrays that manufacturers designed to consolidate what would otherwise require dozens of discrete TTL chips into a single package. When a custom IC fails, there is no datasheet, no drop-in substitute, and often no second-hand supply. Capcom's CPS-1 system, for instance, relies on a custom sprite sorting chip; Konami's early 1980s boards use custom sound chips whose internal logic is not publicly documented.

The first constraint to internalize is that PAL-based custom logic is frequently reproducible if the original PAL's equations can be recovered. Many PALs used in arcade hardware are unprotected — their fuse maps are readable with a programmer — and the extracted equations can be reimplemented in a GAL (Generic Array Logic, an electrically erasable equivalent) using WinCUPL or GALasm. The 16V8 and 22V10 GAL devices cover the vast majority of registered and combinational PAL types from this era. Where a PAL is security-fused and cannot be read directly, the community at forums like KLOV and Jamma+ has in many cases already reverse-engineered the equations through functional analysis and published replacement .jed files for direct programming.

For true custom ASICs — the Capcom CPS-A/B chips, the Namco C series, the Atari SLAGS — no general-purpose substitute exists. Here the diagnostic priority shifts: confirm the custom IC is actually the fault before pronouncing the board irreparable. A dead ASIC is often preceded or accompanied by a failed decoupling capacitor directly adjacent to its VCC pin, a corroded power trace reducing supply voltage at the chip's pin below 4.75 V, or a bus contention fault upstream that has caused overstress. Measure voltage directly at the ASIC's power pins under load, not at the JAMMA connector. A 0.3 V drop across a corroded trace between the connector and the chip is enough to place the supply out of specification and cause behavior indistinguishable from a failed chip. Repair the supply path, re-test, and you may find the ASIC recovers.

When a custom IC is confirmed dead and no functional spare exists, some boards have FPGA-based replacement modules available through the community — Atari's SLAGS chip has a documented FPGA substitute for the System 1 hardware, and several Capcom CPS-1 custom replacements are available as open-source Verilog implementations. These require more soldering complexity — the FPGA module connects via point-to-point wiring to the pads of the removed original chip — but represent the professional path to full restoration when parts are exhausted.

### Socketing Techniques for Long-Term Maintainability

The practice of socketing — replacing direct-soldered ICs with low-profile turned-pin or machine-pin DIP sockets — is both a preventive measure and a repair technique. Turned-pin (machined-pin) sockets, as opposed to stamped-pin sockets, provide reliable gold-plated contact with far lower insertion force and far longer contact life. For any board that has gone through one restoration, the ROMs, the CPU, and the main RAM chips should all be socketed as a matter of course.

The technique for installing a socket where a chip was directly soldered begins with complete, clean desoldering. A temperature-controlled desoldering station with a vacuum pump (the Hakko FR-301 is the benchmark tool here) removes solder from each via cleanly enough that a socket pin can be inserted without force. Work at 350–370°C on the desoldering tip, maintaining contact with both the pad and the pin for two seconds before activating the vacuum. After all pins are cleared, remove the chip with a DIP extractor, then inspect each hole under magnification. Any partially blocked hole should be cleared with a 0.8 mm desoldering braid or a hand solder pump before the socket is installed. Install the socket with its notch aligned to the board's silkscreen orientation mark, tack solder two opposing corner pins first to lock position, then solder all remaining pins in a cross-pattern to prevent thermal warping of the socket body.

Do not use dual-leaf stamped sockets for CPUs or any chip that cycles through significant temperature variation during operation. The stamped contacts develop micro-arcing at their contact points as the IC package expands and contracts, creating intermittent faults that are maddening to diagnose because they disappear when the board cools and the socket returns to its nominal geometry. Turned-pin sockets eliminate this failure mode.

### Corrosion Remediation

Battery-backed SRAM is the most common source of deep corrosion damage on arcade PCBs from the late 1980s onward. Games that required high-score preservation or configuration storage used a 3 V or 3.6 V lithium cell or a rechargeable NiCd cell connected directly to the SRAM's VCC pin through a Schottky diode. When these cells reach end of life, they leak alkaline or acidic electrolyte depending on chemistry, and the electrolyte attacks copper traces, through-hole plating, and the FR4 laminate itself. On a Capcom CPS-1 board with an NiCd suicide battery, you may find a 3 cm radius of active corrosion around the battery holder, with traces that appear intact under normal light but are entirely corroded through their cross-section under magnification.

The remediation workflow begins with physical removal of all corrosion products using 99% isopropyl alcohol and a fiberglass scratch brush (not a wire brush, which deposits conductive metal fibers across the board). Work in one direction, from the outer edges of the damaged area inward toward the battery site, to avoid spreading contaminated fluid over clean copper. After mechanical cleaning, neutralize any remaining acid residue with a very light application of baking soda solution (5 g per 100 mL distilled water), followed by a thorough flush with fresh isopropyl alcohol and air drying. Inspect every trace in the affected area under 10× minimum magnification and probe each one for continuity. Map every break before beginning any repair work — finding a second break in the middle of a repair because you did not fully characterize the damage first is a recoverable but time-wasting error.

After trace repair, replace the battery with a CR2032 coin cell holder wired through a 1N5817 Schottky diode to the SRAM VCC circuit, with the original through-hole battery footprint jumpered out. This non-rechargeable configuration eliminates the leakage failure mode permanently, since CR2032 cells do not leak under normal discharge conditions and can be replaced in sixty seconds without soldering.

### Oscilloscope-Based Timing Diagnosis

For intermittent faults that do not appear as a static logic level problem, the digital oscilloscope becomes the primary instrument. The specific workflow for clock signal verification begins at the crystal oscillator or clock generator chip — typically an HC-49/S crystal with a 74HCT04 or dedicated oscillator module. Measure the clock output at the CPU's CLK pin with the oscilloscope set to trigger on a rising edge. You are looking for three things: correct frequency (a Z80 running at 4 MHz should show a period of 250 ns), clean rise and fall times (under 10 ns for a 74HCT driver, under 20 ns for a standard TTL output), and absence of ringing. Ringing — oscillation on the leading or trailing edge of the clock pulse — indicates a transmission-line impedance mismatch, often caused by a trace that has lost its ground-plane reference through a delamination crack below the surface. Ringing of more than 20% of the signal amplitude on a 5 V rail can cause double-clocking of edge-sensitive flip-flops, producing data corruption that appears random but is in fact systematic.

For video sync signal diagnosis, the two-channel oscilloscope allows you to compare the CSYNC signal at the JAMMA connector against the board's internal sync generator output. A missing or malformed HSYNC pulse — the horizontal sync pulse should be 4.7 µs wide at 0 V within a standard 15 kHz composite signal — will cause the monitor to roll horizontally or produce no picture at all, even when the board is operating correctly in every other respect. This is a common failure mode on boards where the sync separator circuit uses a 74LS08 AND gate whose output has degraded to a logic-indeterminate level, too weak to drive the monitor's sync separator input. Replacing the 74LS08 is a ten-minute repair, but only the oscilloscope reveals it.

The practitioner who moves through a diagnostic session with this combination of instruments — JAMMA power verification, logic probe bus activity survey, in-circuit ESR measurement, ROM checksum verification, trace continuity mapping, and oscilloscope timing analysis — will resolve the overwhelming majority of faults that arrive on the bench. The boards that remain after this methodology are exhausted are the ones where a custom IC has genuinely failed and no community-sourced substitute yet exists. Those boards are set aside, documented thoroughly, and revisited as the restoration community's collective knowledge expands. Even that outcome is not defeat — it is the honest boundary of the current state of the art, clearly located by a disciplined process.
