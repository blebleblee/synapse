# Lesson 1: Hardware Foundations of Arcade Systems

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The arcade cabinet represents a crystallization of constraints that defined an entire medium. Between 1978 and 1995, before polygonal 3D rendering and networked gameplay became economically viable, arcade game design existed within an envelope of hard physical limits: the processing throughput of 8-bit and 16-bit microprocessors, the scanline refresh constraints of raster CRT monitors, the addressable space of ROM and RAM chips, and the thermal dissipation capacity of circuit boards enclosed in wood-and-metal cabinets standing in poorly ventilated arcade floors. These were not abstract limitations discussed in design documents; they were physical realities that engineers confronted every time they toggled an oscilloscope probe against a Z80 clock line or watched a sprite flicker because the video subsystem had exhausted its scanline budget.

Understanding arcade hardware architecture requires internalizing the distinction between *theoretical instruction throughput* and *practical cycle availability*. A Zilog Z80 clocked at 3.58 MHz does not deliver 3.58 million useful operations per second to game logic. The CPU must share bus access with video refresh circuitry, sound synthesis hardware, and interrupt service routines that fire every 16.67 milliseconds to synchronize with the 60 Hz vertical blanking interval of NTSC displays. The Z80's four-cycle minimum instruction latency means that even a simple register load consumes 1.12 microseconds of wall-clock time, and multi-byte operations like 16-bit additions require eleven to fifteen cycles. When the video system asserts a WAIT signal to steal bus cycles during active display, the CPU freezes mid-instruction, burning time that could have been spent advancing enemy AI state machines or collision detection routines.

This interplay between the CPU, memory subsystem, and display hardware establishes the first invariant of arcade system design: **every operation has an opportunity cost measured in scanlines**. A game running at 60 frames per second has 16,667 microseconds per frame to execute all game logic, audio mixing, input polling, and preparation of the next frame's tile and sprite data. At 3.58 MHz with realistic bus contention, a Z80-based system might have 40,000 to 50,000 effective CPU cycles per frame. Subtracting the fixed overhead of interrupt handling, input debouncing, and sound register updates leaves perhaps 35,000 cycles for gameplay code. When a single 16×16 sprite requires fifteen cycles to compute its screen position and another twenty cycles to queue its tile indices into video RAM, the sprite budget for a smooth gameplay experience collapses to around sixty active objects before frame timing degrades. Developers did not discover these limits through profiling tools; they measured them with logic analyzers and oscilloscopes, observing the exact moment when the CPU's instruction fetch coincided with the video system's DMA request.

The second invariant centers on **memory topology as a determinant of software architecture**. Unlike modern systems with uniform memory access and gigabytes of DRAM, arcade boards partitioned memory into distinct functional regions with vastly different access characteristics. Program ROM resided on mask-programmed EPROMs or socketed 2732/2764 chips, offering read-only access at full CPU speed but requiring physical replacement to update game code. Work RAM, typically 2 to 16 kilobytes of 6116 or 6264 static RAM chips, provided fast read-write storage for game state variables, stack frames, and temporary computation buffers. Video RAM, often dual-ported to allow simultaneous CPU writes and video chip reads, held tile indices, sprite attribute tables, and color palette registers. Each memory region mapped to a specific address range decoded by glue logic chips—74LS138 decoders and 74LS00 NAND gates that translated address bus signals into chip-select lines. Accessing video RAM during active display could trigger wait states or corruption if the video chip held the bus, forcing programmers to queue graphics updates during vertical blank periods when the electron beam returned to the top of the screen and the video subsystem released its bus hold.

This segmentation meant that data structure placement was a hardware design decision, not a runtime memory allocation problem. Character tile definitions burned into graphics ROM could not be modified by game code; animated sprites required pre-rendering all frames into ROM and selecting indices at runtime. The scanline-based rendering model of CRT displays imposed a strict temporal ordering: the video chip fetched tile and sprite data in raster order, left to right, top to bottom, thirty-two pixels per fetch cycle. A sprite positioned at screen coordinates (128, 96) would not be rendered until the video chip's line counter reached scanline 96 and its horizontal counter swept past pixel 128. Missing the timing window meant the sprite would not appear until the next frame. This deterministic, scanline-synchronous rendering model eliminated frame buffering and reduced memory requirements, but it also locked programmers into a rigid update schedule where all sprite positions, tile scrolling offsets, and palette swaps had to be committed before the vertical retrace interrupt fired.

The third invariant concerns **clock domain synchronization and the physics of signal propagation**. Arcade boards contained multiple subsystems operating at different clock frequencies: the CPU at 3 to 6 MHz, the video chip at 6 to 12 MHz, the audio synthesis chip at 1.5 to 4 MHz, and the master oscillator generating the 60 Hz vertical sync pulse. These clocks derived from crystal oscillators or divided-down master clocks, but they were not phase-locked; slight drift between domains required careful edge detection and handshaking. A CPU write to a sound register might occur during the sound chip's internal sample computation cycle, leading to glitches if the write setup and hold times violated the chip's specifications. Engineers used dual-rank flip-flop synchronizers—cascaded 74LS74 D-type flip-flops—to safely cross clock domains, accepting two clock cycles of latency in exchange for metastability protection. When debugging audio glitches or sprite tearing, technicians would probe these synchronizer chains with oscilloscopes, verifying that signals transitioned cleanly without runt pulses or voltage droop. The physical distance between chips mattered; PCB traces longer than six inches at 6 MHz clock rates exhibited measurable propagation delay and ringing, requiring series termination resistors or controlled impedance routing.

The Z80, MOS 6502, and Motorola 68000 represent three distinct architectural philosophies, each shaping the games built atop them. The Z80, derived from Intel's 8080, offered a rich instruction set with indexed addressing modes and a shadow register bank for fast interrupt context switches, but its 8-bit data bus and multiplexed address/data lines created bottlenecks for graphics-intensive titles. The 6502, with its minimal transistor count and emphasis on zero-page addressing, favored tight loops and deterministic cycle counts, enabling precise raster effects and smooth scrolling at the cost of limited addressable memory. The 68000, a 16/32-bit hybrid with a linear address space and orthogonal instruction set, powered the sprite-heavy, large-ROM games of the late 1980s and early 1990s, but its higher cost and power requirements delayed its adoption. These were not abstract tradeoffs; they manifested in specific design patterns: Z80 games used tile-based rendering and minimal sprite counts, 6502 games exploited zero-page self-modifying code for fast graphics blits, and 68000 games layered multiple hardware sprite planes with per-pixel priority and transparency.

The hardware constraints of arcade systems were not obstacles to be overcome through clever abstraction; they were the medium itself, as fundamental to game design as canvas texture is to oil painting. Programmers wrote directly to hardware registers, not through operating system APIs or hardware abstraction layers. They toggled individual bits in memory-mapped I/O ports to trigger sound effects, adjust scrolling offsets, and poll joystick inputs. When a game scrolled smoothly at sixty frames per second despite an 8-bit CPU and 16 kilobytes of RAM, it was because the programmer had internalized the exact cycle cost of every instruction, the bus arbitration protocol between CPU and video chip, and the scanline timing of sprite fetching. The discipline of working within these constraints produced a design language of efficiency and precision that remains instructive today, even as modern hardware has relaxed every physical limit these systems imposed.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

To dissect the hardware architecture of arcade systems, we begin with the signal flow from power-on reset through a single frame of gameplay. When the operator flips the cabinet's power switch, the 5-volt power supply rail stabilizes within milliseconds, but the CPU does not immediately begin fetching instructions. A reset circuit—typically a 555 timer or RC delay network—holds the CPU's RESET line low for 100 to 500 milliseconds, allowing all capacitors to charge and ensuring that volatile RAM reaches a defined state. As the reset pulse releases, the CPU reads a reset vector from a fixed address in ROM (address $0000 on the Z80, $FFFC on the 6502, and $000004 on the 68000) and begins executing the boot initialization routine. This code performs a hardware self-test, clearing RAM regions, initializing the sound chip's registers, writing default values into palette RAM, and setting up the interrupt vector table.

The memory map of a typical early-1980s Z80 arcade board reveals the physical layout of the system's address space:

```text
$0000 - $7FFF : Program ROM (32 KB, multiple 2764 EPROMs)
$8000 - $87FF : Work RAM (2 KB, 6116 SRAM)
$8800 - $8FFF : Video RAM - Tile Map (2 KB)
$9000 - $97FF : Video RAM - Sprite Attributes (2 KB)
$9800 - $9FFF : Color Palette RAM (512 bytes)
$A000 - $A003 : Sound Chip Registers (AY-3-8910)
$A800 - $A80F : Input Ports (Joystick, Buttons, DIP Switches)
$B000 - $B000 : Watchdog Timer Reset
$C000 - $FFFF : Banked ROM or Unused
```

This map is not a software convention; it is hardwired into the address decoding logic on the PCB. A 74LS138 3-to-8 decoder receives the three high-order address bits (A15, A14, A13) and generates eight chip-select signals, each enabling a different memory or I/O region. Addresses beginning with %000 (binary) activate the program ROM's chip-select line; addresses beginning with %100 enable work RAM. The video RAM regions use additional decoding stages to distinguish between tile map, sprite attribute, and palette accesses. When the CPU writes to address $9020, the address decoder asserts the sprite attribute RAM's chip-select line, and the write enable signal propagates through a 74LS373 latch that holds the sprite data until the video chip fetches it during the next frame.

The video subsystem operates as a semi-autonomous state machine synchronized to the master clock. A typical tile-based video chip generates a 256×224 pixel raster display by dividing the screen into an 8×8 pixel tile grid (32 tiles wide by 28 tiles tall). The tile map in video RAM is a two-dimensional array of byte indices, each referencing a tile pattern stored in graphics ROM. During each scanline, the video chip fetches tile indices from VRAM, uses them to address tile pattern ROM, and shifts the resulting pixel data into a parallel-to-serial converter that drives the RGB DACs. Horizontal and vertical counters increment with each pixel clock and scanline clock, and when the vertical counter reaches line 224, the video chip asserts the VBLANK interrupt line, signaling the CPU that it has 16 scanlines (approximately 1,000 microseconds) to update VRAM before active display resumes.

Sprite rendering introduces a second rendering pass layered atop the tile background. Sprite attribute RAM stores an array of four-byte entries, each defining one sprite: X position (1 byte), Y position (1 byte), tile index (1 byte), and attributes (1 byte encoding flip, palette, and priority). During each scanline, the video chip scans the entire sprite attribute table, compares each sprite's Y coordinate against the current scanline number, and if they match, stages that sprite's pixel data into a line buffer. When the horizontal counter reaches each staged sprite's X coordinate, the video chip muxes the sprite pixels with the background tiles, using the attribute byte's priority bit to determine whether the sprite draws in front of or behind the background. The limited width of the line buffer constrains the maximum number of sprites per scanline—typically eight to sixteen—and exceeding this limit causes sprites to flicker or disappear, a visual artifact players of early arcade games know intimately.

The Z80's instruction execution pipeline reveals the granular mechanics of CPU operation. Every instruction begins with an opcode fetch: the CPU places the program counter's value on the address bus, asserts the MREQ (memory request) and RD (read) control signals, waits for the memory subsystem to place data on the data bus, latches the opcode byte into the instruction register, and increments the program counter. This sequence consumes four clock cycles—the Z80's minimum instruction duration. Simple instructions like `LD A, B` (load register A from register B) complete in four cycles because they operate entirely within the CPU's internal register file. Instructions that access memory, such as `LD A, ($8000)`, require additional cycles: four for the opcode fetch, three for the address decode, and three for the memory read, totaling ten cycles. Arithmetic instructions like `ADD A, (HL)` combine memory access and computation, requiring eleven cycles: four for opcode fetch, three for address decode, three for memory read, and one for the addition operation.

The 6502 presents a contrasting execution model centered on zero-page addressing. The 6502's zero page is the first 256 bytes of memory ($0000 to $00FF), addressable with a single-byte operand instead of the two-byte addresses required for absolute addressing. An instruction like `LDA $20` (load accumulator from zero-page address $0020) completes in three cycles: two for opcode fetch and operand read, one for the zero-page memory access. The equivalent absolute addressing mode, `LDA $8020`, requires four cycles. This single-cycle savings becomes significant in tight loops where every cycle contributes to visible frame timing. The 6502 also permits self-modifying code through zero-page indexed indirect addressing: `LDA ($20), Y` dereferences a pointer stored at zero-page address $0020, adds the Y register as an offset, and loads from the resulting address. By modifying the pointer at $0020 each frame, programmers created fast sprite blitting routines that avoided costly indexed addressing overhead.

The Motorola 68000 represented a generational leap in architectural sophistication, though at significant cost and power budget. Its sixteen 32-bit general-purpose registers, linear 16-megabyte address space, and orthogonal instruction set allowed compilers to generate efficient code without the register pressure and memory segmentation of 8-bit architectures. A 68000 instruction like `MOVE.L D0, (A0)+` (move long word from data register D0 to the address in A0, then increment A0 by 4) executed in twelve cycles—slow by 6502 standards, but the 68000's higher clock rate (8 to 12 MHz in arcade applications) and ability to manipulate 32-bit data in a single operation offset the cycle count disadvantage. The 68000's instruction prefetch queue and pipelined execution allowed it to overlap instruction fetch with execution, provided the next instruction resided in contiguous memory and no bus contention occurred.

Clock cycles translate directly into frame timing budgets. Consider a Z80 clocked at 3.58 MHz in a 60 Hz arcade system. Each frame lasts 16,667 microseconds, during which the CPU executes approximately 59,667 clock cycles. Subtracting interrupt handling overhead (200 cycles for context save, interrupt vector dispatch, and context restore), input polling (500 cycles to read and debounce eight input bits), and sound register updates (300 cycles to write four AY-3-8910 registers), the game logic has roughly 58,667 cycles available. If the game maintains sixty-four active enemy objects and each requires 150 cycles for AI logic and position updates, enemy updates consume 9,600 cycles. Collision detection between the player and sixty-four enemies, using bounding box tests (25 cycles per test), adds 1,600 cycles. Scrolling the background tilemap by updating VRAM offset registers costs 50 cycles. The remaining 47,417 cycles cover player input response, projectile physics, explosion animations, and score rendering. When the frame overruns its cycle budget, the game either drops to 30 Hz (skipping every other VBLANK interrupt) or exhibits visible slowdown as entities lag behind their expected positions.

The watchdog timer enforces this real-time constraint from the hardware side. A typical watchdog circuit consists of a 555 timer configured as a monostable multivibrator with a timeout period of 500 milliseconds to one second. The game code writes any value to the watchdog reset address (e.g., $B000) at least once per frame, retriggering the 555's timing cycle. If the CPU crashes, enters an infinite loop, or fails to service the watchdog within the timeout window, the 555's output pulse triggers the system reset line, rebooting the cabinet. This prevents a locked-up game from remaining frozen on screen, which would cost the operator revenue and damage the cabinet's reputation. Developers verified watchdog functionality by intentionally commenting out the watchdog poke and confirming that the system reset after the expected interval.

The interaction between memory-mapped I/O and hardware registers reveals the granular control arcade programmers exercised. Writing to the AY-3-8910 sound chip's registers required a two-step protocol: first, write the register index to the chip's address port ($A000), then write the data value to the data port ($A001). Setting channel A's tone period to generate a 440 Hz tone (concert A) required calculating the period value from the formula period = clock_frequency / (16 × desired_frequency). With a 1.789 MHz clock, the period value equals 1,789,000 / (16 × 440) ≈ 254. The code sequence would be:

```text
LD A, $00        ; Register 0 = Channel A Tone Period Low
OUT ($A000), A
LD A, $FE        ; Period low byte = 254
OUT ($A001), A
LD A, $01        ; Register 1 = Channel A Tone Period High
OUT ($A000), A
LD A, $00        ; Period high byte = 0
OUT ($A001), A
LD A, $08        ; Register 8 = Channel A Amplitude
OUT ($A000), A
LD A, $0F        ; Maximum amplitude = 15
OUT ($A001), A
```

Each OUT instruction consumed eleven cycles on the Z80, meaning the six register writes required sixty-six cycles—significant overhead if sound effects triggered frequently. Optimized code batched sound updates into a single interrupt service routine, amortizing the I/O cost across all audio channels.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Optimization in arcade systems began with cycle-accurate instruction selection and addressing mode choice. Consider the task of clearing 2 kilobytes of work RAM to zero at system startup. The naive approach uses a loop with indexed addressing:

```text
LD HL, $8000      ; Start address
LD BC, $0800      ; Byte count (2048)
CLEAR_LOOP:
LD (HL), $00      ; Write zero
INC HL            ; Advance pointer
DEC BC            ; Decrement counter
LD A, B           ; Test if BC == 0
OR C
JR NZ, CLEAR_LOOP
```

This loop consumes twenty-eight cycles per iteration: seven for `LD (HL), $00`, six for `INC HL`, six for `DEC BC`, four for `LD A, B`, four for `OR C`, and seven for the conditional jump when taken. Clearing 2048 bytes requires 2048 iterations at twenty-eight cycles each, totaling 57,344 cycles—nearly an entire frame budget. Unrolling the loop by four reduces loop overhead:

```text
CLEAR_LOOP:
LD (HL), $00      ; 7 cycles
INC HL            ; 6 cycles
LD (HL), $00
INC HL
LD (HL), $00
INC HL
LD (HL), $00
INC HL            ; Four writes complete
DEC BC
DEC BC
DEC BC
DEC BC            ; Decrement by 4
LD A, B
OR C
JR NZ, CLEAR_LOOP
```

Unrolling by four reduces loop iterations from 2048 to 512, cutting overhead by 75 percent and reducing total cycle count to approximately 20,000 cycles—a 65 percent improvement. Further unrolling reaches diminishing returns as instruction cache limitations and code size constraints dominate.

Sprite management represents the canonical optimization problem in arcade game development. The video hardware imposes a fixed sprite-per-scanline limit—typically eight or sixteen—beyond which sprites flicker or vanish. Games with many simultaneously active objects implement priority sorting to ensure critical sprites (the player, bosses, projectiles) always render while less important objects (background decorations, distant enemies) flicker under load. A simple priority sort compares each sprite's Y coordinate against the current scanline during VBLANK, maintaining a linked list or priority queue of active sprites. When the list exceeds the hardware limit, the lowest-priority sprites are culled. This sorting pass adds 500 to 1500 cycles per frame depending on sprite count and list structure, but it prevents catastrophic failure modes where the player character flickers and becomes untrackable.

Collision detection on 8-bit hardware without floating-point units or hardware multipliers required integer-only bounding box algorithms. The separating axis theorem provides a cycle-efficient test: two axis-aligned rectangles overlap if and only if their projections overlap on both the X and Y axes. For rectangles defined by (X1, Y1, W1, H1) and (X2, Y2, W2, H2), the overlap condition is:

```text
(X1 < X2 + W2) AND (X1 + W1 > X2) AND (Y1 < Y2 + H2) AND (Y1 + H1 > Y2)
```

Each comparison requires two additions and one comparison—approximately fifteen cycles per axis. The four comparisons and three logical ANDs total roughly seventy cycles per collision pair. A game with a single player and sixty-four enemies performs sixty-four collision tests per frame, consuming 4,480 cycles. Spatial partitioning using a coarse grid reduces tests to only objects within adjacent grid cells, cutting average collision costs by 60 to 80 percent for sparse object distributions.

Memory bandwidth contention between the CPU and video chip manifests as unpredictable wait states that disrupt timing-critical code. Video RAM accesses during active display trigger the video chip's bus hold signal, freezing the CPU until the current tile fetch completes. A VRAM write that occurs during scanline rendering might stall the CPU for three to fifteen cycles, depending on where the memory request falls relative to the video chip's fetch cycle. To avoid this variability, critical code paths disable interrupts and defer all VRAM updates to the vertical blank period. A typical VBLANK handler executes a display list: a queue of pending VRAM operations (sprite position updates, tile map changes, palette swaps) that execute atomically during the guaranteed idle period. The display list structure separates game logic from display synchronization, allowing deterministic frame timing independent of scene complexity.

Scrolling algorithms expose the tension between hardware capabilities and software flexibility. Early arcade boards implemented hardware scrolling through VRAM offset registers: writing a new X or Y offset shifted the entire tile map by the specified number of pixels without CPU intervention. Smooth eight-directional scrolling required updating both X and Y offsets each frame, costing approximately 50 cycles for register writes. However, hardware scrolling applied uniformly to the entire screen; selective scrolling of foreground and background layers required partitioning VRAM into separate regions or using scanline-based offset modulation. Advanced techniques like raster interrupts allowed changing scroll offsets mid-frame, creating parallax effects where distant background tiles scrolled at half speed relative to foreground action. Implementing parallax via raster interrupt required a tightly timed ISR that triggered at a specific scanline, updated the scroll register, and returned within the horizontal blank period—approximately sixty cycles of execution time.

Audio synthesis on the AY-3-8910 and similar PSG (programmable sound generator) chips imposed strict update rate constraints. The AY-3-8910's three square wave channels and one noise channel updated at the chip's sample rate, derived by dividing the master clock by sixteen. At a 1.789 MHz clock, the sample rate was approximately 111 kHz, but the chip's envelope generator and amplitude registers updated asynchronously to the CPU, creating potential for glitches if registers changed mid-cycle. Best practice reserved a dedicated interrupt handler for audio updates, ensuring that all channel registers wrote atomically during the same frame. Music playback from a sequenced note table required converting note frequency values (e.g., MIDI note 69 = 440 Hz) into AY-3-8910 period registers using lookup tables precomputed at development time. A typical four-channel music player consumed 800 to 1500 cycles per frame, depending on the number of active voices and envelope updates.

Failure modes in arcade hardware often presented as intermittent glitches rather than hard crashes, complicating diagnosis. Sprite flickering could indicate exceeding the sprite-per-scanline limit, address decoding glitches from loose EPROM sockets, or video RAM corruption from bus contention. Sound distortion might result from incorrect period values, clock domain metastability, or power supply ripple coupling into analog audio stages. Screen tearing—horizontal discontinuities in the displayed image—occurred when the CPU updated VRAM during active display instead of waiting for VBLANK, causing the video chip to fetch partially updated tile indices mid-scanline. Debugging these issues required logic analyzers and oscilloscopes to observe bus transactions and signal timing at the hardware level. Technicians would probe the video chip's VBLANK output to confirm interrupt timing, measure the CPU's address bus to verify memory access patterns, and check power supply rails for voltage droop under load.

Thermal management represented a persistent reliability concern. Arcade boards dissipated ten to twenty watts of heat in enclosed cabinets with limited airflow, causing CPU, ROM, and video chips to operate at junction temperatures exceeding 60°C. Thermal cycling during daily power-on/power-off sequences fatigued solder joints, creating intermittent connections that manifested as random crashes or graphics corruption. Preventive maintenance included reflowing solder joints on high-power components, cleaning PCB edge connectors with isopropyl alcohol, and replacing electrolytic capacitors that dried out over years of operation. Operators learned to recognize failure signatures: a game that crashed consistently after ten minutes indicated thermal shutdown or marginal timing; a game that worked after power-cycling but failed after hours suggested electrolytic capacitor degradation affecting voltage regulation.

The watchdog timer introduced a subtle failure mode when interrupt-heavy code exceeded frame timing budgets. If game logic consumed more than 16,667 microseconds per frame, the VBLANK interrupt would fire before the previous frame's logic completed, causing nested interrupt context switches and stack overflow. Without a watchdog poke in the delayed frame, the system reset. Developers mitigated this by instrumenting frame timing with port writes to unused addresses, then probing those addresses with a logic analyzer to measure execution time distributions. When optimization failed to meet timing, games implemented adaptive quality degradation: reducing sprite counts, simplifying AI, or dropping animation frames under load to preserve the watchdog cadence.

The hardware foundations of arcade systems were not abstract specifications; they were physical, measurable, and unforgiving. Every design decision—processor choice, memory layout, video architecture—shaped the games built atop them as directly as the sculptor's choice of marble or bronze shapes the finished work. Mastering these foundations required fluency in assembly language, oscilloscope operation, and the cycle-by-cycle behavior of CPU pipelines and bus arbitration. The developers who built these systems did not abstract away the hardware; they embraced it, exploited it, and through their constraints achieved a precision and economy of design that remains a benchmark for efficiency in interactive systems.
