# Lesson 3: Toolpath Synthesis, Print Head Motion, Timing Optimization

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

At the heart of every sliced 3D print lies a profound tension between geometric fidelity and kinematic reality. The slicer's fundamental task—decomposing a continuous three-dimensional surface into a sequence of linear extrusion moves—appears deceptively simple until one confronts the hard physical constraints of actuator inertia, belt elasticity, and thermoplastic rheology. A naive toolpath that commands instantaneous velocity reversals or treats the print head as a massless point will produce ringing artifacts, dimensional inaccuracy, and layer adhesion failures. The synthesis of toolpaths, therefore, must reconcile the abstract geometry exported from CAD with the dynamic response characteristics of a machine constrained by Newton's laws and the time-dependent behavior of molten polymer.

The motion planning pipeline begins with perimeter sequencing and infill pattern generation, but the true complexity emerges when the slicer must transform these geometric primitives into timed motor commands. Every linear segment in the toolpath carries not just spatial coordinates but also a target volumetric flow rate, which in turn determines the nozzle velocity required to maintain dimensional accuracy. The relationship $v = \frac{Q}{A}$, where $v$ is linear velocity, $Q$ is volumetric flow rate, and $A$ is the cross-sectional area of the extruded bead, establishes the baseline constraint. However, the physical print head cannot instantaneously achieve arbitrary velocities; it must accelerate and decelerate according to acceleration limits $a_{\text{max}}$ dictated by stepper motor torque curves, belt tension, and the risk of skipped steps or resonant vibration.

The junction between two toolpath segments presents a critical decision point. If the angle between consecutive moves is sharp, the print head must decelerate to a lower junction velocity to avoid overshooting the corner, a phenomenon governed by the jerk-limited motion profile. The maximum safe junction velocity $v_j$ for two segments forming an angle $\theta$ follows from centripetal acceleration constraints: $v_j = \sqrt{a_{\text{max}} \cdot r}$, where $r$ is the effective radius of curvature at the junction. For acute angles approaching 180 degrees (near-reversals), this radius shrinks dramatically, forcing the toolpath planner to insert velocity reductions that can dominate total print time on geometries with dense feature detail.

Extrusion pressure dynamics introduce a second layer of complexity. The molten filament in the hot end behaves as a compressible viscoelastic column, exhibiting both elastic storage and viscous flow. When the extruder motor advances filament, pressure builds in the melt zone before the polymer exits the nozzle, creating a temporal lag between commanded extrusion and actual material deposition. This lag is quantified by the extruder's pressure advance coefficient $K$, typically ranging from 0.03 to 0.15 mm per mm/s of velocity change depending on filament stiffness and melt zone geometry. Without compensation, abrupt velocity changes cause over-extrusion at accelerations and under-extrusion at decelerations, manifesting as corner bulges and gaps.

Retraction mechanics further complicate the extrusion state machine. When transitioning between disjoint perimeter segments or traversing open space, the extruder must reverse direction to relieve nozzle pressure and prevent oozing. The retraction distance $d_{\text{retract}}$, typically 0.5–2.0 mm for direct-drive extruders and 4–8 mm for Bowden systems, must be carefully tuned to balance stringing suppression against the risk of air gaps upon re-priming. The retraction velocity $v_{\text{retract}}$, often 25–45 mm/s, interacts with the printer's maximum volumetric throughput: retracting too aggressively can create temporary under-pressure that persists through the next extrusion, while insufficient retraction leaves residual pressure that bleeds material during travel moves.

Bridging detection and overhang strategies represent specialized cases where geometric analysis must override default speed settings. When the slicer identifies a span with no supporting material beneath it, the toolpath generator must reduce speed to allow the extruded filament to cool and tension-stiffen mid-air before sagging. Empirical bridge speeds range from 15 to 40 mm/s depending on filament thermal properties and cooling efficiency, with PLA achieving superior results due to its low glass transition temperature and rapid crystallization. The slicer must also modulate fan speed—often to 100% for bridges—to accelerate heat extraction and promote solidification before gravity can deform the suspended strand.

Print speed modulation based on feature size introduces another layer of adaptive control. Small perimeters and intricate details require reduced speeds to prevent the cumulative heat input from raising localized bed temperatures above the polymer's softening point, causing warping or loss of definition. The minimum layer time heuristic, typically 5–15 seconds, ensures adequate cooling between layers by inserting wait commands or reducing travel speed when the toolpath completes a layer faster than this threshold. Conversely, large solid infill regions can tolerate higher speeds—often 150% to 200% of perimeter speeds—without sacrificing accuracy, allowing total print time reduction without compromising surface quality.

The invariants governing toolpath synthesis can be distilled into five core principles. First, every move must respect the printer's kinematic acceleration limits to avoid missed steps or mechanical resonance. Second, junction velocities must be computed from the geometric angle and permissible centripetal acceleration to prevent corner overshoot. Third, extrusion commands must incorporate pressure advance to compensate for melt zone compliance and maintain consistent bead width through velocity changes. Fourth, retraction and unretraction sequences must be injected at strategic transitions to prevent stringing while avoiding air gaps. Fifth, speed must be modulated based on feature geometry, thermal constraints, and layer cooling requirements to balance throughput with fidelity. These principles form the foundation upon which all high-performance toolpath planning is built.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The toolpath synthesis pipeline operates as a staged transform, converting the geometric shell of sliced layers into a temporally sequenced stream of machine commands. At the input stage, the slicer possesses a set of closed polyline contours representing perimeters and a grid or fractal pattern representing infill, all defined in two-dimensional layer space. The first transformation identifies the extrusion order, determining which perimeter should be printed first (inner-to-outer or outer-to-inner) and how infill orientation alternates between layers to maximize isotropy. This ordering decision has profound implications for dimensional accuracy: printing outer perimeters first allows the inner perimeter and infill to push outward, compensating for shrinkage, while inner-first sequencing risks dimensional overshoot if the hot inner material deforms the already-solidified outer wall.

Once sequencing is established, the motion planner converts each polyline segment into a linear move command with associated extrusion. The fundamental data structure is the move object, containing start coordinates $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$, end coordinates $(x_1, y_1, z_1)$, feedrate $F$ in millimeters per minute, and extrusion distance $E$ in filament millimeters. The feedrate encodes the desired velocity along the segment, but this target velocity is subject to modification by the acceleration planner. The extrusion distance is computed from the segment length $L = \sqrt{(x_1 - x_0)^2 + (y_1 - y_0)^2}$, desired line width $w$, layer height $h$, and an extrusion multiplier $m$ (typically 1.0, adjusted for calibration) according to the volumetric relationship:

$$
E = \frac{L \cdot w \cdot h \cdot m}{\pi (d_{\text{filament}}/2)^2}
$$

where $d_{\text{filament}}$ is the nominal filament diameter (1.75 or 2.85 mm). This formula assumes a rectangular cross-section approximation, though in reality the extruded bead exhibits a slightly elliptical profile due to die swell and surface tension effects.

The acceleration planning stage analyzes the entire sequence of moves to compute achievable velocities at each junction. The planner begins by assigning each move its requested feedrate as the maximum allowable velocity, then performs a backward pass to propagate deceleration constraints. For each junction $i$ between moves $i-1$ and $i$, the planner computes the maximum entry velocity $v_{\text{entry},i}$ based on the junction angle and allowable centripetal acceleration. If the previous move's exit velocity exceeds this safe junction velocity, the planner inserts a deceleration constraint that propagates backward, potentially reducing velocities along an entire chain of preceding moves. The forward pass then resolves these constraints by computing actual entry and exit velocities, ensuring that the printer can physically achieve the commanded motion profile without exceeding acceleration limits.

```
Acceleration Planning Pseudocode:

for each move M in toolpath:
    M.max_entry_velocity = min(M.feedrate, junction_velocity(M.prev, M))
    M.max_exit_velocity = min(M.feedrate, junction_velocity(M, M.next))

// Backward pass: propagate deceleration limits
for each move M in reverse(toolpath):
    if M.exit_velocity > M.next.max_entry_velocity:
        M.exit_velocity = M.next.max_entry_velocity
        M.entry_velocity = sqrt(M.exit_velocity^2 - 2 * a_max * M.length)

// Forward pass: enforce acceleration limits
for each move M in toolpath:
    if M.entry_velocity > M.prev.exit_velocity:
        M.entry_velocity = M.prev.exit_velocity
    M.exit_velocity = min(M.exit_velocity, 
                          sqrt(M.entry_velocity^2 + 2 * a_max * M.length))
```

Pressure advance compensation modifies the extrusion command in proportion to the rate of velocity change. When the print head accelerates along a segment, the extruder must advance additional filament equal to $K \cdot \Delta v$ to preemptively build pressure in the hot end, where $\Delta v$ is the velocity change and $K$ is the pressure advance coefficient. This advance is gradually unwound during deceleration. Modern firmware implementations (Marlin, Klipper, RepRapFirmware) handle this compensation in real-time by computing instantaneous velocity derivatives from the commanded trajectory, but the slicer can optionally pre-compute and embed these corrections directly into the extrusion values.

Retraction sequencing operates as a state machine tracking the extruder's prime status. The slicer maintains a boolean flag indicating whether the nozzle is primed (containing pressurized melt ready to extrude) or retracted (filament pulled back, pressure relieved). When the toolpath transitions from an extruding move to a travel move exceeding a minimum distance threshold (typically 1.5–5 mm), the slicer injects a retraction command: a negative extrusion move at the configured retraction speed. Before resuming extrusion, an unretraction command (positive extrusion at the same distance) restores the filament position. The slicer must also manage z-hop or z-lift during travel moves, commanding a small upward z displacement (0.2–0.6 mm) to clear obstacles and prevent nozzle collisions with previously printed features.

Bridging detection relies on geometric analysis of the unsupported spans in each layer. The algorithm scans perimeter segments to identify those with no material in the layer below within a tolerance threshold. For each detected bridge segment, the slicer reduces the feedrate to the configured bridge speed and sets the fan speed to maximum. The direction of extrusion matters: the slicer prioritizes bridging moves that anchor at both ends, printing from one supported point to another, rather than cantilevered extrusions that start over open space. This directional preference is encoded by rotating the infill pattern or adjusting perimeter start points to align with the bridge's supporting geometry.

The minimum layer time constraint introduces conditional wait states and speed reductions. After generating the complete toolpath for a layer, the slicer sums the time required for all moves, computed as $t = \sum \frac{L_i}{v_i}$, where $L_i$ is the length of move $i$ and $v_i$ is its planned velocity. If this sum falls below the configured minimum layer time $t_{\text{min}}$, the slicer has two options: insert a dwell command (`G4`) at the end of the layer to pause for the difference, or uniformly scale down all feedrates on that layer to stretch the motion duration to $t_{\text{min}}$. The latter approach is generally preferred because it avoids stationary hotend dwell, which can create localized overheating or blemishes.

Dynamic speed modulation for thermal management requires distinguishing between feature types. Perimeters typically print at the baseline speed $v_{\text{perim}}$, but small perimeters—those with a total length below a threshold such as 6 mm—print at a reduced fraction, often 50–70% of $v_{\text{perim}}$, to prevent heat accumulation. External perimeters may receive an additional speed reduction (10–20%) to improve surface finish by allowing more precise dimensional control and better cooling. Infill speeds are set as a multiple of perimeter speed, commonly 1.5× to 2×, leveraging the fact that infill geometric tolerance is less critical and hidden from external view. Solid infill (top and bottom layers) often uses an intermediate speed between perimeter and sparse infill to balance surface quality with throughput.

The resulting G-code stream encodes this multi-layered motion plan as a sequence of `G1` (linear move) commands with X, Y, Z, E, and F parameters. A typical segment looks like:

```
G1 X105.342 Y87.234 E0.02145 F2400
```

This command moves the print head to position (105.342, 87.234) at a feedrate of 2400 mm/min (40 mm/s) while extruding 0.02145 mm of filament. The firmware receives this command, performs its own trapezoidal velocity planning to respect acceleration limits, computes pressure advance corrections, and generates step pulses for the stepper motors at microsecond intervals. The fidelity of the final printed part depends on the accuracy of every transform in this chain, from geometric slicing through motion planning to real-time firmware execution.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Achieving maximum throughput without sacrificing dimensional accuracy or surface quality requires systematic tuning of acceleration parameters, extrusion multipliers, and speed profiles. The primary lever for speed optimization is raising the maximum acceleration limit $a_{\text{max}}$, which allows the print head to reach target velocities faster and spend less time ramping between segments. However, this improvement has mechanical limits: excessive acceleration induces ringing (oscillatory artifacts radiating from corners) caused by elastic deformation in belts, frame flex, and toolhead mount compliance. The resonant frequency of these mechanical components, typically 30–80 Hz for Cartesian printers and 50–120 Hz for CoreXY designs, determines the safe acceleration ceiling. Empirical testing using accelerometer data or qualitative inspection of test prints with sharp corners reveals the onset of ringing, allowing the user to tune $a_{\text{max}}$ to the highest value before visible artifacts emerge.

Input shaping represents a firmware-level solution to ringing suppression, enabling higher accelerations without mechanical reinforcement. The technique convolves the commanded velocity profile with a shaping function—typically a zero-vibration (ZV) or multi-zero-vibration (MZV) impulse response—that cancels excitation at the printer's resonant frequency. Klipper firmware popularized this approach, requiring the user to characterize resonance by mounting an accelerometer (ADXL345 or similar) to the toolhead and executing sweep tests that identify peak frequencies. The measured resonance profile then parameterizes the input shaper, allowing acceleration limits to increase by 50–100% without inducing ringing. The cost is slight trajectory smoothing at high frequencies, which may soften fine details, requiring a tradeoff assessment for each print profile.

Jerk settings—more accurately termed junction deviation or instantaneous velocity change limits—control how aggressively the firmware permits velocity discontinuities at sharp corners. Traditional Marlin firmware uses jerk values (mm/s) for each axis, allowing instantaneous velocity changes up to that magnitude without enforced deceleration. Modern approaches like junction deviation compute permissible velocity changes from a maximum allowable deviation distance (typically 0.01–0.05 mm) and the corner geometry, producing smoother motion profiles that reduce stepper motor load and acoustic noise. Increasing junction deviation accelerates prints with many small segments (like circles approximated by short lines) but risks loss of corner sharpness on geometries requiring crisp edges.

Pressure advance calibration directly addresses extrusion consistency across velocity changes. The optimal $K$ value depends on filament compliance, Bowden tube length, hot end geometry, and nozzle diameter. A calibration print featuring a series of alternating fast and slow extrusions—such as a square tower with varying perimeter speeds—reveals under-extrusion (gaps at corners) or over-extrusion (bulges at corners). The user iteratively adjusts $K$ in increments of 0.01–0.02 mm until corners exhibit uniform width. For Bowden systems, $K$ values typically range from 0.3 to 0.8 mm, while direct-drive extruders achieve adequate compensation with $K$ between 0.03 and 0.15 mm. Some slicers permit per-filament $K$ values, recognizing that flexible filaments require higher compensation due to greater elasticity.

Volumetric flow rate imposes a hard ceiling on achievable speeds determined by hot end thermal capacity. The maximum volumetric throughput $Q_{\text{max}}$ (mm³/s) depends on heater power, melt zone geometry, and thermal conductivity of the hot end block. Standard all-metal hot ends with 40W heaters achieve 10–15 mm³/s with PLA, while high-flow designs (Volcano-style extended melt zones, CHC Pro with 80W+ heaters) reach 25–35 mm³/s. For a given line width $w$ and layer height $h$, the maximum speed follows from $v_{\text{max}} = \frac{Q_{\text{max}}}{w \cdot h}$. Exceeding this limit causes under-extrusion as the extruder cannot melt filament fast enough to maintain bead cross-section. Slicers with volumetric limiting features (PrusaSlicer, SuperSlicer) automatically reduce speeds on thick extrusions or wide infill to respect $Q_{\text{max}}$, trading time for consistency.

Cooling strategy tuning distinguishes between layer height regimes and material types. For PLA and PETG, aggressive part cooling (60–100% fan speed) improves overhang quality and bridge performance by rapidly solidifying extrudate. However, excessive cooling on the first layer or when printing high-temperature materials like ABS causes warping due to differential thermal contraction. A typical cooling strategy ramps fan speed from 0% on layer one to full speed by layer four, then modulates speed based on layer time: slow layers (high detail, small cross-sections) receive maximum cooling to prevent heat buildup, while fast layers (large infill areas) tolerate reduced cooling since the print head spends less time over each region. Bridging overrides trigger 100% fan speed regardless of default settings.

Retraction tuning prevents stringing while avoiding clogging and grinding. Excessive retraction distance pulls molten filament too far up the hot end's thermal gradient, allowing heat creep to soften filament in the cold zone and causing jams. Insufficient retraction leaves residual pressure that oozes during travel moves. The calibration process prints a series of spaced vertical pillars, gradually increasing retraction distance in 0.25 mm increments until stringing disappears. Retraction speed must balance responsiveness against mechanical stress: too slow and ooze continues during the retraction itself, too fast and the extruder gear chews into filament or skips steps. Direct-drive systems tolerate higher retraction speeds (40–60 mm/s) than Bowden setups, where excessive speed can induce filament buckling in the tube.

Failure modes in toolpath execution manifest as visible defects that trace back to specific planning or configuration errors. Corner bulging indicates insufficient pressure advance or excessive junction velocity, allowing over-extrusion as the head decelerates. Gaps at corners or seams signal under-extrusion caused by inadequate pressure advance or retraction recovery that falls short of re-priming the nozzle. Ringing artifacts radiating from features reveal acceleration settings exceeding the printer's mechanical rigidity or absent input shaping. Stringing between features points to inadequate retraction distance or travel moves through already-printed geometry without z-hop. Layer shifting indicates skipped stepper steps from excessive acceleration, belt slippage, or pulley set-screw loosening. Dimensional inaccuracy in the X-Y plane suggests improper extrusion multiplier calibration or shrinkage compensation.

Sagging bridges and failed overhangs result from insufficient cooling, excessive speed, or poor extrusion temperature control. Lowering bridge speed to 15–25 mm/s and ensuring 100% fan speed addresses most cases, but some materials (nylon, polycarbonate) require slower speeds or support structures due to inherently high melt viscosity. Pillowing or rough top surfaces on solid infill signal insufficient top layer count or inadequate cooling on slow layers, allowing trapped heat to soften the surface before the next layer adheres.

Thermal runaway on small features occurs when layer time drops below the minimum threshold without triggering adequate speed reduction or wait insertion. The slicer's minimum layer time logic must be verified for complex geometries with tall narrow sections, ensuring that the print head does not deposit heat faster than the part can dissipate it. Warping and layer adhesion failures on large prints trace to inconsistent bed temperature, ambient drafts, or material-specific thermal contraction exceeding the adhesion capacity of the first layer bond.

Toolpath optimization for multi-material or multi-extruder prints introduces tower management and wipe strategies. Prime towers consume extra material but ensure each tool change delivers fully primed extrudate, critical for clean color transitions or soluble support interfaces. The slicer must balance prime tower volume against print time, placing the tower to minimize travel distance while avoiding collisions with the printed part. Wipe-into-object and wipe-into-infill strategies reduce waste by purging transition material into sacrificial regions, but require careful sequencing to avoid contaminating visible surfaces.

Advanced slicers expose scripting interfaces (post-processing plugins in Cura, custom G-code insertion points in PrusaSlicer) allowing users to inject conditional logic, dynamic parameter adjustments, or external sensor feedback into the toolpath. For example, a script can insert temperature changes based on layer height, adjust fan speed in response to chamber humidity sensors, or trigger enclosure venting when printing materials that emit volatile compounds. These customizations extend the slicer's static toolpath generation into adaptive real-time control, bridging the gap between pre-computed planning and the dynamic physical reality of the printing process.
