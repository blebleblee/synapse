# Lesson 3: Motion Systems

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The quality of every fused deposition modeling print is fundamentally constrained by the mechanical precision and dynamic behavior of its motion system. While enthusiasts often fixate on nozzle diameter, layer height, or filament chemistry, the underlying kinematic architecture—the arrangement of actuators, linkages, and control algorithms that translate G-code coordinates into physical toolhead displacement—determines the ultimate resolution, repeatability, and speed ceiling of the machine. A printer is, at its mechanical core, a three-axis robotic manipulator tasked with positioning a heated extruder within a volumetric workspace to sub-hundred-micron tolerances while executing rapid direction changes thousands of times per layer. This demands an understanding not merely of static geometry but of the dynamic interplay between inertia, motor torque delivery, structural compliance, and the computational motion planning that governs acceleration profiles.

Three dominant kinematic paradigms have emerged in desktop FDM: Cartesian systems with independently actuated orthogonal axes, delta robots employing parallel kinematics through radially arranged arms, and CoreXY implementations that decouple motor placement from toolhead mass through crossed belt paths. Each architecture embodies distinct tradeoffs in mechanical simplicity, achievable acceleration, print volume efficiency, and susceptibility to cumulative positioning error. The Cartesian bed-slinger, moving the build platform in Y while an X-carriage traverses a vertical Z-axis gantry, leverages intuitive coordinate mapping and simple belt tension management at the cost of asymmetric inertial loading—the bed's mass penalizes Y-axis acceleration far more severely than the lightweight toolhead constrains X-axis dynamics. The delta printer inverts this relationship entirely, suspending a lightweight effector from three independently controlled vertical carriages whose combined motion through trigonometric forward and inverse kinematics enables rapid toolhead repositioning within a cylindrical or conical volume, trading mechanical complexity and non-Euclidean calibration challenges for exceptional speed potential on small parts with minimal directional preference in acceleration capacity.

CoreXY systems represent a synthesis strategy: by routing two continuous belts in a crossed configuration where each motor contributes to motion along both horizontal axes through precisely choreographed coordination, the architecture isolates all heavy actuators to the frame while permitting the XY gantry to carry only the extruder assembly. The kinematic transformation is elegant—motor A advancing while motor B reverses produces pure X-axis translation; both motors rotating in unison drive Y-axis motion—but the belt path topology introduces mechanical coupling that transforms any tension asymmetry or pulley eccentricity into correlated positioning artifacts across both axes simultaneously. Understanding these architectural distinctions at a visceral level requires grappling with the physics of rotational inertia, the torque-speed curves of stepper motors under resistive load, and the computational constraints of real-time motion planning algorithms executing on eight-bit microcontrollers clocked at sixteen megahertz.

The stepper motor itself, ubiquitous in desktop FDM for its open-loop position holding and lack of encoder feedback requirement, operates through sequential electromagnetic coil energization that advances a toothed rotor in discrete angular increments—typically 1.8 degrees per full step, yielding two hundred steps per revolution. Microstepping drivers interpolate intermediate positions by energizing adjacent coils with sinusoidally varying current ratios, subdividing each full step into sixteen, thirty-two, or even two hundred fifty-six microsteps to smooth motion and reduce resonance. However, this electronic subdivision does not proportionally increase holding torque; the static torque specification quoted at full-step energization degrades precipitously as rotational velocity increases due to back-EMF voltage that opposes the driver's ability to establish coil current rapidly. The resulting torque-speed curve decays hyperbolically—a NEMA 17 motor rated for 4.0 Newton-meters holding torque at standstill may deliver only 0.8 Nm at 600 RPM, constraining achievable linear carriage velocity through the geometric relationship between pulley diameter and belt pitch.

Acceleration limits arise not from steady-state velocity constraints but from the motor's ability to overcome combined rotational inertia of the rotor, pulley, and reflected linear carriage mass during rapid direction reversals. Newton's second law governs this relationship with brutal simplicity: the angular acceleration alpha achievable by a motor delivering torque tau against moment of inertia I is given by the rotational form $\tau = I\alpha$, with linear acceleration at the belt related through the pulley radius. Every additional gram added to a toolhead assembly—a direct-drive extruder upgrade, an accelerometer mount, an all-metal hotend—subtracts from available acceleration margin before the motor begins skipping steps, the catastrophic loss of position tracking that manifests as layer shifts in the printed object. This is why delta printers, with effector assemblies often weighing under 150 grams compared to 400-gram direct-drive Cartesian toolheads, can sustain print accelerations exceeding 5000 mm/s² where bed-slingers struggle past 1500 mm/s².

Jerk, the rate of change of acceleration, enters the motion planning domain as a computational necessity rather than a pure physical phenomenon. Microcontroller firmware like Marlin or Klipper must decompose arbitrary G-code toolpaths—potentially containing tens of thousands of short linear segments approximating curves—into trapezoidal or S-curve velocity profiles that respect maximum velocity, acceleration, and crucially, the instantaneous velocity change permitted at segment boundaries without decelerating to zero. This instantaneous velocity discontinuity, termed jerk in the RepRap community despite its dimensional mismatch with the calculus definition, prevents the motion planner from grinding to a halt at every G1 line segment transition. A jerk value of 8 mm/s permits the toolhead to corner at 8 mm/s without deceleration; higher values enable faster printing of detailed geometry but amplify mechanical ringing as the abrupt direction change excites structural resonances in the frame and gantry assembly.

Mechanical hysteresis, the path-dependent error between commanded and actual position arising from cumulative backlash in couplers, belt tooth engagement slop, and elastic compliance in frame members, imposes a fundamental limit on dimensional accuracy that no amount of microstepping can resolve. A belt-driven axis with 0.1 mm of backlash—the clearance between belt tooth and pulley tooth—will exhibit bidirectional positioning error of that magnitude, manifesting as dimensional inconsistency between features printed in opposing directions. Leadscrew-driven Z-axes, while offering superior backlash characteristics through anti-backlash nuts with spring-loaded split threads, introduce periodic error at the screw pitch frequency due to manufacturing imperfections that modulate layer height with each revolution. Recognizing and compensating for these mechanical realities through calibration, component selection, and strategic print orientation separates functional prototypes from dimensionally verified mechanical components.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

### Cartesian Kinematics: Orthogonal Independence and Asymmetric Inertia

The Cartesian architecture maps machine coordinates to actuator displacement with perfect linearity: a commanded 10 mm motion in X-axis translates to precisely 10 mm of stepper motor rotation transmitted through a timing belt or leadscrew with no trigonometric transformation. The firmware kinematic module requires no coordinate system conversion; the G-code position vector directly specifies motor target positions after scaling by steps-per-millimeter constants derived from pulley tooth count and microstepping configuration. For a GT2 timing belt with 2 mm tooth pitch engaging a twenty-tooth pulley on a 1.8-degree stepper at sixteen-times microstepping, the relationship crystallizes to 3200 microsteps per motor revolution yielding 40 mm linear travel, or exactly 80 steps per millimeter.

This mechanical directness simplifies calibration and troubleshooting—dimensional error in X manifests exclusively from X-axis mechanical issues—but imposes asymmetric mass distribution. In the predominant bed-slinger topology, the heated build platform constitutes the Y-axis moving mass, often exceeding two kilograms when supporting a cast aluminum tooling plate and borosilicate glass print surface. The X-axis carriage traversing horizontally carries only the extruder assembly, perhaps 400 grams. This mass disparity produces profoundly different dynamic responses: the X-axis sustains 3000 mm/s² acceleration comfortably while the Y-axis, moving five times the inertia, struggles past 1200 mm/s² before belt tension losses or motor skipping intervene. Printing a circular perimeter thus proceeds in alternating fast and slow quadrants as the slicer's motion planner oscillates between X-dominant and Y-dominant segments, creating visible artifacts at the quarter-circle transition points where velocity profiles mismatch.

The Z-axis typically employs leadscrews rather than belts, trading speed for holding torque and elimination of gravity-induced bed drop. A TR8×8 trapezoidal leadscrew with 8 mm pitch advances the bed 8 mm per motor revolution, but the kinematic disadvantage ratio means a NEMA 17 motor delivering 0.4 Nm must overcome not just the bed weight but also frictional drag in linear bearings multiplied by the mechanical advantage. The leadscrew pitch selection becomes a studied compromise: finer pitches like TR8×2 offer superior resolution and torque multiplication but slow layer transitions; coarser 8 mm pitch variants permit rapid Z-hops for retraction strategies but approach the motor's torque ceiling when supporting heavy bed assemblies. Anti-backlash nuts address play through spring-loaded split threads that press against opposing screw flanks, but this preload induces periodic binding that manifests as Z-axis banding—visible horizontal lines repeating at the leadscrew pitch frequency caused by varying frictional resistance during rotation.

Belt tensioning in Cartesian systems directly determines positional accuracy and maximum achievable acceleration. Undertensioned belts permit tooth skipping under high acceleration, while overtensioning loads bearings and induces motor shaft bending. The target tension lies in the range producing 30-40 Hz resonant frequency when the belt span is plucked like a guitar string, measured with a spectrum analyzer or smartphone tuner application. Gates Corporation technical documentation specifies 4-6 pounds tension force for GT2 belts in hobby applications, verifiable with a luggage scale pulling perpendicular to the belt at mid-span and measuring deflection. Tension asymmetry between parallel Z-axis leadscrews causes gantry tilt, diagnosed through dual independent Z-motor drivers that permit software-corrected tramming by commanding differential microsteps to level the X-axis carriage relative to the bed plane.

### Delta Kinematics: Parallel Linkages and Trigonometric Transformation

Delta printers achieve toolhead positioning through the coordinated vertical motion of three carriages, each traveling along a vertical linear rail arranged at 120-degree radial intervals around the build volume perimeter. Six parallel connecting rods, joined through ball joints or magnetic rotary pivots, link the carriages to a central effector platform carrying the hotend. The forward kinematic problem—determining effector position from known carriage coordinates—and the inverse problem—computing required carriage positions for a desired effector coordinate—both demand iterative trigonometric solution of the coupled constraint equations relating rod lengths, tower positions, and effector location in three-dimensional space.

The inverse kinematics solution underpinning every firmware move command treats each tower independently. For a target effector position $(x, y, z)$ in Cartesian workspace coordinates, the firmware must compute the required height $h_i$ for each of the three carriages $i \in \{A, B, C\}$. The geometric constraint emerges from the constant rod length $L$: the Euclidean distance from carriage $i$ at tower position $(t_{xi}, t_{yi}, h_i)$ to the effector ball joint position $(x + e_x, y + e_y, z)$ must equal $L$, where $(e_x, e_y)$ represents the effector radius offset for that particular rod attachment point. This yields a quadratic equation in $h_i$:

$$h_i = z + \sqrt{L^2 - (x + e_x - t_{xi})^2 - (y + e_y - t_{yi})^2}$$

Solving three such equations every motion planning cycle transforms a G1 X10 Y10 command into coordinated vertical carriage displacements: perhaps tower A descends 2.8 mm, tower B ascends 4.1 mm, and tower C descends 1.9 mm to position the effector at the commanded location. Calibration complexity explodes compared to Cartesian systems because dimensional errors in any parameter—tower radius, rod length, effector offset, individual tower verticality—couple into all three axes simultaneously. A 0.5 mm error in measured rod length produces radial position error proportional to effector distance from center, creating the characteristic "bowl-shaped" dimensional distortion where prints measure correctly at center but contract toward edges.

The parallel kinematic structure yields exceptional acceleration potential by minimizing moving mass. Effector assemblies often achieve 100-150 gram total weight, permitting 8000 mm/s² acceleration before belt tension or motor torque limits intervene. However, the workspace topology shifts from a rectangular prism to a cylindrical or conical envelope, with usable volume efficiency dropping to approximately 40% of the enclosing cube compared to 85-90% for Cartesian machines. The trigonometric transformation also introduces non-uniform positioning resolution: effector position sensitivity to carriage microstep changes varies with workspace location, finest at build volume edges where the rods approach vertical orientation and coarsest near center where they angle outward. This geometric dilution demands higher microstepping ratios—often 128× or 256×—to maintain sub-50 micron positioning resolution throughout the workspace.

Delta calibration procedures leverage automated probing routines that measure effector height at concentric rings of radial test points, fitting the measured error surface to the parameterized kinematic model through least-squares optimization. The firmware adjusts endstop positions, tower radius, rod length, and individual tower angular offsets iteratively until residual position error across the bed surface minimizes, typically requiring three to five probe-adjust-reprobe cycles to converge within ±0.05 mm tolerance. Magnetic ball joints at rod ends eliminate friction and binding present in traditional ball-and-socket joints, but their limited angular range restricts maximum effector tilt, complicating designs that attempt to incorporate belt-driven XY motion atop the delta platform for hybrid kinematics.

### CoreXY Mechanics: Crossed Belt Topology and Motor Coordination

The CoreXY kinematic arrangement decouples motor mass from toolhead motion through an elegant belt routing topology where two continuous belts cross at the moving carriage, with all four belt endpoints and both motors anchored to the stationary frame. The belts follow a specific path: belt A routes from motor A to an upper corner pulley, crosses diagonally to engage the carriage, continues to the opposite corner pulley, and returns to motor A's second pulley shaft. Belt B follows a mirrored path, crossing belt A at precisely ninety degrees at the carriage intersection point. When both motors rotate in the same direction and at identical velocities, their contributions sum to produce pure Y-axis translation; when the motors counter-rotate at equal rates, their opposing X-components cancel and Y-components subtract, yielding pure X-axis motion.

The mathematical relationship formalizes through matrix transformation. Define motor angular positions as $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$ in radians, and pulley radius as $r$. The carriage position $(x, y)$ relates through:

$$x = r(\theta_A - \theta_B)$$
$$y = r(\theta_A + \theta_B)$$

Inverting this relationship for motion planning yields the required motor rotations for a desired displacement:

$$\theta_A = \frac{x + y}{2r}$$
$$\theta_B = \frac{y - x}{2r}$$

This coupling means any tension imbalance, pulley eccentricity, or belt stretch affects both axes simultaneously in characteristic diagonal patterning. A loose A-motor belt manifests not as pure X-axis position error but as correlated XY drift at forty-five degrees to the primary axes. Calibration demands meticulous belt tension matching, verified by commanding large alternating X-only and Y-only moves while measuring carriage displacement with a dial indicator; discrepancies between commanded and actual travel indicate unequal belt contributions.

The mechanical advantage of CoreXY emerges in its lightweight gantry. With motors residing on the frame, the XY carriage need carry only the extruder assembly, achieving moving masses comparable to delta effectors—150 to 300 grams depending on direct versus Bowden extrusion—while retaining Cartesian's rectangular workspace and simple calibration. This permits acceleration settings rivaling deltas, typically 4000-6000 mm/s², within a mechanically simpler frame requiring no trigonometric transformation overhead. However, the crossed belt path introduces torsional loading on the carriage; if the belts do not intersect at perfectly perpendicular angles due to frame racking or pulley misalignment, the carriage experiences rotational torque that must be resisted by linear rail preload, inducing premature bearing wear and periodic positioning error.

The Z-axis in CoreXY implementations almost universally employs triple or quad leadscrew configuration with independent motors linked through belt synchronization or individual driver control. Three leadscrews arranged in triangular geometry self-compensate for minor frame twisting during thermal expansion, while four-point systems demand active bed leveling through per-corner Z-offset mesh compensation. The bed assembly remains stationary, eliminating Y-axis inertia penalties but requiring taller frame construction to accommodate the elevated XY gantry and sufficient Z-travel beneath it. Automatic bed leveling via inductive or capacitive proximity probes measures the build surface topology in a grid, storing height deviations in firmware memory and applying real-time Z-axis corrections during the first few layers to compensate for bed tilt or surface non-flatness up to ±0.5 mm.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

### Stepper Motor Torque Delivery and Driver Tuning

Achieving maximum acceleration within the stepper motor's torque envelope demands precise current limiting and microstepping configuration at the driver level. Modern TMC2209 or TMC5160 drivers employ chopper-mode current regulation, rapidly switching MOSFET drive to the motor coils to maintain target RMS current even as back-EMF rises with velocity. The tunable parameters—run current, hold current, and chopper off-time—directly determine available torque and thermal dissipation. Run current, typically set to 80-90% of the motor's rated current to provide thermal margin, should be calculated from the motor's coil resistance and inductance using the driver's current sense resistor value:

$$I_{\text{RMS}} = \frac{V_{\text{ref}}}{8 \times R_{\text{sense}}}$$

For a TMC2209 with 0.11-ohm sense resistors, a reference voltage of 1.2V yields approximately 1.36A RMS current, appropriate for a NEMA 17 motor rated at 1.5A. Hold current, active when the motor is stationary, can be reduced to 50-70% of run current to minimize heat generation during idle periods, but excessive reduction permits rotor drift under carriage weight, manifesting as Z-axis layer offset when the gantry settles between moves.

Microstepping interpolation resolution trades positioning smoothness against computational overhead and diminishing torque returns. Full-step energization delivers maximum holding torque but produces coarse 1.8-degree rotation increments and pronounced vibration at low velocities. Sixteen-times microstepping provides adequate 0.1125-degree resolution for most applications while maintaining approximately 98% of full-step torque. Advancing to 256× microstepping yields silky motion and eliminates audible resonance but drops torque availability to roughly 70% of full-step rating and burdens the microcontroller's step pulse generation with sixteen-fold higher interrupt frequency. For high-acceleration CoreXY systems pushing 6000 mm/s², the computational load of maintaining 256× microstepping on all four motors (X, Y, dual Z) at peak velocity can saturate an 8-bit AVR processor, causing stutter and position loss; such configurations mandate 32-bit ARM controllers operating at 120+ MHz to sustain real-time step generation.

Dynamic torque fade as velocity increases necessitates acceleration limit tuning through empirical testing rather than theoretical calculation. The procedure involves commanding progressively higher acceleration values while printing a single-perimeter cube with sharp ninety-degree corners; the acceleration ceiling reveals itself when corners begin rounding due to motor stall-induced position loss, or when the characteristic grinding noise of skipped steps emerges. Logging motor current during these test prints using a current clamp ammeter probe on the motor phase wires identifies the velocity threshold where current demand exceeds driver supply capacity, visible as a plateau in the current waveform despite increasing commanded velocity. Velocity-dependent acceleration scaling in firmware, configured through M204 commands in Marlin or acceleration_to_deceleration ratios in Klipper, reduces acceleration automatically as velocity increases to remain within the torque curve envelope.

### Jerk Tuning and Motion Planning Pathologies

The jerk value, despite its colloquial misnomer, functions as a motion planning gate that permits instantaneous velocity changes below its threshold without forcing deceleration to cornering speed. In a toolpath containing hundreds of short segments approximating a circular arc, excessively conservative jerk settings compel the planner to decelerate at every segment junction, transforming what should be continuous motion into a stuttering crawl that destroys surface finish through periodic flow rate oscillations. The mathematical impact crystallizes when examining trapezoidal velocity profiles: for two consecutive segments with entry velocity $v_1$ and exit velocity $v_2$, if their vector difference exceeds the jerk limit, the planner inserts deceleration and acceleration phases between them, extending move duration and introducing bulging at corners where molten filament deposits during the dwell period.

Optimal jerk tuning begins with conservative values—5 mm/s for X and Y, 0.4 mm/s for Z, 2 mm/s for extruder—then incrementally increases while printing a complex curved geometry such as a hollow cylinder with 1 mm wall thickness. The jerk ceiling manifests as visible ringing artifacts: sinusoidal ripples radiating from corners where the abrupt direction change excites the frame's mechanical resonance. Modern firmware implementations including Klipper's input shaping feature actively compensate for these resonances by pre-distorting the acceleration profile to produce destructive interference with the frame's natural frequency response, permitting jerk values two to three times higher than mechanically tolerable through open-loop control.

Junction deviation, an alternative motion planning algorithm available in Marlin and standard in Klipper, replaces jerk with a geometric approach that permits velocity through corners based on the deviation distance tolerable between the idealized sharp corner and the actual rounded path the toolhead will follow. A junction deviation value of 0.05 mm permits cornering at whatever velocity maintains the toolhead within 50 microns of the theoretical intersection point, automatically adapting speed to corner acuity—sharp ninety-degree turns impose greater velocity reduction than shallow angular deviations. This approach eliminates the artificial distinction between jerk and acceleration, producing smoother overall motion profiles particularly in organic curved geometries where constant-velocity cornering better matches the physical constraints.

S-curve acceleration, implemented through configurable jerk derivative limits, further smooths the velocity profile by ramping acceleration itself rather than applying it instantaneously. The traditional trapezoidal profile transitions from zero to maximum acceleration in a single time step, exciting high-frequency vibrations; S-curve profiles apply a constant jerk (true calculus definition: derivative of acceleration) during the transition, yielding a velocity-time graph with smooth rather than kinked transitions. The tunable parameter, maximum jerk in mm/s³, determines how rapidly acceleration can change; values around 10,000 mm/s³ provide perceptible smoothing without excessively extending move duration. However, the computational overhead of calculating cubic rather than linear velocity curves burdens firmware execution, and overly conservative jerk values cause motion to bog down in busy toolpaths, transforming what should be fluid motion into sluggish ramp-up.

### Mechanical Hysteresis Compensation and Frame Rigidity

Backlash, the clearance between mating mechanical components permitting bidirectional play, accumulates from belt tooth engagement, bearing slop, pulley bore tolerance, and shaft coupling flex. A GT2 belt engaging a printed PLA pulley exhibits roughly 0.08 mm of tooth-to-tooth clearance; adding 0.04 mm from bearing radial play and 0.03 mm from shaft-to-pulley-bore tolerance yields cumulative single-axis backlash approaching 0.15 mm. This manifests as dimensional error between features printed in opposing travel directions—a hole measured in X+ direction may center 0.15 mm away from a boss measured in X- direction, despite both being geometrically concentric in the CAD model.

Mechanical compensation begins with component selection: substituting machined aluminum pulleys with precision-bored H7 tolerance holes for printed variants eliminates 0.03 mm immediately; upgrading to proper F623 flange bearings with 5 micron radial runout rather than generic 623 skateboard bearings reclaims another 0.02 mm. Belt tension must reside in the optimal 30-40 Hz frequency window—undertensioning permits tooth skipping that accumulates position error, while overtensioning preloads bearings inducing premature wear and periodic binding that modulates layer height. Verifying tension uniformity across parallel belts in CoreXY systems demands commanding diagonal moves while measuring actual displacement; unequal tensions cause the carriage to drift perpendicular to the commanded path, visible as parallelogram skew in printed rectangles.

Software compensation for measured backlash, available through firmware backlash correction in Marlin via M425 commands, applies corrective microsteps when reversing direction to pre-load the mechanical system against the backlash clearance before resuming normal motion. The correction amount must be empirically determined by printing bidirectional test patterns—arrays of small cylinders with half printed during X+ travel and half during X− travel—then measuring positional offset between the two sets with digital calipers. However, backlash compensation introduces its own artifacts: the corrective pre-loading pulse appears as a brief velocity discontinuity potentially visible as a surface mark, and if the backlash value changes with temperature or bearing wear, the fixed compensation value diverges from mechanical reality, progressively introducing error rather than removing it.

Frame rigidity fundamentally limits achievable precision through deflection under motor torque and toolhead inertia. Aluminum extrusion frames, ubiquitous in hobby printers, exhibit lateral bending when subjected to the reaction forces of high acceleration; a 500 mm unsupported vertical 2020 extrusion carrying a Y-axis motor torquing against 5 kg bed mass at 1500 mm/s² experiences measurable flexure. Diagonal cross-bracing, corner gussets, and enclosed frame topologies transform the structure from a collection of independent beams into a truss network distributing loads through triangulated geometry. The quantitative target: frame deflection under maximum motor torque should remain below 0.05 mm, verifiable by clamping a dial indicator to the frame and commanding maximum acceleration moves while monitoring deflection magnitude.

Linear rail preload adjustment, particularly critical in CoreXY systems where belt forces induce torsional carriage loading, must balance low rolling resistance against elimination of play. MGN12H rails with medium preload provide approximately 20 micron radial clearance, sufficient for smooth motion while constraining off-axis deflection below positioning resolution. Excessive preload generates friction that masquerades as increased motor load, forcing higher current settings that accelerate stepper overheating; insufficient preload permits the carriage to rock on its rail during direction reversals, manifesting as vertical layer line artifacts at walls where travel direction reverses after perimeter printing. The empirical test: with motors disabled, the carriage should glide smoothly under gentle push with no perceptible play when attempting to tilt it perpendicular to the rail axis.

Resonance tuning through accelerometer-based frequency analysis, pioneered by Klipper's input shaping module, identifies the frame's natural frequencies by commanding impulse accelerations while an ADXL345 three-axis accelerometer mounted to the toolhead records resulting vibrations. Fast Fourier transform of the logged acceleration data reveals peaks in the frequency spectrum corresponding to structural resonance modes, typically 30-80 Hz for desktop printers. Configuring input shaping with matched filter profiles—zero-vibration (ZV), modified zero-vibration (MZV), or extra-insensitive (EI)—pre-distorts commanded acceleration to produce destructive interference with these resonant modes, permitting 50-100% higher acceleration and jerk values without inducing ringing artifacts. The tradeoff manifests as slight corner rounding from the filter's smoothing effect, quantified as the filter's vibration tolerance parameter; MZV filters provide excellent ringing suppression with minimal smoothing for single-mode resonances, while EI filters handle dual-mode responses at the cost of more pronounced corner softening.
