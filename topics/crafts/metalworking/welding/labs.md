# Operational Verification Labs: Welding

## Objective & Operational Lab Setup

The operational verification laboratory for welding establishes a controlled environment where weld quality, structural integrity, and procedural consistency can be tested against first-principles metallurgical behavior and industry acceptance criteria. Unlike production welding where throughput and visual inspection dominate, verification labs prioritize destructive testing, parametric measurement, and failure analysis to build physical intuition about heat-affected zone (HAZ) transformation, penetration depth as a function of arc energy input, and the relationship between interpass temperature control and residual stress distribution.

Your laboratory bench must accommodate both the welding execution station and the analytical measurement station. The welding station requires adequate ventilation with minimum 100 cubic feet per minute exhaust capture at the arc point, a stable work surface with copper backing bars for heat dissipation during test coupon welding, and calibrated power supplies with digital amperage and voltage readout accurate to within two percent. For gas metal arc welding (GMAW/MIG) and gas tungsten arc welding (GTAG/TIG) processes, you will need regulated shielding gas delivery systems with flow meters calibrated in cubic feet per hour or liters per minute, and you must verify gas purity specifications—argon at 99.996% minimum for aluminum TIG work, and 75% argon / 25% CO₂ blends for carbon steel MIG applications.

The analytical measurement station must include a calibrated dial caliper or digital micrometer with 0.01 mm resolution for bead geometry measurement, a properly calibrated type-K thermocouple with data logging capability to capture interpass temperature profiles, and a metallurgical macro-etch station consisting of ferric chloride solution (100g FeCl₃ per liter distilled water) for revealing weld fusion boundaries and HAZ extent on polished cross-sections. Hardness testing equipment—either a calibrated Rockwell hardness tester or a portable Leeb rebound hardness device—allows you to map hardness traverses across the weld zone, HAZ, and base metal to quantify the thermal cycle's effect on material properties. For destructive bend testing, you need a hydraulic or arbor press with a roller die set conforming to AWS B4.0 dimensional specifications, typically a 38 mm diameter mandrel for 6–10 mm plate thickness test coupons.

Material procurement for verification testing requires careful specification. Purchase hot-rolled ASTM A36 carbon steel plate in 6 mm and 10 mm thickness, cut into 150 mm × 50 mm coupons with sheared or plasma-cut edges deburred with a flap disc to remove scale and oxide. For austenitic stainless steel verification, obtain 304L or 316L plate in similar dimensions. Aluminum alloy testing demands 6061-T6 plate with similar geometry. Filler metal selection must match base metal chemistry and intended service conditions—ER70S-6 solid wire for carbon steel MIG work with 0.9 mm diameter, ER308L stainless wire for austenitic joints, and ER4043 aluminum wire for 6061 alloy welding. For TIG processes, procure 2.4 mm and 3.2 mm diameter filler rods in matching chemistries, and ensure tungsten electrodes are 2% lanthanated or 2% ceriated for contamination-free arc initiation.

Calibration and baseline establishment precedes all verification testing. Record ambient temperature and relative humidity, as these affect shielding gas behavior and electrode conditioning. Verify power source output by running a bead-on-plate weld at nominal parameters—for example, 140 amperes at 18.5 volts for short-circuit transfer MIG on 6 mm carbon steel—and measure the resulting bead width, height, and penetration depth on a cross-sectioned sample. This baseline allows you to detect equipment drift or consumable variability during subsequent parametric studies. Establish your personal arc strike and travel speed consistency by welding multiple beads at constant machine settings and measuring the coefficient of variation in bead geometry; competent manual welders achieve less than 8% variation in bead width across replicate passes.

## Exercise 1: Foundational Practical Implementation

The foundational verification exercise establishes your capability to produce a reference-quality groove weld in carbon steel plate, document the complete thermal and geometric measurement protocol, and perform destructive bend testing to verify internal soundness according to AWS D1.1 structural welding code acceptance criteria. This exercise integrates arc manipulation, thermal management, and post-weld analytical techniques into a complete verification cycle that will serve as the template for all subsequent parametric investigations.

Begin by preparing two 150 mm × 50 mm × 10 mm A36 carbon steel coupons with a single-V groove preparation. The groove geometry must be machined or ground to create a 60-degree included angle with a 2 mm root face and a 3 mm root opening. This geometry provides adequate access for electrode manipulation while maintaining sufficient root reinforcement to prevent burn-through. Deburr all edges with 80-grit abrasive and clean both groove faces and the backside within 25 mm of the joint line using acetone or denatured alcohol to remove oils, then follow with mechanical abrasion using a clean stainless steel wire brush dedicated exclusively to carbon steel work to prevent copper contamination.

Tack weld the assembly in three locations—one at each end positioned 10 mm from the edge, and one at the center. Each tack should be 15 mm long, welded at 120 amperes with the same GMAW short-circuit transfer process you will use for the root pass. Allow each tack to cool below 150°C before placing the next tack to prevent angular distortion from sequential heating. Verify the root opening remains within 3±0.5 mm after tacking by measuring with feeler gauges at five points along the joint length. Mount the tacked assembly on a copper backing bar with the root face downward, and clamp securely to the welding table to prevent movement during thermal expansion.

Set your GMAW power source to constant voltage mode with 18.5 volts output and wire feed speed corresponding to 140 amperes for 0.9 mm diameter ER70S-6 wire, typically 4.2 meters per minute depending on manufacturer-specific contact tip efficiency. Set shielding gas flow to 16 liters per minute for the 75/25 argon-CO₂ mixture, verified with a rotameter-type flow gauge by purging into atmosphere with the gun trigger held open. Preheat the assembly to 100°C using a propane torch, measuring with the thermocouple probe at multiple points to ensure uniform temperature distribution, then begin the root pass.

Position the contact tip 12–15 mm from the work surface and establish a 10-degree push angle in the direction of travel. Initiate the arc on the leading edge of the groove, allowing the first 8–10 mm of weld to establish a molten pool before beginning forward travel. Maintain a travel speed of approximately 4 mm per second, adjusting dynamically to keep the weld pool trailing the arc by one pool diameter and to ensure the pool wets into both sidewalls and the root face simultaneously. Listen for the characteristic bacon-frying sound of short-circuit transfer occurring at 90–120 Hz; deviation to a hissing or spattering sound indicates excessive voltage or inadequate wire feed speed. Complete the root pass in a single continuous run, terminating by releasing the trigger while maintaining forward motion for 10 mm to fill the crater, then immediately returning to deposit additional filler metal into the crater to bring it flush with the surrounding bead.

Allow the root pass to cool to below 150°C, verified by thermocouple measurement or by the ability to hold your bare hand on the plate 25 mm from the weld for five seconds without discomfort. This interpass temperature control prevents grain coarsening in the HAZ and limits the peak temperature excursion during subsequent passes. Remove spatter and light slag from the root pass surface using a chipping hammer and wire brush, then inspect the root bead profile. You should observe complete fusion to both sidewalls with no undercut deeper than 0.5 mm, a bead width of 8–10 mm, and reinforcement height of 1–2 mm above the plate surface.

Deposit the fill and cap passes using the same travel technique but increase the current to 160 amperes and voltage to 19.5 volts to accommodate the larger weld pool volume required to fill the groove. Weave the electrode in a slight side-to-side oscillation with 2–3 second pause at each toe to ensure complete sidewall fusion and prevent undercut. Deposit the first fill pass along one side of the groove, the second fill pass along the opposite side in a stringer bead technique, then deposit a final cap pass with slight weaving to produce a convex face profile with 1–2 mm reinforcement. Measure interpass temperature before each pass and maintain the 100–150°C window throughout the welding sequence.

After the final pass cools to ambient temperature, visually inspect the completed weld for surface discontinuities. Acceptable welds exhibit uniform ripple spacing of 2–4 mm, smooth transition from weld face to base metal toes without undercut, and no surface porosity, cracks, or slag inclusions. Measure the completed weld geometry at three cross-sections by cutting transverse slices at 40 mm, 75 mm, and 110 mm from the starting end using a band saw with cutting fluid to prevent thermal damage. Mount each slice in a cold-mounting epoxy resin system, then grind the cross-section face progressively through 120, 240, 400, and 600 grit silicon carbide papers on a rotary lap using water lubrication. Polish the ground surface on a felt lap with 1 micron diamond suspension to remove grinding scratches and reveal the weld metal microstructure under low-magnification optical examination.

Macro-etch each polished cross-section by swabbing with ferric chloride solution for 30–90 seconds until the fusion boundary and HAZ become clearly visible as darker-etching regions due to differential attack rates between the weld metal, HAZ microstructures, and unaffected base metal. Photograph each macro-etched section using a digital camera with scale reference, then measure fusion penetration depth from the original plate top surface to the deepest point of weld metal penetration into the base metal. Full penetration through the 10 mm plate thickness with 1–2 mm root reinforcement on the backing bar side confirms adequate heat input and travel speed control. Measure the HAZ width on both sides of the fusion boundary; typical values for single-pass GMAW of carbon steel range from 2–4 mm, corresponding to the region that experienced peak temperatures between the lower critical transformation temperature of 723°C and the fusion temperature of approximately 1500°C.

Perform a transverse face bend test on one of the remaining unwelded sections according to AWS B4.0 procedure. Cut a specimen 38 mm wide using a band saw, then remove the weld reinforcement by grinding flush with the plate surface to prevent stress concentration at the weld toes during bending. Mark the weld centerline on both faces for alignment reference. Place the specimen in the bend test jig with the weld face in tension (convex side against the mandrel) and apply steady force through the plunger until the specimen forms a U-shape around the 38 mm diameter mandrel with the legs parallel. Remove the bent specimen and examine the outer tension surface under magnification. AWS D1.1 acceptance criteria permit no cracks or other discontinuities exceeding 3 mm in any dimension, except for corner cracks that do not exceed 6 mm measured in any direction. Uniformly dispersed micro-cracking at the weld toes is acceptable if individual cracks do not exceed these dimensions.

Record all measurements in a structured laboratory notebook with the following data fields: base metal specification and thickness, filler metal specification and diameter, shielding gas composition and flow rate, preheat and interpass temperatures, welding current and voltage for each pass, travel speed estimated by timed runs over known distances, bead width and reinforcement height at five measurement points per pass, macro-etch penetration depth and HAZ width averaged across three sections, and bend test results with photographic documentation of the tension surface. This complete data set establishes your procedural baseline for subsequent parametric variation exercises.

## Exercise 2: Parameter Variation & Empirical Analysis

The second verification exercise systematically varies critical welding parameters—heat input, travel speed, and shielding gas composition—to establish empirical relationships between process control variables and measurable outcomes including penetration depth, HAZ extent, tensile strength, and distortion magnitude. This exercise builds quantitative intuition about the non-linear response of weld quality metrics to input parameter changes and trains your ability to diagnose out-of-specification conditions from first-principles thermal and mechanical reasoning.

Heat input per unit length of weld represents the fundamental parameter governing weld pool thermal behavior, penetration depth, cooling rate, and resulting microstructure. Heat input (H) in kilojoules per millimeter is calculated from the arc power and travel speed according to the relationship H = (V × I × 60) / (1000 × v), where V is arc voltage in volts, I is welding current in amperes, and v is travel speed in millimeters per minute. The dimensionless coefficient 60/1000 converts units appropriately, and typical values for GMAW of 6–10 mm carbon steel plate range from 0.6 to 1.4 kJ/mm. Lower heat input produces narrower beads with reduced penetration and faster cooling rates that promote martensitic transformation in the HAZ of hardenable steels, while excessive heat input causes wide HAZ bands, increased distortion, and potential burn-through on thin sections.

Prepare nine test coupons of identical 150 mm × 50 mm × 6 mm A36 geometry with all coupons surface-prepared identically to eliminate confounding variables. Design a three-by-three factorial experiment with voltage set at 17.5, 19.0, and 20.5 volts, and wire feed speed set to deliver 120, 145, and 170 amperes measured with a clamp-on ammeter during test beads. Hold shielding gas composition constant at 75/25 argon-CO₂ and flow rate at 16 L/min. For each parameter combination, weld a single stringer bead along the centerline of a coupon, maintaining travel speed at 5 mm/sec measured by timing the arc-on duration over the 100 mm test zone marked on the plate surface. Allow each coupon to cool to ambient temperature on an insulated firebrick surface to simulate free-air cooling conditions representative of field welding.

Measure bead geometry on each of the nine test welds by cross-sectioning at the midpoint, mounting, polishing, and macro-etching as described in Exercise 1. Record bead width, height, penetration depth, and HAZ width on both sides of the fusion boundary. Calculate heat input for each weld using the measured voltage, current, and travel speed. Construct empirical regression curves relating heat input to penetration depth and HAZ width by plotting the measurements in a spreadsheet application and fitting power-law or polynomial trend lines. You should observe that penetration depth increases approximately with the square root of heat input for constant travel speed, reflecting the diffusion-controlled nature of thermal penetration into the base metal ahead of the moving arc. HAZ width similarly increases with heat input, but the relationship includes geometric factors related to the weld bead aspect ratio.

Perform microhardness traverses across one low-heat-input specimen and one high-heat-input specimen to quantify the thermal cycle's effect on base metal properties. Using a calibrated Vickers or Knoop hardness tester with 500 gram load, indent the polished cross-section at 0.5 mm intervals along a line perpendicular to the weld fusion boundary, starting 10 mm into the base metal and progressing through the HAZ, fusion boundary, and into the weld metal. The resulting hardness profile reveals the extent and severity of HAZ hardening or softening. Carbon steel typically exhibits a hardness peak in the fine-grained HAZ immediately adjacent to the fusion boundary where rapid cooling from austenite produces fine pearlite or low-carbon martensite, with hardness values 20–40% higher than the base metal. The coarse-grained HAZ farther from the fusion line experiences peak temperatures sufficient for grain growth but slower cooling rates, resulting in coarser microstructures with hardness approaching or slightly below base metal values.

Compare the HAZ hardness peak magnitude between low and high heat input conditions. Higher heat input produces slower cooling rates calculated from the empirical relationship cooling rate (°C/s) ≈ 2πk(T_c - T_0)² / H, where k is thermal conductivity, T_c is the critical transformation temperature, and T_0 is ambient temperature. For A36 carbon steel with thermal conductivity near 50 W/(m·K) and critical temperature 723°C, doubling heat input from 0.7 to 1.4 kJ/mm approximately halves the cooling rate from 20°C/s to 10°C/s, allowing more time for diffusion-controlled transformation to pearlite rather than diffusionless martensitic transformation. This manifests as a reduction in peak hardness by 30–50 HV in the HAZ of the high-heat-input weld.

Extend the parametric investigation to shielding gas composition by welding three additional coupons with argon-CO₂ ratios of 85/15, 75/25, and 65/35 while holding voltage, current, and travel speed constant at the mid-range values from the previous factorial experiment. The CO₂ fraction strongly affects arc temperature, metal transfer mode, and weld pool fluidity through its dissociation and recombination reactions. Higher CO₂ content increases oxidation potential in the weld pool, producing more spatter and a less stable arc with greater tendency toward short-circuit transfer mode. Lower CO₂ content reduces oxidation but also reduces penetration depth due to lower arc force and decreased weld pool stirring from CO₂ dissociation dynamics.

Section and measure these three gas-composition variants, paying particular attention to penetration profile shape. The 85/15 argon-rich mixture produces shallower but wider penetration with a flatter profile, while the 65/35 CO₂-rich mixture creates a narrower, finger-like penetration profile driven by arc force and electromagnetic stirring. Measure oxygen content in the weld metal by optical emission spectroscopy if equipment is available, or estimate from the appearance and quantity of dross and spatter on the weld surface and surrounding plate. Higher oxygen content correlates with increased spatter loss, more prominent silicon-manganese deoxidation slag on the bead surface, and potentially reduced ductility measured in subsequent bend testing.

Quantify distortion magnitude for the nine factorial test coupons by measuring out-of-plane warpage. Before welding, mark three transverse measurement lines at 50, 75, and 100 mm from the coupon edge perpendicular to the planned weld path. After each coupon cools to ambient temperature, place it on a precision surface plate and measure the gap between the plate and the coupon underside at five points along each transverse line using feeler gauges or a height gauge. The maximum out-of-plane displacement quantifies angular distortion induced by weld shrinkage during cooling. Plot distortion magnitude against heat input to establish the empirical relationship; distortion typically scales linearly with heat input at low and moderate values, then increases super-linearly at high heat input as the softened HAZ provides less restraint against weld metal contraction.

Compile all parametric data into a multi-variable regression model or response surface plot that allows interpolation of expected outcomes for intermediate parameter values. This empirical model serves as a predictive tool for procedure development and a diagnostic reference when weld quality deviates from specifications. The model also reveals interaction effects—for example, the observation that travel speed increases have greater impact on penetration depth at high current than at low current due to the competing effects of arc energy delivery rate and weld pool residence time.

## Exercise 3: Stress Testing, Boundary Failure & Recovery Protocol

The third verification exercise deliberately drives welding procedures to the edge of acceptable performance and into failure modes to establish empirical failure boundaries, train your ability to recognize pre-failure warning signs during execution, and develop corrective action protocols based on first-principles understanding of failure mechanisms. This exercise encompasses hydrogen-induced cold cracking in restrained joints, solidification cracking from excessive dilution, incomplete fusion from inadequate heat input, and burn-through from excessive penetration, along with the metallurgical and procedural corrections required to recover acceptable quality.

Cold cracking represents the most insidious failure mode in carbon steel welding because cracks initiate hours or days after welding when hydrogen diffuses to high-stress regions in the HAZ and assists dislocation motion at stress intensities below the normal fracture toughness of the material. Hydrogen sources include moisture in shielding gas, surface contamination with hydrocarbons or rust, and hydrogen absorbed into the weld pool from dissociated water vapor. The driving force for cracking comes from tensile residual stresses generated by weld metal contraction during solidification and cooling, combined with microstructural hardening from rapid cooling through the austenite-to-martensite transformation when cooling rates exceed the critical value for the alloy composition.

To deliberately induce and then prevent cold cracking, prepare three test assemblies using ASTM A572 Grade 50 high-strength low-alloy steel plate in 12 mm thickness rather than the mild A36 steel used in previous exercises. The higher carbon equivalent (CE = %C + %Mn/6 + (%Cr + %Mo + %V)/5 + (%Ni + %Cu)/15) of A572 Grade 50, typically 0.42–0.48 compared to 0.35–0.40 for A36, increases hardenability and makes the material susceptible to HAZ hardening and hydrogen-assisted cracking. Cut three 150 mm × 75 mm plates and prepare single-V groove joints as in Exercise 1.

For the first assembly, intentionally introduce hydrogen by contaminating the groove faces with light machine oil applied with a cloth, allowing it to partially evaporate but leaving a residual film. Weld the root pass at low heat input—100 amperes, 17 volts, 6 mm/sec travel speed—without preheat, producing approximately 0.5 kJ/mm heat input and rapid cooling. The combination of hydrogen contamination, rapid cooling to produce hard martensitic HAZ microstructure with hardness exceeding 350 HV, and high residual stress from restrained contraction creates the classic triad of conditions for hydrogen-induced cracking. After welding, clamp the assembly rigidly to the steel table to prevent stress relief through distortion, maintaining maximum restraint intensity.

Inspect the first assembly at 24, 48, and 72 hours post-weld using dye penetrant inspection. Apply red penetrant dye to the weld surface and HAZ, allow 10 minutes dwell time, remove excess with solvent cleaner, then apply white developer and inspect under good lighting for indications of surface-breaking cracks. You should observe transverse cracks initiating in the coarse-grained HAZ within 24–48 hours, propagating perpendicular to the welding direction and potentially extending into the base metal. Section through a crack location and examine the fracture surface after breaking the specimen open in a vise; cold cracks exhibit intergranular fracture morphology with little plastic deformation, and the fracture surfaces may show small secondary cracks and a relatively bright, faceted appearance compared to ductile overload fracture.

For the second assembly, eliminate the oil contamination by cleaning thoroughly with acetone followed by wire brushing, but maintain the low heat input and high restraint. This removes the hydrogen source while retaining the hard microstructure and high residual stress. Inspect at the same intervals; you may observe no cracking or significantly delayed cracking compared to the contaminated specimen, demonstrating that hydrogen is necessary but not sufficient for cold cracking in this intermediate-hardenability alloy. The hard HAZ can tolerate the residual stress levels in the absence of hydrogen-assisted dislocation motion.

For the third assembly, implement the complete cold-crack prevention protocol specified in welding procedure specifications for high-strength steels. Preheat the assembly to 150°C using a torch or electric resistance blanket, measuring temperature at multiple points with thermocouple or temperature-indicating crayons. Maintain this preheat temperature throughout welding by reheating between passes if necessary. Increase heat input to 1.0 kJ/mm by reducing travel speed to 3.5 mm/sec while increasing current to 145 amperes. After completing the weld, maintain the assembly above 100°C for one hour of post-weld hold time to allow hydrogen to diffuse out of the weld metal and HAZ before thermal contraction stresses reach maximum magnitude during cooling to ambient temperature. This combination of preheat to reduce peak hardness through slower cooling, increased heat input to further reduce cooling rate, and post-weld hydrogen bake-out should prevent cracking entirely. Verify by dye penetrant inspection at 72 hours, then section for hardness testing to confirm that HAZ peak hardness has been reduced below 300 HV, the typical threshold for hydrogen cracking susceptibility in structural carbon steels.

Solidification cracking represents a distinct failure mode occurring during weld metal freezing rather than during post-weld HAZ cooling. This failure mode results from the development of tensile stress in the final liquid films between solidifying dendrites, causing inter-dendritic rupture when the liquid film cannot accommodate the strain imposed by thermal contraction. Susceptibility increases with wide solidification temperature ranges, high impurity content (especially sulfur and phosphorus that segregate to inter-dendritic regions and lower the local melting point), and high restraint intensity that prevents the semi-solid material from accommodating contraction strain by plastic flow.

Induce solidification cracking by welding 6061-T6 aluminum alloy plate without filler metal addition using autogenous GTAW. Prepare two 100 mm × 50 mm × 6 mm plates with square-butt edges held at 1 mm root opening, and tack weld at the ends. Set the TIG power source to 180 amperes DCEN with 100% argon shielding at 12 L/min, using a 2.4 mm diameter 2% lanthanated tungsten electrode ground to a 30-degree included angle point. Weld the joint by melting the base metal without adding filler rod, relying on edge fusion to fill the joint. The resulting weld metal consists entirely of melted base metal with no dilution from filler alloy, and the solidification occurs with the eutectic silicon present in 6061 alloy segregating to grain boundaries and forming wide mushy-zone temperature ranges. Upon cooling, you should observe centerline solidification cracks running longitudinally along the weld axis, visible as fine dark lines on the cleaned weld surface.

Section transversely through a solidification crack and examine under optical microscopy after polishing and etching with Keller's reagent (2 mL HF, 3 mL HCl, 5 mL HNO₃, 190 mL water, 10 seconds immersion). The crack path follows inter-dendritic boundaries with residual eutectic silicon particles visible along the crack faces. To prevent solidification cracking, re-weld an identical joint while adding ER4043 filler rod (aluminum-5% silicon alloy) continuously during welding to modify the solidification behavior. The added filler metal changes the fusion zone composition, narrows the solidification temperature range, and provides additional metal volume to compensate for solidification shrinkage. The filler addition also alters the dendrite growth morphology to reduce susceptibility to inter-dendritic tearing. This second weld should exhibit no solidification cracking when sectioned and examined.

Incomplete fusion failure occurs when the base metal sidewall or previously deposited weld bead fails to melt and merge with the depositing weld pool, leaving a planar lack-of-fusion defect that acts as a sharp crack-like stress concentrator under tensile loading. This failure mode results from inadequate heat input, excessive travel speed, incorrect electrode angle that directs arc force away from the intended fusion face, or intervening surface contamination (mill scale, rust, or slag) that prevents intimate metal-to-metal contact. Deliberately create incomplete fusion by welding a T-joint fillet weld with the electrode directed too much toward the horizontal plate member and insufficient dwelling at the vertical plate toe.

Set up a T-joint by tacking a 100 mm × 50 mm × 6 mm vertical plate onto a 150 mm × 75 mm × 6 mm horizontal plate at 90 degrees. Using GMAW with parameters from Exercise 1, weld a fillet bead along the joint with the electrode oriented at 60 degrees from the horizontal plate (30 degrees from vertical) and travel speed increased to 8 mm/sec. The electrode angle directs arc force and heat primarily into the horizontal plate, while the fast travel speed does not allow adequate dwell time for the arc to climb up the vertical plate toe and melt it thoroughly. After cooling, section the T-joint transversely and macro-etch. You should observe a lack-of-fusion defect at the vertical plate toe where the weld metal contacted but did not melt into the base metal, leaving a sharp notch.

Quantify the impact of this defect by performing a fillet break test. Cut the T-joint into 38 mm wide specimens, then load each specimen in a press with the vertical plate under tension until fracture occurs. The fracture initiates at the lack-of-fusion defect and propagates through the weld throat. Measure the load at fracture and calculate the nominal stress on the fillet throat area. Compare this to an identical T-joint welded with correct 45-degree electrode angle and 5 mm/sec travel speed that produces complete fusion. The properly welded joint should withstand 50–80% higher load before fracture, demonstrating the severe strength reduction from incomplete fusion defects. This exercise viscerally demonstrates why proper electrode angle and travel speed control are non-negotiable parameters in structural welding procedures.

Burn-through represents the opposite extreme where excessive heat input on thin material or slow travel speed causes complete melting through the plate thickness with loss of weld metal through the opening. Induce burn-through by welding 2 mm thick cold-rolled steel sheet with excessive heat input. Set GMAW parameters to 160 amperes and 20 volts—appropriate for 6 mm plate—but weld on 2 mm sheet at 3 mm/sec travel speed. The arc energy delivery rate far exceeds the heat dissipation rate through conduction into the thin base metal, causing complete through-thickness melting and hole formation. The molten metal falls through the hole, leaving an irregular opening edged with solidified drips on the backside.

Develop the corrective protocol for burn-through recovery by stepping down heat input in 20% increments (reducing to 130 amperes, then 100 amperes) while increasing travel speed proportionally to maintain similar heat input per unit length at the lower current settings. At 100 amperes, 18 volts, and 5 mm/sec travel speed, corresponding to 0.36 kJ/mm heat input, the thin sheet should weld successfully with full penetration visible on the backside as a smooth bead but no burn-through holes. This demonstrates the critical nature of procedure qualification for each material thickness range and the need to reduce heat input by 40–50% when welding thickness decreases by a factor of three.

## Operational Reference Guide & Diagnostic Checklist

The operational reference guide provides systematic troubleshooting decision trees, parameter adjustment protocols, and acceptance criteria organized by failure mode and observable symptoms. This section serves as your field reference during verification testing when weld quality deviates from specifications and you must diagnose root causes and implement corrections rapidly without extended experimental iteration.

When penetration depth measures less than specified minimum values, typically 70–80% of base metal thickness for single-V groove joints, the root cause derives from insufficient heat delivery to the joint region or excessive travel speed that does not allow adequate residence time for conductive heating ahead of the arc. Increase penetration by raising welding current in 10–15 ampere increments, reducing travel speed by 15–20%, or adjusting electrode angle to direct arc force more perpendicular to the joint plane. Verify that contact-tip-to-work distance (stick-out) has not increased beyond the specified 12–15 mm range, as excessive stick-out causes resistive heating of the electrode wire before it reaches the arc, reducing the arc energy available for base metal melting. Confirm shielding gas composition contains adequate CO₂ fraction (minimum 15%) to produce sufficient arc force for penetration; pure argon or argon-rich mixtures produce soft, broad arcs with minimal penetrating force.

When HAZ cracking appears during post-weld inspection, immediately halt production welding and implement the cold-cracking prevention protocol. Measure HAZ hardness on cracked specimens; values exceeding 350 HV indicate hard martensitic microstructure requiring preheat implementation. Calculate required preheat temperature from the carbon equivalent using the empirical relationship T_preheat (°C) = 700 × (CE - 0.35) for carbon equivalents above 0.35, with minimum 100°C for CE between 0.35–0.45 and minimum 150°C for CE above 0.45. Implement hydrogen control by verifying electrode storage in heated rod ovens at 150°C for low-hydrogen covered electrodes, confirming shielding gas dew point below -40°C for GMAW and GTAW processes, and removing all surface contamination within 25 mm of the joint by solvent cleaning followed by mechanical abrasion. Reduce residual stress magnitude by sequencing weld passes to balance contraction forces—for example, alternating between opposite sides of a double-V groove rather than completing one side before starting the other.

When weld metal porosity exceeds acceptance limits specified in radiographic or ultrasonic testing results, typically 2% maximum pore area in any radiographic view, investigate shielding gas delivery first. Measure flow rate with the torch trigger held open; inadequate flow below 12 L/min for GMAW or 8 L/min for GTAW allows atmospheric contamination of the weld pool. Excessive flow above 20 L/min creates turbulence at the shielding gas boundary that entrains air. Verify gas hose connections for leaks by pressurizing the system and applying soap solution; even small leaks allow air aspiration into the shielding stream. Inspect the gas diffuser and contact tip for spatter blockage that disrupts laminar flow. On the base metal side, confirm that surface preparation removed hydrated rust, mill scale, and paint within 50 mm of the joint; these coatings decompose under arc heating to release hydrogen and water vapor that form pores during solidification.

When undercut depth at weld toes exceeds the AWS D1.1 limit of 1.0 mm for groove welds or 0.5 mm for fillet welds, the root cause involves excessive arc voltage creating a broad, fluid weld pool that flows away from the solidifying edges faster than surface tension can retain it, or travel speed fast enough that the arc pushes a cavity ahead of the pool that does not refill before solidification. Reduce undercut by decreasing arc voltage by 1.0–1.5 volts to reduce pool fluidity and arc spreading force, or by reducing travel speed 10–15% to allow more time for pool edges to wet back into the parent metal. Modify electrode weaving technique for wide weave beads by pausing 1–2 seconds at each toe position to deposit additional metal at the undercut-susceptible locations, or switch to stringer bead technique that produces narrower beads with less undercut tendency.

When distortion exceeds fabrication tolerances, typically 3–5 mm out-of-plane displacement for 300 mm span lengths on structural components, implement distortion control through thermal management and restraint strategies. Reduce heat input to the minimum value compatible with required penetration depth, as distortion scales approximately linearly with total heat input. Sequence welding to balance thermal contraction forces about neutral axes—for example, alternating short segments between opposite sides of a built-up beam rather than completing one flange before starting the other. Apply strongbacks or fixtures to restrain components during welding, but recognize that restraint increases residual stress magnitude while reducing distortion; for fatigue-critical applications, allow limited distortion during welding then mechanically straighten afterward to avoid high tensile residual stresses at crack-sensitive locations. For thin-section work below 3 mm thickness, consider switching to pulsed GMAW or GTAW processes that deliver heat in intermittent bursts with cooling periods between pulses, reducing average heat input by 30–50% compared to continuous spray transfer or globular transfer modes.

When tensile or bend test specimens fail below minimum specified strength values, section through the fracture origin and perform fractographic analysis. Ductile fracture initiating from weld toes indicates proper weld metal and HAZ strength but stress concentration from undercut or excessive weld reinforcement; dress weld toes to smooth profiles with radius grinders to eliminate geometric stress risers. Brittle fracture through the HAZ indicates hardening or embrittlement from excessive cooling rates or hydrogen damage; implement preheating and interpass temperature control to reduce hardness below 300 HV. Fracture through the weld metal with reduced ductility suggests improper filler metal selection with insufficient toughness for the service temperature, or excessive oxygen or nitrogen contamination from inadequate shielding; verify filler metal conforms to specified AWS classification and that shielding gas purity meets minimum 99.9% for argon-based mixtures.

Construct a personal welding parameter logbook that records successful parameter sets organized by material type, thickness, joint geometry, and position. For each verified procedure, record voltage, current, travel speed, heat input calculated from these values, shielding gas composition and flow rate, preheat and interpass temperature, filler metal specification and diameter, and resulting penetration depth, HAZ width, hardness profile, and mechanical test results. This empirical database allows rapid procedure development for new applications by interpolation from the nearest existing qualified procedure rather than starting from generic handbook values that may not account for your specific equipment characteristics and personal arc manipulation technique.

Maintain calibration records for all measurement equipment with verification performed at intervals appropriate to equipment stability and usage intensity. Verify welding power source voltage and current output monthly using calibrated digital multimeters during test beads; drift exceeding 5% requires power source recalibration or repair. Verify shielding gas flow meters quarterly against a primary standard or by water displacement timing method; rotameter floats can stick or graduations can fade, introducing systematic errors. Verify thermocouples annually against boiling water (100°C) and ice water (0°C) reference points; type-K thermocouples develop drift from oxidation and thermal cycling that can reach 5–10°C error after extended use in high-temperature environments.

Document all verification test results in structured formats that support statistical process control analysis. For production welding following a qualified procedure, track key output metrics—penetration depth, HAZ hardness, tensile strength, bend test pass/fail results—on control charts with statistically derived control limits set at ±3 standard deviations from the process mean. When successive measurements approach or exceed control limits even without violating acceptance criteria, investigate for assignable causes such as equipment drift, consumable lot changes, or welder technique variation before out-of-specification conditions develop. This proactive approach maintains process capability indices (Cpk) above 1.33, ensuring that 99.99% of production welds meet specifications even with normal process variation.
