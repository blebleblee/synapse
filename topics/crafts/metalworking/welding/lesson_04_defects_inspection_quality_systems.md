# Lesson 4: Discontinuity Formation Mechanisms, NDT Principles, Code Compliance

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The permanence of a welded joint depends not merely on achieving fusion but on producing a microstructure free from critical flaws that propagate under service loads. Discontinuities—a term deliberately chosen over "defects" to distinguish detectable features from acceptance-violating flaws—arise from thermodynamic instabilities, kinetic trapping phenomena, and boundary condition failures during the weld thermal cycle. Understanding their formation mechanisms requires tracing the energy landscape from liquid metal through solidification into the solid state, where competing processes of diffusion, phase transformation, and stress relaxation determine whether a discontinuity remains benign or evolves into a crack initiation site.

Consider porosity, the most visually obvious volumetric discontinuity. Gas porosity originates from solubility inversions during solidification. Hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen exhibit retrograde solubility in most metals: their equilibrium concentration in liquid iron at 1600°C far exceeds their solubility in the face-centered cubic austenite that forms during cooling. As the solidification front advances, rejected gas atoms accumulate ahead of the interface. When local supersaturation exceeds the nucleation barrier for bubble formation, spherical voids appear, often decorated with oxide or nitride precipitates that served as heterogeneous nucleation sites. The critical insight is that porosity reflects a kinetic race between diffusion away from the solidification front and entrapment by rapid solidification velocity. In aluminum alloys, hydrogen solubility drops by two orders of magnitude from liquid to solid, making even 0.2 mL per 100 g sufficient to generate widespread microporosity if cooling rates exceed the diffusive time constant.

Hot cracking, by contrast, emerges from mechanical failure of a semi-solid network. During the final stages of solidification, a coherent solid skeleton forms while residual liquid films persist along grain boundaries. This mushy zone possesses near-zero ductility because the liquid films cannot accommodate tensile strain, yet the solid fraction lacks continuity to sustain stress redistribution. Thermal contraction and external restraint impose tensile strains precisely when the material is most vulnerable. Solidification cracking occurs when the accumulated strain exceeds the liquid film's ability to backfill by capillary flow. Alloys with wide freezing ranges—the temperature interval between liquidus and solidus—are particularly susceptible because the mushy zone persists longer, allowing greater strain accumulation. Aluminum 6061, with its roughly 60°C freezing range and low-melting-point eutectic constituents, exhibits profound hot-cracking sensitivity unless filler alloys with narrower freezing ranges like 4043 or 5356 are introduced to modify the terminal solidification sequence.

Cold cracking, also termed hydrogen-assisted cracking or delayed cracking, is a time-dependent embrittlement phenomenon occurring after the weld has fully solidified and cooled below 200°C. It requires the simultaneous presence of three conditions: diffusible hydrogen in the microstructure, a susceptible martensitic or bainitic microstructure with high hardness, and sufficient residual tensile stress from thermal contraction and restraint. Hydrogen atoms, small enough to occupy interstitial lattice sites, diffuse to high-stress regions at notches, grain boundaries, and prior austenite boundaries. There they reduce cohesive strength, lower the fracture stress, and enable transgranular or intergranular crack propagation at stresses far below the material's nominal yield strength. The delay between welding and cracking—ranging from minutes to days—reflects the time required for hydrogen to diffuse to critical concentration at stress risers. This time-temperature-dependent process explains why preheating and controlled cooling prove so effective: elevated temperatures accelerate hydrogen effusion out of the weldment before sufficient local concentration accumulates to trigger cracking.

Slag inclusions and oxide entrapment represent captured non-metallic phases that failed to float out during solidification. In shielded metal arc welding (SMAW), the flux coating generates a molten slag layer that protects the weld pool from atmospheric contamination. Proper slag removal between passes is not mere housekeeping but a metallurgical imperative: trapped slag films between weld beads create planar discontinuities that act as crack initiators under cyclic loading. Incomplete fusion—a failure to achieve metallurgical bonding between weld metal and base metal or between weld passes—arises from insufficient heat input, poor joint access, or oxide films that prevent atomic-level diffusion across the interface. These planar discontinuities are particularly insidious because they lie perpendicular to principal stress directions in typical joint geometries, maximizing their stress intensity factor and crack propagation driving force.

The thermodynamic framework governing discontinuity formation reveals that welding is fundamentally a non-equilibrium process. Cooling rates of 10–1000°C per second trap high-temperature phases, supersaturate solid solutions, and freeze in compositional gradients that would homogenize given sufficient diffusion time. The heat-affected zone (HAZ)—the base metal region that experiences thermal cycling without melting—undergoes its own sequence of phase transformations, grain growth, and precipitation reactions, creating a microstructural gradient with spatially varying mechanical properties. In hardenable steels, the HAZ adjacent to the fusion line may transform to untempered martensite with hardness exceeding 400 HV, while the region farther from the fusion line undergoes tempering or over-aging if the base metal was previously heat-treated. This heterogeneity explains why HAZ cracking often initiates slightly removed from the fusion boundary, where peak hardness coincides with peak residual stress.

The invariants that govern weld integrity are therefore clear: minimize compositional segregation and freezing range to suppress hot cracking, control cooling rates and hydrogen content to prevent cold cracking, maintain clean surfaces and proper technique to avoid inclusions and lack of fusion, and manage restraint and residual stress through joint design, preheat, and post-weld heat treatment. These principles are not negotiable; they derive from the thermodynamic, kinetic, and mechanical behavior of materials under the extreme thermal gradients and rapid solidification velocities inherent to fusion welding.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

Non-destructive testing (NDT) methods probe weld integrity through physical interactions that reveal internal discontinuities without sectioning or destroying the component. Each method exploits a distinct physical phenomenon—electromagnetic radiation attenuation, acoustic impedance mismatch, magnetic flux leakage, or capillary fluid penetration—and possesses characteristic sensitivity, resolution, and applicability boundaries determined by the underlying physics.

Radiographic testing (RT) relies on differential attenuation of X-rays or gamma rays passing through the weldment. When high-energy photons traverse matter, they undergo photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering, and pair production, with the total linear attenuation coefficient $\mu$ depending on material density, atomic number, and photon energy. The transmitted intensity $I$ after traversing thickness $t$ follows the exponential attenuation law $I = I_0 e^{-\mu t}$. A volumetric discontinuity such as porosity or a slag inclusion presents lower density than the surrounding metal, attenuating fewer photons and appearing as a darker region on the radiographic film or digital detector. The technique's sensitivity is fundamentally limited by thickness, with practical penetration ranging from a few millimeters in lead to 100+ mm in aluminum. Contrast sensitivity—the smallest density change detectable—is typically 1–2% of the base metal thickness for film radiography, improving to 0.5% with digital radiography and image processing.

The geometric constraints of radiographic inspection impose strict setup requirements. The radiation source, discontinuity, and detector must be collinear to maximize contrast. Planar discontinuities such as lack of fusion or tight cracks oriented parallel to the beam direction may be entirely invisible because the path length through the discontinuity is negligible. This geometric sensitivity necessitates multiple exposure angles, typically two orthogonal views for critical welds. Penetrameters or image quality indicators (IQIs)—standardized plaques containing holes and wires of known dimensions—are placed on the weldment to verify that achieved sensitivity meets code requirements. For example, ASME Section V specifies that a 2T hole (diameter equal to 2% of the material thickness) must be visible to demonstrate adequate technique.

Ultrasonic testing (UT) exploits acoustic wave propagation and reflection at material interfaces. A piezoelectric transducer generates high-frequency mechanical waves (typically 2–10 MHz) that propagate through the material at the longitudinal wave velocity $v_L = \sqrt{(K + 4\mu/3)/\rho}$, where $K$ is the bulk modulus, $\mu$ the shear modulus, and $\rho$ the density. When the acoustic wave encounters a boundary between materials with differing acoustic impedance $Z = \rho v$, a portion of the energy reflects according to the reflection coefficient $R = [(Z_2 - Z_1)/(Z_2 + Z_1)]^2$. A gas-filled void with $Z \approx 0$ reflects nearly 100% of the incident energy, producing a strong echo that is detected by the transducer or a separate receiver.

Ultrasonic inspection distinguishes itself by providing depth information—the time delay between the initial pulse and the echo directly measures the round-trip distance to the reflector. Angle beam shear wave inspection uses a wedge to introduce transverse waves at a specific angle, typically 45°, 60°, or 70°, that propagate parallel to the weld interface and reflect from vertically oriented discontinuities that would be invisible to normal beam incidence. The skip distance—the lateral distance traveled before the beam reflects off the back wall—is calculated from the wedge angle and material thickness, allowing precise positioning to interrogate the weld root or cap regions. Phased array ultrasonic testing (PAUT) extends this capability by using an array of transducer elements with programmable time delays to electronically steer and focus the beam, enabling rapid volumetric scanning without mechanical manipulation.

Magnetic particle testing (MT) detects surface-breaking and near-surface discontinuities in ferromagnetic materials by observing magnetic flux leakage. When a magnetizing current or external field establishes magnetic flux density $\vec{B}$ in the component, a discontinuity perpendicular to the flux lines creates a high-reluctance path that forces flux to leak into the surrounding air. Ferromagnetic particles applied as a dry powder or wet suspension concentrate at the leakage field, rendering the discontinuity visible. The method's sensitivity depends on achieving sufficient magnetizing force—typically 3000–6000 ampere-turns per meter for circular magnetization using prods or a coil—and orienting the field perpendicular to the suspected crack orientation. Longitudinal cracks require circumferential magnetization, while transverse cracks require longitudinal magnetization, necessitating sequential magnetization in orthogonal directions for comprehensive inspection.

Liquid penetrant testing (PT) detects surface-breaking discontinuities in any non-porous material through capillary action. A low-surface-tension penetrant liquid is applied to the cleaned surface and allowed to dwell, during which capillarity draws the penetrant into surface-opening cracks, pores, and seams. Excess penetrant is removed, and a developer—a fine white powder or suspension—is applied to draw the trapped penetrant back to the surface, creating a visible indication larger than the original discontinuity through lateral spreading. Visible dye penetrants use red dye for contrast against white developer under natural light, while fluorescent penetrants contain compounds that fluoresce yellow-green under ultraviolet (365 nm) illumination, providing superior sensitivity to fine cracks. The technique's fundamental limitation is that it cannot detect subsurface discontinuities; a tight crack that does not reach the surface remains invisible regardless of penetrant dwell time.

The selection matrix for NDT methods is dictated by physics, geometry, and economics. Radiography excels at detecting volumetric discontinuities in accessible geometries where two-sided access permits source and detector placement, but struggles with planar flaws and incurs radiation safety burdens. Ultrasonic testing provides excellent sensitivity to planar discontinuities and depth sizing, but requires skilled operators to interpret A-scan waveforms and distinguish geometric reflections from true flaws. Magnetic particle testing offers rapid screening for surface cracks in ferromagnetic materials but cannot inspect austenitic stainless steels or aluminum. Liquid penetrant testing is universally applicable to non-porous materials and requires minimal training, but detects only surface-breaking discontinuities and imposes cleanliness requirements that challenge field applications.

Code compliance frameworks impose acceptance criteria that distinguish between minor discontinuities that do not compromise structural integrity and rejectable defects. ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section VIII Division 1 for pressure vessels, AWS D1.1 for structural steel, and API 1104 for pipeline construction each define acceptance limits based on discontinuity type, size, distribution, and service severity. The criteria reflect fracture mechanics principles: small isolated discontinuities below a critical size cannot nucleate cracks under design loads, while clustered porosity or elongated slag inclusions reduce effective load-bearing area and introduce stress concentrations.

Radiographic acceptance criteria typically specify maximum individual porosity size, maximum porosity density (number per unit area or length), and prohibit certain defect types entirely. For example, AWS D1.1 Table 6.1 for statically loaded non-tubular connections permits isolated gas pores up to t/4 in size (where t is the thinner base metal thickness), with spacing requirements preventing clustering. Elongated indications exceeding 3:1 length-to-width ratio are evaluated as cracks regardless of their actual morphology, reflecting the conservative principle that planar discontinuities are more dangerous than rounded voids. Ultrasonic acceptance criteria specify maximum allowable amplitude compared to a reference reflector—a flat-bottom hole or side-drilled hole of known size—calibrated through distance-amplitude correction (DAC) curves that account for beam spreading and attenuation with depth.

The welding procedure specification (WPS) is the central quality assurance document that translates material properties, joint geometry, and service requirements into explicit welding parameters. A compliant WPS under ASME Section IX or ISO 15614 enumerates essential variables including base metal specification and thickness range, filler metal classification, welding process and transfer mode, preheat and interpass temperature limits, travel speed and heat input bounds, shielding gas composition and flow rate, and post-weld heat treatment requirements. Each parameter is designated as essential or non-essential based on whether deviation affects mechanical properties: increasing heat input from 1.5 to 2.5 kJ/mm alters cooling rate, grain size, and HAZ hardness, requiring requalification, whereas changing electrode manufacturer within the same AWS classification does not.

Procedure qualification requires destructive testing of coupons welded per the proposed WPS to verify tensile strength, bend ductility, and impact toughness meet code minimums. Transverse tensile specimens machined perpendicular to the weld axis must achieve ultimate tensile strength no less than the base metal minimum, with failure permitted in base metal, weld metal, or HAZ provided the load exceeds the threshold. Guided bend tests—side bends for thin sections, face and root bends for thicker sections—are wrapped around a mandrel diameter calculated to impose 20–30% outer fiber strain, revealing lack of fusion, slag inclusions, or brittleness through crack initiation and propagation. Charpy V-notch impact testing at specified temperatures (often −20°C or −40°C for low-temperature service) quantifies toughness through absorbed energy, with acceptance criteria typically requiring 20–27 J average across three specimens and no individual specimen below 14–18 J.

Welder performance qualification—the certification that individual welders can execute a qualified WPS—imposes additional testing and limitations. A welder qualified in the 6G position (pipe inclined 45° with axis horizontal, requiring all welding positions simultaneously) holds qualification for all positions, while 1G qualification (flat position) covers only flat and horizontal fillet welds. Thickness qualification ranges follow logarithmic rules: testing on 19 mm plate qualifies 3–38 mm, while testing on 6 mm qualifies only 3–13 mm, reflecting the distinct challenges of thin-section burn-through versus thick-section heat extraction and hydrogen control. Process qualification is non-transferable: SMAW qualification does not permit GMAW work, and each process requires separate demonstration of skill and knowledge.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Achieving zero-defect welding in production requires systematic control of the variables that govern discontinuity formation and early detection of process deviations before they propagate through multiple joints. The optimization strategy balances material cleanliness, thermal management, operator skill, and inspection rigor to minimize both defect incidence and false rejection costs.

Hydrogen control is paramount in welding high-strength steels where cold cracking susceptibility rises sharply above 350 HV. Low-hydrogen electrodes—AWS E7018 for SMAW or ER70S-6 with argon-CO2 shielding for GMAW—maintain hydrogen content below 5 mL per 100 g deposited metal, yet atmospheric moisture recontamination during storage or exposure to humid air can double or triple this value within hours. Proper electrode storage in heated ovens at 120–150°C drives off adsorbed moisture, while limiting exposure time after removal (typically 4 hours for E7018) prevents rehydration. Austenitic stainless steel electrodes and flux-cored wires are even more hygroscopic and require storage below 10% relative humidity to maintain low-hydrogen designation.

Preheat temperature selection balances competing objectives: elevating base metal temperature before welding slows cooling rate, tempering martensitic microstructures and reducing hardness, while simultaneously accelerating hydrogen effusion and reducing residual hydrogen concentration. The minimum preheat temperature is calculated from carbon equivalent formulas that account for hardenability: $\text{CE} = C + \frac{Mn}{6} + \frac{Cr+Mo+V}{5} + \frac{Ni+Cu}{15}$ for the International Institute of Welding formula, or more sophisticated functions incorporating plate thickness and restraint intensity. For a CE of 0.45 and 25 mm thickness, minimum preheat typically falls between 100–150°C. Interpass temperature—the maximum temperature permitted between weld passes—prevents excessive grain growth and δ-ferrite formation in austenitic stainless steels, typically capped at 150–200°C to maintain corrosion resistance, while carbon steels tolerate higher interpass temperatures up to 300°C without metallurgical degradation.

Heat input optimization requires balancing penetration depth, dilution ratio, and cooling rate. Increasing voltage and decreasing travel speed raises energy per unit length (calculated as $\text{HI} = \frac{60 \times V \times I \times \eta}{S}$ where $V$ is voltage, $I$ is current, $\eta$ is arc efficiency, and $S$ is travel speed in mm/min), producing wider weld beads and slower cooling but risking excessive grain growth and reduced toughness. Pulsed current welding strategies modulate heat input on sub-second timescales, using high peak current pulses to maintain arc stability and penetration while low background current reduces average heat input. This thermal cycling refines grain size through repeated recrystallization and can suppress hot cracking by periodically backfilling semi-solid regions before strain accumulation exceeds critical thresholds.

Joint geometry modifications address root-cause failure modes. Increasing root opening from 1.5 to 3 mm in open-root pipe joints improves penetration consistency and reduces lack-of-fusion risk, but demands precise fit-up and gap uniformity to prevent burn-through. Backing strips—consumable or non-consumable—support the molten weld pool during root pass deposition, eliminating burn-through risk but introducing potential lack-of-fusion at the backing interface if surface oxides are not removed. Backing gas purging with argon or nitrogen during root pass welding on stainless steels prevents chromium carbide precipitation and intergranular corrosion by excluding oxygen from the weld backside.

Residual stress mitigation through mechanical methods includes peening—controlled plastic deformation of the weld cap through hammering or shot impact—which introduces compressive residual stress in the surface layer, suppressing fatigue crack initiation. Vibratory stress relief applies low-amplitude cyclic loading at the component's natural frequency to accelerate stress relaxation without the energy cost and distortion risk of furnace post-weld heat treatment. These methods are not universally code-approved; ASME prohibits peening on austenitic stainless steels due to work-hardening effects that can trap hydrogen.

Common inspection failure modes arise from operator error, calibration drift, and physical limitations. Radiographic underexposure produces low-contrast images where small discontinuities vanish into noise, while overexposure saturates the detector and obscures density variations. Geometric unsharpness—blurring of the shadow edges—increases with source size and source-to-film distance ratio, limiting resolution to 0.5–1 mm for industrial X-ray tubes. Scatter radiation from thick sections fogs the film and reduces contrast unless lead screens and collimation are employed.

Ultrasonic inspection fails when couplant—the liquid or gel layer that transmits sound from transducer to part—dries out or traps air bubbles, introducing signal attenuation that mimics deep discontinuities. Dead zones near the surface, where the initial pulse obscures echoes, range from 2–10 mm depending on transducer frequency and damping, rendering near-surface lack-of-fusion invisible to straight-beam inspection. Grain scattering in coarse-grained materials like cast stainless steel generates noise that obscures small reflectors, requiring lower frequencies (1–2 MHz) at the cost of reduced resolution.

Magnetic particle testing produces false indications from surface roughness, machining marks, and magnetic write—residual magnetization patterns from prior inspections or material handling that create leakage fields unrelated to structural discontinuities. Proper demagnetization after inspection prevents these artifacts but requires careful selection of field strength and frequency to avoid remagnetizing the component. Liquid penetrant testing yields false indications from porous surfaces, embedded smeared metal from grinding, and incomplete cleaning that traps penetrant in benign surface texture rather than true cracks.

The ultimate failure mode is complacency—assuming that code-minimum inspection frequency suffices and that qualified procedures guarantee quality. Statistical process control demands that inspection results feed back into process parameter adjustment: increasing porosity incidence signals rising dew point or contaminated shielding gas, while repeated lack-of-fusion indicates insufficient heat input or improper joint preparation. Real-time weld monitoring systems that track voltage, current, wire feed speed, and travel speed deviations provide early warning before discontinuities form, enabling immediate corrective action rather than costly post-weld repair.

Repair welding of rejectable discontinuities introduces its own hazards. Gouging or grinding to remove a slag inclusion or lack-of-fusion creates a notch with stress concentration factors exceeding 3.0, demanding careful profiling to achieve gradual tapered transitions. Multiple repair cycles in the same location progressively coarsen grain size and accumulate residual stress, eventually producing a weld whose properties fall below original base metal despite meeting radiographic acceptance. Codes limit repair frequency—typically three repairs per location—and require engineering evaluation beyond this threshold to assess fitness-for-service through fracture mechanics analysis.

The convergence of discontinuity metallurgy, NDT physics, and code compliance creates a robust quality assurance framework where each element reinforces the others. Understanding why porosity forms enables process modifications that prevent its occurrence; comprehending how ultrasound interacts with planar discontinuities guides beam angle selection for maximum sensitivity; recognizing that acceptance criteria derive from fracture mechanics provides the confidence to accept minor indications that meet code without demanding perfection. This integrated knowledge transforms welding from an art practiced by trial-and-error into an engineering discipline where outcomes are predictable, measurable, and continuously improvable.
