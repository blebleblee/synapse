# Lesson 2: Arc Physics, Energy Transfer Modes, Process Mechanics

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The electric arc is one of the most energetically dense phenomena a craftsman can hold in their hands. At its core, it is a self-sustaining plasma discharge bridging two conductors through an ionized gas column, sustaining temperatures between 6,000 °C and 20,000 °C depending on process and parameters. That range exceeds the surface temperature of the sun's photosphere. What makes this manageable in a shop environment—rather than catastrophically destructive—is the arc's dependency on a narrow set of electrical and atmospheric conditions that the welder learns to read and control with the same intuition a machinist develops for chip color and tool chatter.

Understanding the arc from first principles begins with ionization. When sufficient voltage is applied across a gap between electrode and workpiece, free electrons in the gap are accelerated by the electric field. These electrons collide with gas molecules—argon, helium, CO₂, nitrogen, or combinations thereof depending on process—and strip additional electrons from their orbitals, producing positively charged ions and yet more free electrons. This avalanche ionization creates the plasma column: a conductive gas that now carries current as efficiently as a metallic conductor. The arc is born. Once established, the arc sustains itself because the plasma's conductivity allows current to flow at a much lower voltage than was required to initiate it—a critical distinction that explains why open-circuit voltage (OCV) on a MIG or stick welder must be significantly higher than the actual arc voltage during welding.

The arc's electrical character is defined by its volt-ampere (V-A) characteristic curve, which is not linear like a resistor but instead exhibits a falling characteristic at lower currents transitioning to a rising characteristic at higher currents. In practical terms, this means the arc actively resists changes in length. Lengthen the arc slightly and voltage climbs; voltage regulators in constant-voltage (CV) MIG machines exploit this by holding voltage constant while allowing current to vary, so wire burn-off rate self-corrects. Constant-current (CC) machines used in SMAW and GTAW instead maintain amperage within a narrow band, tolerating arc length variation while preventing the runaway current that would destroy a tungsten or melt a stick electrode's flux coating prematurely. This CC/CV distinction is not merely a circuit design choice—it encodes a philosophy about how the process manages its own thermal output moment to moment.

The plasma column itself is electrically asymmetric. Under DCEN (Direct Current Electrode Negative, also called straight polarity), electrons flow from the electrode toward the workpiece. Because electron emission carries kinetic energy, approximately two-thirds of the arc's thermal energy is deposited at the anode—the workpiece—producing a narrow, deep penetration profile. This is the standard configuration for GTAW on steel and stainless steel, where precise, deep fusion is prioritized. Reverse polarity, DCEP (electrode positive), flips the energy distribution: ions bombard the electrode, the workpiece receives less heat, and penetration becomes shallower and wider. DCEP is the standard for SMAW and GMAW, and critically, in GTAW on aluminum it provides cathodic cleaning—the ion bombardment physically blasts the refractory aluminum oxide layer from the weld pool surface, an effect no chemical flux can replicate as reliably during the weld itself. Alternating current (AC) alternates between these conditions at line or transformer-controlled frequency, and the balance between EP and EN cycles can be tuned on modern inverter AC TIG machines from roughly 30/70 to 70/30, allowing the welder to dial in cleaning action versus penetration for different aluminum alloys and oxide conditions.

Heat transfer out of the arc and into the base metal operates through all three classical modes simultaneously, but their relative contributions vary dramatically by process and position. Conduction is the dominant mode for thermal travel through solid metal once energy has been deposited in the weld pool: heat moves through the crystal lattice by phonon transport and free-electron diffusion, governed by the material's thermal conductivity. Copper's conductivity of 401 W/m·K means it bleeds heat from the fusion zone so aggressively that preheat and sometimes active inter-pass heating are required to maintain a wettable pool, while stainless steel at 16 W/m·K retains heat so stubbornly that inter-pass temperature management becomes critical to avoid sensitization of the heat-affected zone (HAZ). Convection carries heat within the weld pool itself through fluid flow driven by surface tension gradients (the Marangoni effect), electromagnetic (Lorentz) forces, and arc pressure. The Marangoni effect in steel—where surface tension decreases with temperature—drives hot metal from the arc center outward and cool metal inward along the pool bottom, producing a wide, shallow pool geometry. Sulfur additions in free-machining steels reverse this gradient, drawing hot metal downward and producing dramatically deeper penetration, which is why seemingly identical mild steel grades can weld with noticeably different fusion profiles. Radiation from the arc plasma column contributes meaningfully to surface heating of the adjacent base metal and is the reason UV protection is non-negotiable—arc radiation in the 200–400 nm UV range causes photokeratitis (arc eye) with exposures measurable in seconds.

The shielding gas is not a passive spectator in arc physics; it is a reactive participant that shapes the arc column's geometry, energy density, and chemical interaction with the weld pool. Argon, being monatomic, ionizes readily at relatively low voltage and produces a narrow, constricted arc with a soft, stable plasma column. It is the standard for GTAW and for GMAW on non-ferrous metals. Helium, also monatomic but with a much higher ionization potential (24.6 eV versus argon's 15.8 eV), demands higher arc voltages and produces a hotter, more energetic arc—useful for high-travel-speed aluminum TIG work and for increasing heat input on thick copper sections, though its low density and high cost make it a deliberate choice rather than a default. Carbon dioxide in GMAW applications actively participates in the chemistry of the arc: CO₂ dissociates in the plasma into CO and atomic oxygen, contributing oxidizing chemistry to the pool that influences silicon and manganese recovery from the wire and produces the characteristically spatterier, deeper-penetrating arc profile compared to argon-rich blends. The standard 75% Ar / 25% CO₂ (C25) mix for short-circuit and spray transfer on steel is a pragmatic compromise: enough argon to stabilize the arc and reduce spatter, enough CO₂ to maintain deep fusion and reduce cost. For spray transfer specifically, argon content must exceed approximately 80% to maintain the axial droplet transfer mode; below that threshold, the arc cannot sustain the electromagnetic pinch force that breaks droplets cleanly off the wire tip.

The electrode classification system in SMAW encodes nearly everything the welder needs to know about a rod in a four-to-five digit AWS designation. An E7018 rod breaks down as follows: E denotes electrode; 70 specifies minimum tensile strength of deposited weld metal in thousands of PSI (70,000 PSI); the third digit (1) indicates all-position capability; the fourth digit (8) encodes both the flux coating chemistry (low-hydrogen, iron powder in this case) and the usable current type (DCEP or AC). The low-hydrogen designation is not incidental—hydrogen is the primary cause of underbead and delayed hydrogen cracking in hardenable steels. E7018 rods are sealed in hermetic containers or baked in rod ovens at 300–350 °C precisely because the iron powder/low-hydrogen coating absorbs atmospheric moisture within hours of exposure, and even 10 mL of water in the arc zone can liberate enough atomic hydrogen to embrittle a structural weld. By contrast, the E6013 rod's rutile-based coating is moisture-tolerant and forgiving but produces a weld deposit with higher hydrogen content—acceptable for mild steel structural tacks and sheet metal, unacceptable for pressure vessels or any application where post-weld hydrogen embrittlement is a risk.

GTAW tungsten electrodes have their own classification system under AWS A5.12. Pure tungsten (green band) maintains a balled tip geometry under AC and was the historical standard for aluminum, but modern inverter AC machines with square-wave output and adjustable balance work better with ceriated (grey band, 2% CeO₂) or lanthanated (gold band, 1.5% La₂O₃) electrodes, which tolerate AC without balling as aggressively and also perform excellently on DCEN for steel and titanium. The 2% thoriated tungsten (red band), once ubiquitous for DCEN work, is being phased out in many shops due to thorium-232's low-level radioactivity and the grinding dust hazard—ceriated and lanthanated alternatives match or exceed its arc-starting and current-carrying performance without the radiological paperwork. Tungsten diameter selection follows a straightforward amperage table: a 1.6 mm (1/16") electrode handles roughly 70–150 A on DCEN; a 2.4 mm (3/32") handles 150–250 A. Exceeding these ranges melts the tip, contaminates the pool with tungsten inclusion, and forces a regrind.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The practical mechanic of the arc can be understood as a system with four interacting layers: the power source and its control loop, the electrode and consumable feed system, the arc plasma column itself, and the weld pool and its solidification dynamics. These layers communicate bidirectionally in real time, and competence as a welder means developing reflexes that intervene at the right layer for each class of problem.

```
POWER SOURCE (CC or CV)
        │
        │  Voltage / Current feedback loop
        ▼
ELECTRODE SYSTEM
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  GTAW: Non-consumable tungsten / filler added   │
  │        separately by hand                       │
  │                                                 │
  │  GMAW: Consumable wire, motor-driven feed,      │
  │        contact tip transfers current            │
  │                                                 │
  │  SMAW: Consumable flux-coated stick electrode,  │
  │        flux vaporizes to form shielding gas     │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
        │
        │  Arc gap (1–6 mm depending on process)
        ▼
ARC PLASMA COLUMN
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  Cathode spot → plasma core → anode spot        │
  │  T: 6,000–20,000 °C                             │
  │  Electromagnetic pinch / arc pressure           │
  │  Shielding gas envelope: excludes O₂, N₂, H₂O  │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
        │
        │  Heat flux (conduction + convection + radiation)
        ▼
WELD POOL & BASE METAL
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  Fusion zone: fully melted, chemical mixing     │
  │  HAZ: solid but thermally affected              │
  │  Marangoni convection drives pool fluid motion  │
  │  Solidification: columnar → equiaxed grains     │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
        │
        ▼
SOLIDIFIED WELD BEAD
  (Microstructure, residual stress, distortion locked in)
```

The penetration profile—the cross-sectional shape of the fusion zone—is the most direct physical record of how energy was delivered and distributed. A narrow, deep profile (high aspect ratio) indicates concentrated heat delivery: high current, long arc, helium shielding gas, or DCEN polarity. A wide, shallow profile indicates distributed energy: lower current, short arc, argon shielding, or the cleaning-cycle bias of AC on aluminum. The penetration profile is measured in production quality control by sectioning a sample coupon transversely, polishing it to 600 grit, etching with a suitable reagent (nital for carbon steel, oxalic acid for stainless, Keller's reagent for aluminum), and examining the macro cross-section. The fusion line—the boundary between the fully melted zone and the HAZ—must show complete fusion to the root with no cold lap, undercut, or lack-of-fusion discontinuities.

GMAW metal transfer modes represent discrete arc physics regimes, not simply parameter settings on a dial. Short-circuit transfer (roughly 15–22 V, 60–200 A for 0.9 mm ER70S-6 wire) occurs when the wire physically contacts the pool, shorts the circuit, current spikes, and surface tension pinches the droplet free before the arc re-establishes—this cycle repeats 50–200 times per second and is audible as the characteristic sizzling crackle of short-arc MIG. It deposits low heat input and is favored for thin sheet and out-of-position welding, but incomplete fusion is the endemic defect risk on thicker sections because the pool never fully reaches the root. Spray transfer (above 24–28 V, above the transition current for a given wire diameter—approximately 220 A for 1.0 mm wire in a 90/10 Ar/CO₂ mix) produces a continuous plasma jet with axially directed droplet transfer, no short circuits, and dramatically higher deposition and heat input. The arc is visually distinct: brighter, more stable, and notably louder with a sharp hissing quality. It is restricted to flat and horizontal positions because the pool fluidity at these heat inputs defeats surface tension's ability to hold metal against gravity in vertical or overhead positions. Globular transfer occupies the unstable intermediate zone between these two modes and is generally avoided in production work because large, irregular droplet detachment produces excessive spatter and inconsistent fusion.

Pulsed GMAW, available on modern inverter-based machines like the Miller Dynasty-class, Lincoln Power Wave, or Fronius TransPulse platforms, synthesizes the benefits of both modes by cycling rapidly between a high peak current pulse (above spray transition) and a low background current that maintains the arc without transferring metal. Each peak pulse detaches exactly one droplet. The result is spray-quality fusion and bead appearance at heat inputs approaching short-circuit levels, with full out-of-position capability—a significant process advantage for aluminum fabrication where heat input control directly governs distortion and HAZ softening.

The shielding gas envelope's coverage is geometry and flow rate dependent in ways that become critical when welding reactive metals. For GTAW on titanium, atmospheric contamination by oxygen or nitrogen above approximately 50 ppm causes visible discoloration of the bead—gold indicates acceptable minor oxidation, blue is marginal, purple and grey indicate significant contamination that compromises ductility and must be rejected. The standard gas lens setup (replacing the standard collet body with a porous bronze or stainless mesh gas lens in the torch head) dramatically improves laminar flow of the shielding gas column, allowing effective coverage at longer electrode-to-work distances and in configurations where the standard collet's turbulent flow would cause atmospheric ingress. For titanium, back-purging of the root side with argon delivered through a trailing shield or a custom-fabricated copper purge dam is mandatory—the heat-affected zone on the underside of the weld reaches temperatures sufficient to form titanium nitrides and oxides just as readily as the top bead surface.

The heat-affected zone is the metallurgist's concern made visible by the etching macro. In carbon and low-alloy steels, the HAZ is subdivided into distinct thermal gradient regions: the coarse-grain HAZ immediately adjacent to the fusion line, where peak temperatures exceeded ~1,100 °C and austenite grains grew dramatically before quenching during cooling; the fine-grain HAZ where normalized grain refinement occurred; and the inter-critical and sub-critical zones at lower peak temperatures. The coarse-grain HAZ is the region most susceptible to hydrogen-induced cracking and to toughness reduction, particularly in steels with carbon equivalents (CE) above 0.4. Carbon equivalent is calculated as:

$$CE = C + \frac{Mn}{6} + \frac{Cr + Mo + V}{5} + \frac{Ni + Cu}{15}$$

When CE exceeds 0.4, preheat becomes mandatory not as a stylistic choice but as a mechanistic intervention: raising the base metal temperature to 100–250 °C (depending on CE and section thickness) slows the cooling rate through the martensite transformation range, reduces hardness in the coarse-grain HAZ, and extends the time window during which dissolved hydrogen can diffuse out of the weld metal before the microstructure locks it in place.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

The practitioner working at high performance levels treats the welding process as a closed-loop system to be optimized, not a manual skill to be executed on faith. Every process variable has a measurable effect on a measurable output, and the diagnostic workflow begins with reading the bead rather than chasing parameter tables.

Bead geometry is the primary diagnostic signal. An ideal stringer bead in flat position GMAW on 6 mm mild steel with 1.0 mm ER70S-6 wire at 24 V / 200 A / 400 mm/min travel speed should exhibit a width-to-height ratio of approximately 3:1 to 4:1, smooth tied-in toes without undercut, and a cross-section revealing fusion penetration of approximately 2–3 mm into the base plate. If the bead is convex and ropey with poor toe wetting, the voltage is too low relative to wire feed speed—increase voltage in 0.5 V increments. If the bead is flat and wide with burn-through risk or excessive spatter from an erratic arc, voltage is too high—reduce it and verify that the contact-tip-to-work distance (CTWD) is consistent at 15–20 mm. CTWD is the most commonly mis-managed variable in GMAW: the electrode extension (stickout beyond the contact tip) adds resistance to the electrical circuit, and inconsistent stickout produces inconsistent current even when the machine's voltage setting is unchanged. Hold the gun at a fixed angle, maintain a consistent drag of 5–15° from vertical, and keep physical stickout to 10–15 mm for most short-circuit applications.

Porosity, the most common volumetric defect in MIG and TIG welds, has three primary causes that must be diagnosed separately because their remediation differs entirely. Contamination porosity—from oil, moisture, or mill scale on the base metal or filler wire—produces fine, uniformly distributed pores throughout the weld cross-section. The fix is mechanical and chemical preparation: wire brush, then wipe with acetone (not lacquer thinner, which leaves a residue) in the direction of welding, never back-and-forth. Shielding gas porosity, caused by atmospheric ingress from insufficient flow rate, drafts in the shop, or a damaged gas hose, produces larger, more randomly distributed pores often concentrated at the weld start and end or on the edges of the bead where coverage is thinnest. Verify flow at the nozzle with a flow meter (not just at the regulator), inspect hoses for cracks at the fittings, and add a physical wind break if welding outdoors or near bay doors. Nitrogen porosity in stainless steel appears as elongated pores aligned with the welding direction and usually indicates a switch to the wrong shielding gas—argon/nitrogen mixtures appropriate for some duplex stainless processes are incompatible with austenitic grades where nitrogen solubility drops sharply during solidification.

Lack of fusion (LOF) is a planar defect and therefore far more structurally serious than porosity for a given flaw size. It is predominantly a heat management failure. In GMAW short-circuit transfer, LOF at the sidewall occurs when travel speed is too high or current too low to fully wet the groove face before the pool solidifies. In GTAW, LOF at the root is the consequence of an arc gap held too far from the root face, reducing current density at the most critical joint location. The fix for GTAW root passes is to shorten the arc to 1–1.5× the tungsten diameter and accept that the puddle must visibly drop into the root gap before adding filler. The temptation to add filler immediately on arc strike, before full penetration is established, is the single most common cause of incomplete root fusion in hand TIG work. The correct rhythm is: establish the keyhole, observe the back-bead beginning to form (or verify with a backing purge that the root is wet), then begin feeding filler at a cadenced rhythm matching the pool's oscillation.

Distortion and residual stress accumulate with every pass in a multi-pass weld and must be managed with equal discipline to the metallurgical concerns. The fundamental mechanism is thermally induced plastic strain: the weld metal contracts as it cools from liquidus to room temperature, and because the surrounding base metal restrains this contraction, the weld metal yields in tension and the base metal bends or warps. Angular distortion in a single-V butt joint is predictable and can be countered by pre-setting the joint to the opposite angle (typically 2–5° for 10 mm plate, empirically determined for a given procedure) or by using a balanced double-V or double-J preparation on plate above 12 mm to distribute shrinkage symmetrically about the neutral axis. Inter-pass sequence matters enormously in multi-pass welds: backstepping (welding short segments in the direction opposite to overall weld progression) reduces net longitudinal shrinkage, and alternating passes between opposite sides of a symmetric joint counteracts angular accumulation. Peening the weld bead immediately after each pass while it is still warm (above 150 °C, below the temperature where peening could crack martensite) mechanically introduces compressive strain that partially offsets tensile residual stress—a technique particularly valuable in repair welding of castings and frames where clamping is impractical.

Tungsten contamination in GTAW is a process-killer that is both easily caused and easily diagnosed. If the tungsten contacts the weld pool even momentarily, molten metal wicks onto the electrode tip and the arc becomes erratic, wandering, and wide—a symptom that persists even after the contaminated tip is removed from the pool. The only correct response is immediate arc termination, re-grinding of the tungsten (for DCEN), or balling and restarting (for AC on some machines), and inspection of the bead for tungsten inclusions, which appear as bright spots on X-ray and as hard, non-fusible foreign inclusions in a cross-section macro. Prevention is a matter of maintaining correct electrode angle (typically 15–20° from vertical in a torch-to-work sense), managing arc length to prevent the pool from rising to meet the electrode on positional welds, and using high-frequency (HF) arc starting rather than scratch starting so the electrode never physically contacts the work during initiation.

The heat input equation governs the relationship between parameters and the thermal cycle delivered to the base metal, and every welding procedure qualification (WPS/PQR under AWS D1.1 or ASME IX) requires that it be calculated and controlled within qualified limits:

$$Q = \frac{V \times I \times 60}{TS \times 1000} \quad [\text{kJ/mm}]$$

where $V$ is arc voltage, $I$ is welding current, and $TS$ is travel speed in mm/min. The factor of 60 converts to minutes, and dividing by 1000 converts joules to kilojoules. A thermal efficiency factor $\eta$ is applied for the process: 1.0 for SMAW, 0.8 for GMAW, 0.6 for GTAW (accounting for arc energy that heats the torch body, gas column, and surrounding atmosphere rather than the workpiece). This equation is the bridge between the variables the welder controls at the machine and the metallurgical outcomes in the HAZ—it explains why increasing travel speed is as effective as reducing current in controlling inter-pass temperature on austenitic stainless, and why a seemingly minor 10% reduction in travel speed can push the heat input over a critical threshold that triggers liquation cracking in nickel superalloys or excessive grain growth in the HAZ of normalized structural steel.

Mastery of arc welding at the advanced practitioner level is ultimately the internalization of this equation—not as a formula to be calculated before every pass, but as a physical intuition that manifests in how the hands adjust travel pace when the puddle runs ahead, how the foot pedal modulates current when the GTAW pool on a thin aluminum edge begins to collapse, and how the ears read the arc sound in GMAW to detect a contact tip beginning to restrict wire feed. The physics described in this lesson are not academic abstractions layered over a manual skill—they are the mechanical reality that the skill is encoding, and understanding them at this depth is what separates a practitioner who can follow a WPS from one who can write one.
