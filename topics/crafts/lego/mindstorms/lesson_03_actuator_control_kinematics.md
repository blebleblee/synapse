# Lesson 3: Actuator Control with Applied Kinematics

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The transformation of electrical energy into precise mechanical motion stands as one of the most fundamental challenges in robotics, and within the Lego Mindstorms ecosystem, this transformation occurs through servo motors equipped with integrated encoders that report angular position with remarkable fidelity. Understanding actuator control requires moving beyond the naïve notion that setting a motor's power level directly translates to predictable motion; in reality, the motor's output torque varies nonlinearly with angular velocity, load characteristics shift dynamically as the robot interacts with its environment, and the mechanical transmission path introduces compliance, backlash, and friction that corrupt the ideal relationship between commanded motion and achieved displacement.

At the heart of actuator control lies the encoder, a quadrature optical sensor that tracks the rotation of the motor shaft by observing transitions on two phase-shifted signal channels. The Lego EV3 Large Motor and Medium Motor both employ 360-count-per-revolution encoders, meaning that each complete rotation generates 360 discrete position increments. The quadrature encoding scheme—where two sensor channels produce square waves offset by 90 degrees in phase—enables the control system to determine not only the magnitude of rotation but also its direction. When channel A leads channel B during a state transition, the motor rotates in the forward direction; when channel B leads channel A, the motor rotates in reverse. This bidirectional position tracking forms the foundation upon which closed-loop control systems are constructed, allowing the Mindstorms brick to measure actual motor displacement and compare it against desired targets.

The mechanical characteristics of Mindstorms motors follow fundamental electromagnetic principles governing all DC motors. The torque-speed relationship exhibits an inverse linear correlation: at zero angular velocity (stall condition), the motor produces maximum torque, while at maximum no-load speed, the motor produces effectively zero torque. The EV3 Large Motor delivers approximately 0.4 Nm of stall torque at rated voltage, while the Medium Motor produces roughly 0.12 Nm. This torque-speed curve can be approximated as $\tau = \tau_{\text{stall}} - k_v \omega$, where $\tau$ represents instantaneous torque, $\tau_{\text{stall}}$ denotes stall torque, $k_v$ is the motor's velocity constant, and $\omega$ represents angular velocity in radians per second. Understanding this relationship proves critical when selecting gear ratios and designing mechanical transmissions, because the motor's ability to accelerate a load depends entirely on the available torque margin above the load's resistive torque at the current operating speed.

Gear reduction introduces a trade-off between torque amplification and angular velocity. When a small driving gear with $N_d$ teeth meshes with a larger driven gear containing $N_g$ teeth, the gear ratio $R = N_g / N_d$ determines both the mechanical advantage and the speed reduction. Output torque increases by the ratio $R$ (minus frictional losses), while output angular velocity decreases by the same factor. A 5:1 gear reduction applied to a Large Motor transforms the 0.4 Nm stall torque into 2.0 Nm at the output shaft, but reduces the no-load speed from approximately 170 RPM to 34 RPM. The selection of appropriate gear ratios requires careful analysis of the application's torque and speed requirements: high-acceleration systems benefit from lower gear ratios that preserve angular velocity, while heavy-load applications demand higher ratios to multiply available torque.

The concept of backlash—the angular slack present in gear meshes and mechanical joints—fundamentally limits positioning accuracy in any mechanical system constructed from discrete components. Lego gears typically exhibit 1-3 degrees of backlash per mesh due to the clearance required for smooth engagement and the inherent tolerances in injection-molded plastic parts. In a compound gear train containing four meshes, cumulative backlash can reach 8-12 degrees at the output shaft. This dead zone means that when the motor reverses direction, the output shaft remains stationary while the motor takes up the slack throughout the transmission. Control algorithms must account for this hysteresis by implementing backlash compensation strategies that preload the mechanism or model the expected slack as a state-dependent offset.

Differential drive kinematics—the mathematical framework governing two-wheeled robots where steering occurs through differential wheel speeds—provides the foundation for mobile robot control. A differential drive robot's instantaneous motion can be decomposed into forward velocity $v$ and angular velocity $\omega$ about the robot's vertical axis. When both wheels rotate at identical speeds, the robot travels in a straight line with velocity determined by the wheel radius and rotation rate. When the wheels rotate at different speeds, the robot follows a circular arc whose radius depends on the velocity differential between the wheels. The kinematic relationships governing this motion are given by $v = (v_L + v_R)/2$ and $\omega = (v_R - v_L)/L$, where $v_L$ and $v_R$ represent the left and right wheel velocities respectively, and $L$ denotes the track width (distance between wheel centers). These equations form the foundation for trajectory planning and path following algorithms that transform desired robot motions into specific motor commands.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The motor control architecture in Mindstorms systems operates as a nested control loop where position, velocity, and power form hierarchical layers of increasing abstraction. At the lowest level, pulse-width modulation (PWM) drives the motor's H-bridge circuit, delivering averaged voltage to the motor windings by rapidly switching between full supply voltage and zero voltage at frequencies typically exceeding 1 kHz. The duty cycle—the fraction of time the switch remains closed during each PWM period—determines the effective voltage applied to the motor. A 50% duty cycle delivers half the supply voltage, while a 100% duty cycle applies the full battery voltage to maximize torque output.

The velocity control layer builds upon the PWM foundation by implementing a feedback controller that adjusts motor power to achieve a target angular velocity. The EV3 firmware maintains an internal velocity estimate derived by differentiating the encoder position measurements over time. This velocity estimate updates at approximately 100 Hz, providing sufficient bandwidth for responsive control of typical robot motions. The velocity controller computes the error between the desired velocity setpoint and the measured velocity, then applies a control law to determine the appropriate PWM duty cycle. Simple proportional control—where motor power scales linearly with velocity error—often suffices for non-critical applications, but sophisticated systems employ proportional-integral (PI) or proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controllers to achieve superior tracking performance.

PID control theory describes a feedback control strategy that combines three distinct error terms to compute control effort. The proportional term $P$ responds to the current error magnitude, generating control action proportional to how far the system deviates from the setpoint. The integral term $I$ accumulates error over time, providing persistent control effort that eliminates steady-state offsets caused by friction or load torque. The derivative term $D$ responds to the rate of error change, providing damping that prevents overshoot and oscillation. The complete PID control law takes the form:

$$u(t) = K_p e(t) + K_i \int_0^t e(\tau) d\tau + K_d \frac{de(t)}{dt}$$

where $u(t)$ represents the control output (motor power), $e(t)$ denotes the error between setpoint and measured value, and $K_p$, $K_i$, and $K_d$ constitute the proportional, integral, and derivative gain coefficients respectively. Tuning these gain parameters determines the control system's responsiveness, stability, and disturbance rejection characteristics.

The position control layer operates at the outermost level of the control hierarchy, commanding the velocity controller to achieve angular displacements measured in degrees or rotations. When a Mindstorms program commands a motor to rotate 360 degrees at 50% power, the firmware executes a motion profile that accelerates the motor to the target velocity, maintains that velocity during the bulk of the motion, and decelerates as the encoder approaches the target position. This trajectory planning prevents abrupt starts and stops that could cause mechanical shock, wheel slippage, or structural damage to the robot. The acceleration and deceleration phases typically employ trapezoidal velocity profiles where velocity increases linearly during acceleration, remains constant during the cruise phase, and decreases linearly during deceleration.

Synchronization between multiple motors demands coordination beyond simple independent control. When two motors must maintain precise angular relationship—such as the drive wheels of a differential drive robot traveling in a straight line—small variations in motor performance, gear friction, or load distribution cause the motors to drift out of synchronization. The EV3 firmware provides synchronized motion commands that actively compensate for these discrepancies by measuring the position error between the motors and applying corrective velocity adjustments to the lagging motor. This synchronization operates as a coupled control system where each motor's velocity setpoint includes both the base trajectory velocity and a correction term proportional to the inter-motor position error:

```text
Synchronization Control Architecture:

Base Trajectory → ω_target → [+] → Motor A Velocity Controller → Motor A
                               ↑
                               |
                    Position Error (θ_A - θ_B) × K_sync
                               |
                               ↓
Base Trajectory → ω_target → [+] → Motor B Velocity Controller → Motor B
```

The synchronization gain $K_{\text{sync}}$ determines how aggressively the system corrects inter-motor position errors. Higher gains provide tighter synchronization but risk oscillatory behavior if set too aggressively, while lower gains reduce oscillation risk at the cost of larger steady-state position offsets between the motors.

Steering geometry calculations transform high-level navigation commands—such as "drive forward 50 cm" or "turn right 90 degrees"—into specific motor rotation angles. For a differential drive robot with wheel radius $r$ and track width $L$, the relationship between motor rotations and robot displacement follows from the arc length formula. A linear displacement $d$ requires each wheel to rotate through an angle $\theta = d/r$ radians, or equivalently $(d/r) \times (180/\pi)$ degrees. A pure rotation of $\alpha$ radians about the robot's center requires the wheels to rotate in opposite directions through angles $\theta = \pm \alpha L / (2r)$. These kinematic equations enable precise dead-reckoning navigation where the robot estimates its position by integrating wheel encoder measurements over time.

The transformation from world coordinates to motor commands requires inverting the forward kinematic equations. Given a desired robot velocity $(v, \omega)$ expressed in the robot's body frame, where $v$ represents forward velocity and $\omega$ represents angular velocity about the vertical axis, the required wheel velocities emerge from the inverse kinematic transform:

$$v_L = v - \frac{L \omega}{2}, \quad v_R = v + \frac{L \omega}{2}$$

These wheel velocities convert to angular velocities by dividing by the wheel radius: $\omega_L = v_L / r$ and $\omega_R = v_R / r$. The motor controller accepts these angular velocity commands and executes the appropriate PWM signals to achieve the desired motion. This kinematic chain—from task-space velocities through wheel velocities to motor angular velocities to PWM duty cycles—forms the complete control pipeline that transforms high-level navigation intentions into low-level electrical signals.

Backlash compensation strategies attempt to mitigate the positioning errors introduced by gear train slack. The simplest approach involves always approaching a target position from the same direction, ensuring that the gear backlash remains consistently loaded in one direction. When a motor must reverse direction to reach a target, the control system commands the motor to overshoot slightly beyond the target, then approach from the preferred direction. More sophisticated algorithms model backlash as a state-dependent dead zone and apply preemptive control corrections when direction reversals occur. These corrections typically take the form of an additional rotation angle equal to the estimated cumulative backlash in the gear train, applied immediately after detecting a direction reversal in the motor command sequence.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Achieving precise motion control requires careful calibration of PID gain parameters, a process that balances competing objectives of responsiveness, stability, and disturbance rejection. The classical Ziegler-Nichols tuning method provides a systematic starting point: increase the proportional gain $K_p$ until the system exhibits sustained oscillation at the stability boundary, then record the critical gain $K_u$ and oscillation period $T_u$. The PID gains are then computed as $K_p = 0.6 K_u$, $K_i = 2 K_p / T_u$, and $K_d = K_p T_u / 8$. While Mindstorms systems rarely expose direct access to firmware PID parameters, understanding these tuning principles informs the selection of motion command parameters such as acceleration ramps and power limits that indirectly influence control loop behavior.

Wheel slippage represents one of the most common failure modes in differential drive navigation, particularly during high-acceleration maneuvers or when operating on low-traction surfaces. When a wheel loses traction, the encoder reports continued rotation while the robot fails to achieve the expected displacement, causing cumulative position estimation errors that compound over time. Detecting wheel slip requires comparing expected motion—derived from motor encoder readings—against independent measurements such as gyroscope angular velocity or external position tracking. When significant discrepancies emerge between encoder-based velocity estimates and gyroscope measurements, the control system can reduce acceleration demands or enter a degraded navigation mode that relies less heavily on dead-reckoning position estimates.

Load-induced velocity variations plague systems that attempt to maintain constant speed under varying mechanical loads. As a differential drive robot climbs an incline or encounters increased rolling resistance, the motor's operating point shifts leftward along the torque-speed curve toward higher torque and lower speed. Without active velocity feedback control, motor speed decreases proportionally to the increased load torque. Velocity control systems compensate for these load variations by monitoring motor speed through encoder differentiation and increasing motor power when measured velocity falls below the setpoint. The integral term in a PI or PID controller proves particularly effective at eliminating steady-state velocity errors caused by constant load torques, because the integrator accumulates error over time until sufficient control effort develops to overcome the resistive load.

Electromagnetic interference (EMI) from motor PWM switching can corrupt encoder signals, particularly when using long cable runs or operating near strong electromagnetic fields. Encoder noise manifests as spurious position jumps or velocity spikes in the telemetry stream, potentially destabilizing control loops that respond to these false measurements. Mitigation strategies include implementing digital low-pass filters on encoder velocity estimates, requiring that multiple consecutive measurements confirm direction changes before accepting them as valid, and physically separating encoder signal wires from high-current motor power conductors. The firmware's internal filters typically reject single-sample encoder anomalies, but sustained EMI can overwhelm these defenses and degrade control performance.

Thermal effects in motor windings introduce time-varying performance characteristics that complicate control system design. As motor current flows through the copper windings, resistive heating elevates the winding temperature, which increases the copper's electrical resistance according to the temperature coefficient of resistivity. Higher winding resistance reduces the current available at a given applied voltage, thereby decreasing torque output. A motor operating continuously at high power levels may exhibit 20-30% torque reduction after thermal equilibrium compared to cold-start performance. Control systems that fail to account for thermal effects may struggle to maintain consistent performance during extended operation, particularly when executing repetitive tasks that prevent thermal dissipation intervals.

Gear train efficiency losses accumulate multiplicatively through compound gear trains, with each mesh typically losing 2-5% of input power to friction. A four-stage gear train with 95% efficiency per stage delivers only $(0.95)^4 = 0.81$ or 81% of the input power to the output shaft. These losses manifest as reduced output torque and increased thermal dissipation within the gearbox. High-efficiency designs minimize the number of gear meshes, ensure proper gear engagement depth, and maintain adequate lubrication on all contacting surfaces. Lego gears typically operate without supplemental lubrication in their as-molded condition, but high-load applications may benefit from light application of PTFE-based dry lubricant to reduce friction in critical meshes.

Motion planning primitives provide reusable building blocks for complex robot behaviors. The point-turn maneuver—where the robot rotates about its center point by driving the wheels in opposite directions at equal speeds—enables precise heading changes without translational displacement. The arc-turn maneuver executes circular paths by maintaining a constant velocity ratio between the wheels, with the turn radius determined by $R = L(v_L + v_R)/(2(v_R - v_L))$. The pivot-turn rotates the robot about one stationary wheel, doubling the turn rate compared to point-turns but introducing translational displacement of the robot's center point. Each primitive offers distinct advantages: point-turns minimize space requirements in confined environments, arc-turns provide smooth trajectory following with continuous motion, and pivot-turns maximize angular acceleration when one wheel can be locked.

Path following control strategies regulate the robot's lateral offset and heading error relative to a desired trajectory. Pure pursuit control—a geometric path following algorithm—commands the robot to steer toward a pursuit point located at a fixed look-ahead distance along the desired path. The steering curvature $\kappa$ required to reach the pursuit point from the robot's current position follows from the geometric relationship $\kappa = 2\sin(\alpha)/L_d$, where $\alpha$ represents the angle between the robot's heading and the line connecting the robot to the pursuit point, and $L_d$ denotes the look-ahead distance. Larger look-ahead distances produce smoother paths but increase corner-cutting on sharp turns, while smaller look-ahead distances provide tighter path tracking at the cost of increased steering oscillation.

Velocity profiling prevents control saturation and mechanical shock by constraining acceleration and deceleration rates within the capabilities of the actuator system. Trapezoidal velocity profiles specify linear acceleration from zero to cruise velocity, constant velocity during the cruise phase, and linear deceleration to zero at the target position. The profile parameters—maximum velocity $v_{\max}$, maximum acceleration $a_{\max}$, and total displacement $d$—determine whether the motion includes a cruise phase or transitions directly from acceleration to deceleration. When the displacement proves insufficient to reach maximum velocity, the motion becomes triangular rather than trapezoidal, with the peak velocity computed as $v_{\text{peak}} = \sqrt{a_{\max} d}$. S-curve profiles extend this concept by limiting the jerk (rate of acceleration change), producing even smoother motion at the cost of increased computational complexity and longer transition times.

Sensor fusion techniques combine encoder-based position estimates with external measurements such as gyroscope angular rates, compass headings, or visual odometry to improve navigation accuracy. A complementary filter merges high-frequency encoder velocity measurements with low-frequency compass heading updates to maintain accurate heading estimates over extended periods, exploiting the encoder's superior short-term accuracy and the compass's immunity to cumulative drift. The filter applies a weighted average: $\theta_{\text{fused}} = \alpha \theta_{\text{encoder}} + (1-\alpha) \theta_{\text{compass}}$, where $\alpha \in [0,1]$ determines the relative trust placed in each sensor. Kalman filtering extends this concept by modeling sensor noise characteristics explicitly and computing optimal state estimates that minimize mean-squared error, though the computational requirements often exceed the capabilities of Mindstorms hardware platforms.

The transition from open-loop to closed-loop control fundamentally transforms system behavior and failure characteristics. Open-loop systems execute predetermined motor commands without measuring actual motion, accepting whatever displacement results from those commands. These systems prove simple to implement and predictable under nominal conditions but exhibit no ability to compensate for load variations, mechanical wear, or environmental disturbances. Closed-loop systems continuously measure actual system state and adjust control efforts to null tracking errors, providing robust performance across varying conditions at the cost of increased complexity and potential instability if poorly tuned. The decision between open-loop and closed-loop control hinges on application requirements: tasks demanding repeatability and precision mandate closed-loop control, while simple, non-critical motions may accept open-loop execution to minimize implementation complexity and computational overhead.
