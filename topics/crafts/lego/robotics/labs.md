# Operational Verification Labs: Robotics

## Objective & Operational Lab Setup

The operational verification laboratory represents the critical transition from theoretical design to physical validation of robotic systems built from Lego Technic components. Unlike software testing where failures leave only log entries, mechanical systems under operational stress reveal themselves through bearing wear, gear tooth deformation, structural buckling, and dynamic instabilities that no CAD simulation can fully predict. This laboratory protocol focuses on quantitative measurement of robot performance across three operational domains: kinematic precision under controlled motion profiles, drivetrain efficiency under variable torque loads, and structural integrity when subjected to cyclic stress beyond nominal design parameters.

Your laboratory workbench must be configured as a dedicated metrology and diagnostic station. The primary work surface should be a rigid platform—a sheet of 12mm plywood or MDF at least 900mm × 600mm serves well—covered with a non-slip rubber mat to prevent baseplate migration during testing. Secure a 48×48 stud Lego baseplate to this surface using painter's tape at the corners; this becomes your datum reference plane for all positional measurements. Position a digital caliper with 0.01mm resolution within arm's reach; you will measure link displacement, wheel circumference, and structural deflection repeatedly. A smartphone or tablet running a slow-motion video application at minimum 120fps becomes your primary motion analysis instrument for capturing gear mesh behavior and linkage kinematics that occur too rapidly for naked-eye observation.

For torque and force measurement, construct a simple beam scale from Lego: suspend a rigid liftarm horizontally on a pivot pin at its center, hang calibrated masses (standard workshop weights or measured water-filled containers) from one end, and attach your robot's output linkage to the other. By measuring the displacement required to lift known masses through measured distances, you derive mechanical advantage and output torque using the lever law $\tau = F \times r$, where force is mass times gravitational acceleration (9.81 m/s²) and radius is the perpendicular distance from pivot to load point. A kitchen scale with 1-gram resolution serves for static load testing and motor stall force measurement.

Power delivery measurement requires a USB current/voltage meter inserted between your Lego Hub battery and the power port, or if using external battery packs, a DC wattmeter capable of reading instantaneous current draw at 100Hz sampling rate. Record voltage under no-load, nominal-load, and stall conditions; the voltage sag curve reveals internal resistance of your battery system and whether your power supply can sustain peak current demands. Motor performance characterization demands a tachometer; the simplest approach uses a slotted disk mounted on the motor shaft and an optical sensor (or simply count interruptions of a smartphone flashlight beam with your camera) to measure rotations per second, converting to angular velocity in radians per second by multiplying by $2\pi$.

Environmental control matters more than hobbyists typically acknowledge. Lego ABS plastic exhibits a glass transition beginning around 105°C but significant thermal expansion occurs at deltas as small as 10°C from room temperature. A beam constrained at both ends will buckle if heated; a gear mesh set at 20°C will bind at 5°C as the nylon pin contracts. Conduct all baseline measurements at a recorded ambient temperature between 20-24°C, and if testing thermal performance, use a heat gun and infrared thermometer to create controlled thermal gradients. Humidity affects friction coefficients in pin joints; maintain relative humidity between 40-60% for repeatable results, measuring with a simple hygrometer.

Data recording follows a laboratory notebook discipline. A bound notebook—not loose sheets—records every measurement with timestamp, ambient conditions, and configuration state. Photograph each robot configuration from multiple angles before beginning a test sequence. Export all digital sensor logs immediately after collection and archive with filename conventions that embed date, robot identifier, and test protocol: `20250115_drivetrain_alpha_torque_sweep.csv`. Every catastrophic failure receives a dedicated failure report documenting pre-failure conditions, failure mode (fracture surface photos at multiple magnifications), and root cause hypothesis.

## Exercise 1: Foundational Practical Implementation

The first verification exercise constructs and characterizes a differential-drive mobile robot platform, the fundamental wheeled robot architecture that dominates everything from warehouse logistics robots to Mars rovers. This configuration uses two independently powered wheels on a common axis with one or more passive casters for stability, enabling zero-radius turning and intuitive kinematic control. The exercise objectives are threefold: achieve straight-line positional accuracy within ±2% over one meter of travel, demonstrate repeatable 90-degree turns within ±3 degrees, and measure the relationship between commanded motor power and actual linear velocity across the full throttle range.

Begin construction with the drivetrain module. Select two Large motors (the angular position encoders are essential for closed-loop odometry) and mount them in mirror-image configuration on a 7×11 liftarm frame. The motor output shafts should be parallel and separated by exactly 11 studs (88mm), measured center-to-center with calipers. Attach a gear reduction stage to each motor: a 12-tooth gear on the motor shaft meshing with a 36-tooth gear on the wheel axle provides a 3:1 reduction ratio, converting the motor's high-speed low-torque output to lower wheel speed with tripled torque. This reduction is critical; direct drive from Large motors produces excessive speed and insufficient torque for controlled motion, causing wheel slip during acceleration.

Mount wheels with 62.4mm diameter (the large Technic wheels with tire) on each driven axle. Pin the wheel to the axle using two half-bushings on the inboard side and one full bushing on the outboard side, ensuring zero axial play—test by attempting to slide the wheel along the axle; any movement indicates insufficient clamping force. The effective wheel circumference is $C = \pi d = 196mm$; verify this empirically by marking the tire contact point with chalk, rolling exactly five rotations forward, and measuring the traveled distance with a tape measure. Divide by five to obtain your calibrated circumference, which will differ from the theoretical value by 2-4% due to tire compliance and tread depth.

The chassis structure must satisfy conflicting requirements: wide wheelbase for stability, compact footprint for maneuverability, and sufficient ground clearance to prevent belly-dragging over irregular terrain. Construct a rectangular frame from 11-stud and 13-stud liftarms forming a perimeter, reinforced by diagonal cross-bracing in the same plane to resist torsional twist. Mount the drivetrain module to the underside of this chassis with the wheel axle positioned 3-4 studs forward of the geometric center; this forward bias prevents backwards tipping when the robot rapidly decelerates. At the rear, install a single ball caster or an omnidirectional wheel—the latter is preferable for smooth motion but must be mounted with its rotation axis perpendicular to the wheelbase axis. The caster mounting height is critical: adjust it so that when the robot sits on a flat surface, the two driven wheels and caster all make firm contact simultaneously. Test by attempting to rock the robot forward and back; any wobble indicates incorrect caster height and will cause erratic tracking.

Install the Lego Powered Up Hub or Spike Prime Hub on the top surface of the chassis, positioning it near the wheelbase centerline to minimize moment of inertia around the vertical axis. Route motor cables through the chassis structure using cable-management clips fashioned from 1×2 plates with clips; loose cables that snag on obstacles have destroyed more test runs than mechanical failures. Connect the left motor to port A and right motor to port B, establishing a consistent convention that all subsequent code will respect. Power on the hub and verify bidirectional motor function with the device's manual control interface before proceeding to programmatic control.

The kinematic control program implements a minimal differential-drive controller with separate functions for forward translation, point turns, and odometry-based dead reckoning. Using Python with the Pybricks library for Spike Prime, establish the motor interface with position tracking enabled:

```python
from pybricks.hubs import PrimeHub
from pybricks.pupdevices import Motor
from pybricks.parameters import Port, Direction
from pybricks.tools import wait
import math

hub = PrimeHub()
motor_left = Motor(Port.A, Direction.COUNTERCLOCKWISE)
motor_right = Motor(Port.B, Direction.CLOCKWISE)

WHEEL_DIAMETER = 62.4  # mm, measured empirically
WHEELBASE = 88.0  # mm, axle separation center-to-center
GEAR_RATIO = 3.0  # 36-tooth driven / 12-tooth driver

# Convert motor degrees to linear distance traveled
def motor_degrees_to_mm(degrees):
    wheel_rotations = degrees / (360.0 * GEAR_RATIO)
    distance = wheel_rotations * math.pi * WHEEL_DIAMETER
    return distance

# Convert linear distance to required motor degrees
def mm_to_motor_degrees(distance):
    wheel_rotations = distance / (math.pi * WHEEL_DIAMETER)
    motor_degrees = wheel_rotations * 360.0 * GEAR_RATIO
    return motor_degrees

# Straight-line motion with closed-loop position control
def drive_straight(distance_mm, speed_deg_per_sec=200):
    target_degrees = mm_to_motor_degrees(distance_mm)
    motor_left.reset_angle(0)
    motor_right.reset_angle(0)
    
    motor_left.run_target(speed_deg_per_sec, target_degrees, wait=False)
    motor_right.run_target(speed_deg_per_sec, target_degrees, wait=True)
    
    # Measure actual travel for calibration
    actual_left = motor_left.angle()
    actual_right = motor_right.angle()
    actual_distance = motor_degrees_to_mm((actual_left + actual_right) / 2.0)
    
    return actual_distance

# Point turn with differential wheel motion
def turn_in_place(angle_degrees, speed_deg_per_sec=150):
    # Arc length for each wheel
    arc_length = (angle_degrees / 360.0) * math.pi * WHEELBASE
    wheel_rotation_degrees = mm_to_motor_degrees(arc_length)
    
    motor_left.reset_angle(0)
    motor_right.reset_angle(0)
    
    if angle_degrees > 0:  # Counter-clockwise turn
        motor_left.run_target(speed_deg_per_sec, -wheel_rotation_degrees, wait=False)
        motor_right.run_target(speed_deg_per_sec, wheel_rotation_degrees, wait=True)
    else:  # Clockwise turn
        motor_left.run_target(speed_deg_per_sec, abs(wheel_rotation_degrees), wait=False)
        motor_right.run_target(speed_deg_per_sec, -abs(wheel_rotation_degrees), wait=True)
    
    actual_left = motor_left.angle()
    actual_right = motor_right.angle()
    
    return actual_left, actual_right
```

Execute the straight-line accuracy protocol. Place the robot on a smooth, hard floor with low-friction coefficient (laminate or sealed concrete; avoid carpet which introduces unpredictable drag). Mark the starting position of one wheel contact point with masking tape. Command the robot to drive forward exactly 1000mm using `drive_straight(1000)`. Mark the ending wheel position and measure the actual traveled distance with a metal tape measure held taut. Calculate the error percentage: $\epsilon = \frac{|d_{\text{actual}} - d_{\text{commanded}}|}{d_{\text{commanded}}} \times 100\%$. Repeat this measurement five times, returning the robot to the start position manually between runs. Acceptable performance shows mean error below 2% with standard deviation below 1%.

If error exceeds tolerance, diagnose the root cause systematically. Errors consistently in one direction (always overshooting or always undershooting) indicate incorrect wheel diameter calibration—measure the actual rolling circumference again using the five-revolution method and update `WHEEL_DIAMETER` accordingly. Left-right asymmetric error causing curved trajectories instead of straight lines indicates motor performance mismatch; swap the left and right motors to determine if the error follows the motor or stays with the side. If error follows the motor, that motor requires internal inspection for grit in the gear train or encoder issues. If error stays with the side, check for differential friction in the wheel bearings or gear mesh binding.

Proceed to rotational accuracy testing. Position the robot at the center of a large sheet of paper and trace the outline of the chassis. Command a 90-degree counter-clockwise turn using `turn_in_place(90)`. Trace the new chassis position on the same paper. Measure the angular displacement by extending lines from the center of rotation through corresponding features on both traced outlines and measuring the angle with a protractor; digital protractor applications on smartphones provide ±0.5-degree accuracy. Repeat the 90-degree turn four times to complete a full rotation, returning ideally to the starting orientation. The cumulative error after 360 degrees of rotation reveals systematic bias in your `WHEELBASE` calibration constant.

The velocity characterization protocol maps commanded motor speed to actual robot linear velocity across the operational range. Mount a metronome application on your smartphone or use a stopwatch with lap function. Mark a precisely measured 2-meter course on the floor with tape. Starting from rest at the starting line, command the robot forward at constant motor speed of 100 degrees/second using `motor_left.run(100)` and `motor_right.run(100)` simultaneously. Trigger the timer as the front axle crosses the start line and stop it at the finish line. Calculate velocity: $v = d/t = 2000mm / t_{\text{seconds}}$. Repeat for motor speeds of 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 degrees/second, recording the velocity achieved at each commanded speed.

Plot commanded speed (x-axis) versus measured velocity (y-axis). The relationship should be approximately linear in the mid-range but will exhibit saturation at high speeds where motor torque becomes insufficient to overcome drag, and dead-band at very low speeds where static friction prevents motion until a threshold torque is reached. Identify the maximum reliable speed where the robot maintains stable straight-line tracking without wobble or wheel slip. This becomes your operational speed limit for autonomous navigation routines. Measure the no-load current draw and loaded current draw at this optimal speed using your inline current meter; the difference reveals the mechanical efficiency of your drivetrain. A well-built system draws 200-400mA per motor under moderate cruise conditions; draws exceeding 600mA indicate excessive friction or gear mesh interference requiring disassembly and inspection.

## Exercise 2: Parameter Variation & Empirical Analysis

The second verification exercise investigates how design parameters affect robot performance through systematic variation of gear ratios, wheel diameters, and load mass. Rather than building entirely new robots for each configuration, you will construct a modular drivetrain testbed that permits rapid reconfiguration, establishing quantitative relationships between mechanical design choices and operational outcomes. This empirical approach builds physical intuition about trade-offs that CAD software cannot reveal: how gear ratio affects acceleration versus top speed, how wheel size influences energy efficiency, and how center-of-mass position determines tipping stability.

Modify your differential-drive robot from Exercise 1 to accept interchangeable gear reduction stages. The motor output shaft remains fixed, but the driven gear and wheel axle assembly should be removable as a complete module. Construct three separate reduction modules using gear pairs readily available in Lego Technic sets: a 1:1 direct drive (12-tooth to 12-tooth for mechanical isolation), a 2:1 reduction (12-tooth to 24-tooth), and the original 3:1 reduction (12-tooth to 36-tooth). Each module must maintain the same wheel axle height above the chassis to prevent geometry changes that would confound your measurements. Use spacer bushings and shims as necessary to achieve this constraint.

The gear ratio sweep protocol measures maximum achievable velocity and acceleration distance for each reduction ratio. Install the 1:1 direct-drive module first. On a smooth floor with at least 4 meters of unobstructed straight-line space, position the robot at a marked starting line. Command both motors to full power (1000 degrees/second or maximum rated speed) and simultaneously start your timer. Mark the position where the robot first reaches steady-state maximum velocity—identified by the motor sound stabilizing to a constant pitch—and measure the distance from start to this point; this is your acceleration distance. Continue at full speed and measure the time required to traverse a precisely measured 2-meter interval at steady state, calculating maximum velocity. Record the peak current draw during acceleration and the steady-state cruise current from your inline meter.

Repeat this protocol with the 2:1 and 3:1 reduction modules, recording all measurements in a structured table. The direct-drive configuration will achieve the highest maximum velocity but longest acceleration distance and possibly poorest straight-line stability if wheel slip occurs during acceleration. The 3:1 reduction will show the shortest acceleration distance, lowest top speed, and highest stability. Calculate the mechanical advantage ratio: $MA = \frac{\tau_{\text{output}}}{\tau_{\text{input}}} = \text{gear ratio}$, and verify that acceleration distance scales inversely with mechanical advantage since $F = ma$ and higher torque produces higher acceleration force.

Quantify the energy efficiency trade-off by measuring battery consumption over a fixed-distance mission. Fully charge your hub battery or install a fresh set of cells with known initial voltage. Command the robot to traverse a 20-meter closed-loop course at 70% of maximum speed for each gear ratio configuration. After completing the course, measure the battery voltage immediately; the voltage drop under load-normalized conditions reveals relative energy consumption. Convert to power consumption using $P = VI$, integrating current over time if your meter supports data logging. The most efficient configuration balances the motor's peak efficiency operating point (typically 60-70% of no-load speed) against mechanical losses in the gear train (higher reduction ratios have more gear mesh friction).

The wheel diameter variation series requires three different wheel sizes: the small 43.2mm diameter wheel, the medium 56mm wheel, and the large 62.4mm wheel used in Exercise 1. Maintain the 3:1 gear ratio for this sequence to isolate wheel size as the independent variable. Construct wheel mounting hubs that position each wheel size at the same height above ground, adjusting axle mounting points in the chassis as necessary. The smaller wheels will require lowering the drivetrain module; the alternative approach fixes the axle height and adjusts caster height, though this introduces ground clearance as a confounding variable.

Execute the standardized velocity and acceleration protocol with each wheel size, maintaining constant commanded motor speed of 600 degrees/second. The larger wheel diameter produces higher linear velocity for the same motor angular velocity, following $v = \omega r$ where $\omega$ is angular velocity in radians per second and $r$ is wheel radius. Calculate the theoretical velocity for each wheel size and compare to measured values; discrepancies reveal tire compliance and slip losses. Measure current draw at steady state for each configuration; smaller wheels spinning faster may show higher losses due to increased bearing friction and air resistance, while larger wheels may show higher losses from greater tire deformation.

Assess traction and slip characteristics by conducting a maximum-load test. Construct a payload sled from a flat Lego plate with low-friction tiles on the bottom surface, connected to the robot's rear by a string or thread. Load the sled with incrementally increasing mass (use calibrated weights, bags of rice, or water bottles of known volume). Command the robot forward at 50% speed across a smooth floor. Incrementally increase sled mass until the wheels begin to slip, identified by motor encoder advancement without corresponding forward motion or by squealing sounds from the tire-floor interface. Record the maximum towable mass for each wheel size. Smaller wheels concentrate normal force over smaller contact patch area, increasing contact pressure and potentially improving traction, but the reduced mechanical advantage may offset this benefit.

The center-of-mass manipulation experiment reveals the critical importance of mass distribution for stability. Add a 200-gram payload mass (a large battery pack or purpose-built weight) to the robot chassis. Mount it in three configurations: forward near the driven wheels, centered over the wheelbase midpoint, and rearward near the caster. For each configuration, measure the maximum acceleration the robot can achieve before the caster lifts off the ground or before wheel slip occurs. Position a thin sheet of paper under the caster; when the caster unloads, you can slide the paper freely. Command increasingly aggressive acceleration profiles using ramped speed commands and observe the point where instability occurs.

Quantify the tipping threshold using a static measurement. Tilt the robot slowly forward around the driven wheel axis until the caster just lifts off the ground. Measure the tilt angle $\theta$ with a digital inclinometer or protractor. The tipping condition occurs when the vertical projection of the center of mass passes through the driven wheel axle contact line. Calculate the center of mass position relative to the axle using $x_{COM} = h \tan(\theta)$, where $h$ is the height of the center of mass above the axle. The forward payload configuration will show the smallest safe tilt angle; the rearward configuration the largest, demonstrating why maintaining a rearward or neutral center of mass is critical for aggressive acceleration maneuvers.

Complete this exercise by constructing a multi-dimensional performance map. Create a spreadsheet or Python script that plots maximum velocity, acceleration distance, energy efficiency (meters per watt-hour), and maximum towable load as functions of gear ratio and wheel diameter. Use a matrix visualization or 3D surface plot to identify the Pareto-optimal configurations—those that cannot be improved in one dimension without sacrificing another. This empirical dataset becomes a design reference for future robot projects, allowing you to select gear ratios and wheel sizes based on mission requirements: high-speed couriers need large wheels and low reduction, heavy-payload transport needs small wheels and high reduction.

## Exercise 3: Stress Testing, Boundary Failure & Recovery Protocol

The third verification exercise deliberately pushes your robotic system beyond nominal operating parameters to map failure modes, identify weak links in the mechanical chain, and develop diagnostic protocols for field troubleshooting. Unlike consumer products designed to never fail within rated specifications, research and competition robots routinely operate at the edge of their performance envelope, making failure prediction and rapid recovery essential operational skills. This exercise codifies a systematic methodology for stress testing that balances the need for empirical failure data against the cost of destructive testing on limited hardware.

Begin with motor thermal stress testing to establish the continuous-duty operating envelope. Remove both motors from the robot chassis and mount them on a test fixture that allows free rotation with no mechanical load beyond the motor's internal gear train. Connect both power leads and encoder cable to your hub, and install the inline current meter. At room temperature (20-24°C), command the motor to full power (1000 degrees/second) and simultaneously begin timing. Monitor motor case temperature using an infrared thermometer, taking readings at 30-second intervals on the black plastic motor housing directly above the motor coil. Record current draw continuously; it will initially be high and should stabilize within 30-60 seconds as bearing friction reaches thermal equilibrium.

Continue the full-power run until motor temperature stabilizes—typically 10-15 minutes—or until temperature exceeds 65°C, whichever occurs first. Modern Lego motors incorporate thermal protection that reduces output power at approximately 70°C and shuts down entirely near 80°C to prevent permanent magnet demagnetization. If your motor reaches the thermal limit, note the time-to-limit and peak power output. Allow the motor to cool to room temperature (at least 30 minutes) before proceeding. Repeat the thermal test at 75%, 50%, and 25% power levels, recording time-to-thermal-equilibrium and equilibrium temperature for each. Plot power level versus steady-state temperature; the linear relationship reveals the motor's thermal resistance coefficient.

Calculate the continuous-duty safe operating power as the level that produces steady-state temperature 15-20°C below the thermal shutdown threshold, providing safety margin for elevated ambient temperatures and restricted airflow inside robot chassis enclosures. This measured value typically falls between 40-60% of maximum rated power for Lego motors. Your Exercise 1 and 2 velocity measurements that demanded sustained high power may have been operating in a thermal failure mode that would reveal itself only on longer missions; compare your measured "maximum" velocities against this thermal-limited continuous power level and revise your operational speed limits accordingly.

Gear train shock load testing identifies the instantaneous torque limit before tooth shearing or pin ejection failure. Construct a stall test fixture by mounting your 3:1 gear reduction module in a rigid frame and connecting the output axle to a lever arm exactly 100mm long, measured from axle center to the load attachment point. Suspend increasingly heavy masses from the lever arm endpoint while the motor is unpowered, allowing you to directly measure the stall torque capacity. Begin with 500 grams; the output torque is $\tau = F \times r = (0.5 kg \times 9.81 m/s^2) \times 0.1m = 0.49 Nm$. Incrementally add 200-gram masses, inspecting the gear mesh after each addition for signs of tooth deformation (white stress marks on the gray plastic) or pin deflection.

The failure will manifest in one of three modes: gear tooth shearing at the root, gear bore fracture causing the gear to spin freely on the axle, or axle pin ejection from the liftarm holes under bending load. Record the failure load and failure mode. Calculate the gear mesh force using the pitch radius of your driven gear (36-tooth gear has pitch diameter approximately 57mm for Lego's module-1 tooth profile, giving pitch radius 28.5mm): $F_{\text{mesh}} = \tau_{\text{axle}} / r_{\text{pitch}}$. A 36-tooth gear typically fails by tooth shear at 12-16 Nm of output torque, corresponding to mesh forces of 420-560 Newtons. Smaller gears fail at lower absolute torque but similar contact stress, following the relationship $\sigma = F / A$ where contact area scales with face width and tooth height.

Inspect the failed component under magnification (a jeweler's loupe or macro photography with your smartphone). Tooth shear fractures show a characteristic failure surface perpendicular to the tooth length with crack initiation at the root fillet where stress concentration is maximum. Pin ejection failures result from exceeding the bearing strength of the ABS plastic liftarm, calculated as $P = F / (d \times t)$ where $d$ is pin diameter (approximately 5mm) and $t$ is liftarm thickness (approximately 6mm for standard liftarms). This failure mode becomes dominant when using long lever arms or high gear reductions that amplify motor torque beyond 8-10 Nm. Mitigation strategies include doubling the load-bearing pins (two parallel pins sharing the load), reducing gear ratio, or transitioning to worm gear drives that have inherent torque-limiting slip behavior.

Conduct a motor stall torture test to verify protective shutdown behavior under locked-rotor conditions. Install a motor in your chassis with the 3:1 reduction driving a wheel. Place the robot on the ground and position a heavy immovable object (a brick or dumbbell weight) directly in front of the wheel so forward motion is impossible. Command the motor to 100% forward power while monitoring current draw. The motor will initially draw peak stall current—typically 1.5-2.5 amperes for Large motors—and temperature will rise rapidly at approximately 3-5°C per second. The hub's current-limiting protection should activate within 2-3 seconds, reducing current to safe levels. If current remains above 1.5A for more than 5 seconds, immediately abort the test to prevent hub damage. Some hub firmware versions have inadequate protection; if you observe this, implement software current monitoring with automatic motor shutdown when current exceeds 1.2A for more than 1 second.

The structural fatigue test reveals cumulative damage from cyclic loading, a critical failure mode for competition robots that execute thousands of repetitive operations. Construct a test linkage consisting of a motor-driven crank arm connected through a pushrod to a pivoting liftarm that deflects and returns to neutral position once per revolution. The liftarm should be cantilevered 15 studs from its pivot point with a 100-gram mass at the free end, creating a bending moment of $M = F \times L = (1 N) \times (120 mm) = 0.12 Nm$. This produces cyclical tensile and compressive stress in the beam with each oscillation.

Program the motor to run continuously at moderate speed (300 degrees/second at motor output, producing approximately 1.67 Hz oscillation after 3:1 reduction). Allow the system to run for 10,000 cycles—approximately 100 minutes of operation—while periodically inspecting for crack initiation. Every 1000 cycles, stop the motor and inspect the liftarm at the pivot pin holes using magnifying optics and raking light to reveal surface cracks. Measure the deflection of the free end under static 100-gram load using calipers; increasing deflection over time indicates material fatigue softening or pin hole elongation. Most Lego ABS components will survive 50,000+ cycles under moderate stress but may show measurable wear after 10,000 cycles in high-stress joints.

Document any crack initiation location photographically. Cracks typically nucleate at stress concentrations: the inner corners of rectangular holes, the interface between pin bore and outer surface, or at sharp geometric transitions. This empirical data guides reinforcement strategies—adding doubling plates at high-stress joints, radiusing sharp corners with filed or sanded modifications, or replacing solid connections with compliant flexure joints that redistribute stress over larger areas.

The power system brownout test identifies minimum operating voltage and characterizes behavior during battery depletion. Begin with a fully charged battery (or fresh alkaline cells reading 9.5+ volts for 6-cell packs). Command a complex autonomous routine that uses both drive motors and a sensor (ultrasonic or color sensor drawing additional current). Record battery voltage and operational behavior every 60 seconds. Modern rechargeable batteries maintain relatively flat voltage over most of their discharge curve, then drop rapidly near depletion. Note the voltage at which motor performance visibly degrades (reduced speed or torque) and the voltage at which the hub shuts down or resets.

A healthy power system should maintain stable operation down to approximately 6.5-7.0 volts, below which the motor driver electronics cannot maintain regulation. If you observe resets or brownouts above 7.5 volts, diagnose high-resistance connections: remove and re-seat all power connectors, inspect for corrosion on battery contacts, and measure voltage directly at the hub input terminals under load using a multimeter. Voltage drop exceeding 0.5V between battery terminals and hub input indicates resistive losses in cabling or connectors that will limit available peak current.

Construct a failure recovery diagnostic flowchart based on your empirical stress test data. Begin with observable symptoms—motor not responding, unexpected motion, structural collapse, intermittent behavior—and develop a binary decision tree that guides systematic diagnosis. For example: Motor not responding → Check hub battery voltage (if below 7V, charge battery; if above 7V proceed) → Command motor manually via hub interface (if responds, software issue; if does not respond proceed) → Swap motor cable to different port (if now responds, original port damaged; if still does not respond proceed) → Disconnect and inspect cable for wire breakage at connector strain relief (if damaged, replace cable; if intact, motor internal failure).

Validate this diagnostic tree by introducing deliberate faults into a working robot configuration and verifying that the flowchart leads to correct diagnosis within 3-5 decision points. Common faults to simulate include: disconnected motor cable, depleted battery, stripped gear tooth, loose wheel on axle, jammed gear mesh from debris, and software communication timeout. Time your diagnostic process; experienced practitioners should isolate common faults within 2-3 minutes using systematic diagnosis versus 10-15 minutes using trial-and-error troubleshooting.

## Operational Reference Guide & Diagnostic Checklist

The operational reference guide consolidates measurement standards, tolerance specifications, and diagnostic protocols into a field-usable format for routine verification and troubleshooting. This section functions as both a calibration standard for new robot builds and a rapid-response guide during competition or demonstration scenarios where time pressure demands efficient diagnosis.

### Dimensional Calibration Standards

Establish and maintain a set of calibrated reference measurements specific to your component inventory, as manufacturing tolerance in Lego parts introduces 0.1-0.2mm variation that accumulates across assemblies. Measure the actual rolling circumference of each wheel type by the five-revolution method described in Exercise 1, recording the value on the wheel hub with permanent marker. For your gear inventory, measure the actual center-to-center distance of meshed gear pairs mounted in liftarm holes; the theoretical distance calculated from pitch diameter assumes zero backlash, but functional meshes require 0.2-0.4mm clearance. This measured distance becomes the calibration constant for turn radius calculations.

The wheelbase measurement—axle separation distance—critically affects turn angle calibration. Measure this dimension three ways for cross-validation: direct caliper measurement between axle centerlines, calculated from the liftarm hole spacing (multiply hole count by 8mm per stud), and empirical measurement from the four-90-degree-turn test where cumulative error reveals calibration mistakes. If the three methods disagree by more than 1mm, inspect for axle misalignment or asymmetric wheel mounting that introduces effective wheelbase variation during motion.

### Motor Performance Baselines

Maintain a performance log for each motor in your inventory, uniquely identified by marking the motor case with a permanent marker or label. Record no-load speed (motor commanded to 100% power spinning freely, measured by tachometer), stall torque (maximum load before the motor stops rotating, measured by lever arm test), and thermal time constant (minutes from room temperature to thermal equilibrium at 50% power). Motors showing degraded performance—no-load speed reduced by >10%, unusual vibration or noise, or premature thermal shutdown—should be disassembled for inspection. Remove the four screws on the rear case, extract the gear train, and check for grit or debris in the plastic gears. Clean with isopropyl alcohol and a soft brush; do not lubricate with oil or grease as this attracts dust and accelerates wear.

The motor encoder provides 360 counts per output shaft revolution (one count per degree of motor rotation) with typical accuracy of ±2 degrees over a full rotation. Validate encoder function by commanding a precisely 3600-degree rotation (10 full turns) and verifying with a mechanical counter or mark on the output shaft. Encoder failures manifest as position drift, motor oscillation when holding position, or complete loss of closed-loop control. These typically result from cable damage rather than sensor failure; inspect the encoder cable for wire breaks at the connector crimp or along the cable length where it flexes during motion.

### Gear Train Inspection Protocol

A properly assembled gear train exhibits smooth rotation with no binding, minimal backlash (0.5-1.0mm of free play measured at the output shaft, depending on reduction ratio), and quiet operation. Audible clicking or grinding indicates mesh interference from misaligned gears or debris in the teeth. To diagnose, rotate the output shaft slowly by hand through multiple revolutions, feeling for tight spots in the rotation. A rhythmic variation in resistance repeating once per gear rotation indicates an out-of-round or damaged gear; variation repeating once per multiple gear rotations suggests misalignment of the gear pair.

Measure backlash by fixing the input shaft and measuring the angular free play at the output shaft before the gear train engages. Convert this to linear displacement at your wheel radius to predict dead-band in motion control. Total system backlash is the sum of backlash in each gear stage; a two-stage reduction (motor to intermediate gear, intermediate gear to wheel) accumulates backlash from both stages. Excessive backlash (>3mm at the wheel for a 3:1 reduction) indicates worn gear teeth or excessive clearance in pin-to-hole joints. Replace worn gears and ensure pins fit snugly in holes; a loose pin can be shimmed with a thin layer of tape or replaced with a tighter-fitting pin from your inventory.

### Electrical System Diagnostics

The power delivery chain from battery through hub to motors introduces multiple potential failure points. Measure voltage at each stage under load: at battery terminals, at hub input, and at motor terminals during operation. Voltage drops exceeding 0.3V at any interface indicate resistive losses requiring attention. Battery terminal corrosion should be cleaned with fine sandpaper or a pencil eraser; hub port contacts can be cleaned with isopropyl alcohol on a cotton swab. Motor cable resistance can be measured with a multimeter set to low-ohm range; a healthy cable shows < 0.5 ohms total resistance for power conductors.

Current draw provides diagnostic information about mechanical load. Establish baseline current measurements for each standard operation: motor running freely (50-100mA), robot driving on smooth floor at cruise speed (200-400mA per motor), and robot climbing a 15-degree ramp (500-800mA per motor). Current draw consistently exceeding these baselines indicates increased friction from misalignment, inadequate gear mesh clearance, or debris. Current draw below baseline, especially accompanied by reduced performance, suggests motor winding damage or poor electrical contact in the power path.

### Software Verification and Calibration

Before attributing failures to mechanical issues, verify software operation systematically. Implement a startup self-test routine that exercises each motor independently, confirms encoder response by commanding a small rotation and verifying position change, and checks sensor connectivity by reading values and validating they fall within expected ranges. Display results on the hub's LED matrix or screen; a traffic-light protocol (green = all systems nominal, yellow = degraded performance, red = critical failure) provides immediate status feedback.

Calibration constants embedded in your control software require periodic validation against physical measurements. The wheel diameter, wheelbase, and gear ratio constants directly affect odometry accuracy. After any mechanical modification or repair, execute the calibration validation sequence: measure straight-line accuracy over 1 meter (should be within ±2%), measure 90-degree turn accuracy (within ±3 degrees), and execute a 1-meter square path returning to start (final position within ±5cm of origin). Failure to meet these tolerances indicates either miscalibration constants or mechanical assembly errors.

### Preventive Maintenance Schedule

Establish a maintenance cycle based on operating hours rather than calendar time, as wear mechanisms are use-dependent. After every 10 hours of operation or one competition event, inspect all pin connections for looseness (manually attempt to flex joints that should be rigid), check gear mesh for tooth damage (look for white stress marks or chipped tooth corners), and verify wheel-to-axle connection security (attempt to slide wheels along axles). Clean the wheel treads with a damp cloth to remove dust and debris that reduces traction. Remove hair or thread tangled around axles, a common failure mode that increases bearing friction and can stall motors.

After 50 hours of operation, perform a complete teardown inspection. Disassemble all gear trains and clean components with isopropyl alcohol. Inspect liftarm pin holes for elongation using a pin gauge or by comparing hole diameter to an unused reference liftarm; elongation exceeding 0.2mm indicates the part should be retired from high-stress applications. Check motor output shafts for burrs or damage that would interfere with gear mounting. Reassemble using a configuration photograph as reference to ensure correct alignment and orientation.

### Field Troubleshooting Decision Trees

When robot behavior deviates from expected performance during operation, execute rapid diagnosis using these prioritized checks. For motion control failures (robot not moving or moving incorrectly): verify battery charge status (>7.0V under load), confirm motor cables fully seated in hub ports, test motors individually via manual control, inspect wheels for obstruction or axle decoupling, and check gear train for jamming. This sequence progresses from quick electrical checks to mechanical inspection requiring disassembly.

For intermittent failures (behavior varies between identical trials): measure battery voltage variation over multiple runs to identify power supply instability, inspect cable strain reliefs for wire fatigue breaks that create intermittent connections, check for loose structural connections that allow components to shift during motion, and verify adequate program memory and execution time margins in your control software. Intermittent failures are the most challenging to diagnose; data logging of sensor values, motor commands, and battery voltage during failed runs provides essential diagnostic information.

For performance degradation (robot still functions but slower or weaker than baseline): measure current draw and compare to baselines to identify increased mechanical losses, inspect for debris accumulation in gear trains and wheel bearings, verify tire inflation and tread condition (worn smooth tires reduce traction significantly), and confirm motor thermal shutdown is not occurring by measuring case temperature during operation. Gradual performance loss typically indicates wear accumulation; sudden degradation suggests a discrete component failure.
