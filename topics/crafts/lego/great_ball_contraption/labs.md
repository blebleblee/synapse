# Operational Verification Labs: Great Ball Contraption

## Objective & Operational Lab Setup

The Great Ball Contraption (GBC) represents one of the most demanding integration exercises in Lego mechanical systems design, requiring precise coordination of timing, torque management, volumetric throughput, and fail-safe error handling across modular transfer stations. Unlike static display models or single-function mechanisms, a GBC module must operate continuously under variable ball supply rates, maintain synchronization with adjacent modules through standardized input/output interfaces, and recover gracefully from jam conditions without human intervention. This laboratory sequence trains practitioners to design, build, verify, and diagnose GBC modules through empirical measurement of cycle time, ball throughput rate, mechanical efficiency, and failure mode analysis.

The GBC standard specifies a ball transfer rate of one standard Lego soccer ball (approximately 40mm diameter, 2.7 grams mass) every one to two seconds, with modules accepting balls from a predecessor at a standardized height of 7-9 Lego bricks (approximately 22-29 cm from baseplate) and delivering to a successor at the same elevation range. Each module must operate on a single Power Functions motor (medium, large, or XL) or equivalent motorization, drawing from a shared 9V power rail, and must incorporate physical buffers to accommodate momentary supply/demand mismatches between adjacent modules without cascading failure. The laboratory environment requires a minimum 32x32 stud baseplate, a calibrated digital scale accurate to 0.1 grams for ball mass verification, a stopwatch or smartphone timer application capable of lap timing to 0.01 seconds, and a servo tester or Power Functions battery box with voltage display to monitor motor supply conditions.

Before commencing module construction, practitioners must inventory ball stock and verify uniformity. Lego soccer balls exhibit manufacturing tolerance variations in wall thickness and overall mass; a batch of twenty balls should be weighed individually, and any outlier exceeding ±0.3 grams from the mean should be segregated for separate testing. Ball surface friction also varies with age and contamination; balls should be wiped with isopropyl alcohol and allowed to dry to establish baseline rolling resistance. Baseplate levelness must be verified using a digital inclinometer or smartphone accelerometer application, ensuring no more than 0.5 degrees of tilt in any axis, as gravitational bias will introduce systematic errors in throughput measurement and cause preferential jamming on inclined ramps.

Motor characterization precedes module design. Connect the selected Power Functions motor to a battery box and measure no-load rotational velocity using a tachometer or by timing ten complete revolutions of a flag attached to the output shaft. Record supply voltage simultaneously using a multimeter across the battery terminals. Load the motor output shaft with a torque arm (a 10-stud beam perpendicular to the shaft with a hanging mass at the far end) and measure the stall torque by incrementally adding mass until rotation ceases. Plot the torque-speed curve by measuring velocity at 25%, 50%, and 75% of stall torque. This empirical motor map informs gear reduction ratio selection and allows prediction of cycle time under varying ball loads. For a typical Power Functions Large motor at 9V, expect approximately 120 RPM no-load speed and 40 N·cm stall torque, with linear decline between these endpoints.

## Exercise 1: Foundational Practical Implementation

Construct a baseline single-station GBC module implementing a direct mechanical lift-and-dump mechanism, the simplest reliable architecture for continuous ball transfer. Begin with a 16x16 stud footprint on the calibrated baseplate. Mount a Power Functions Large motor vertically in the center using a 5x7 Technic frame, with the output shaft pointing upward. Attach a 24-tooth gear directly to the motor shaft. Mesh this with a 40-tooth gear on a vertical axle offset by 4 studs, achieving a 1.67:1 reduction ratio that delivers approximately 72 RPM at the lifting mechanism under no load.

The lifting mechanism employs a four-bar linkage constructed from 11-stud and 7-stud liftarms arranged in a parallelogram configuration. Pin the 11-stud liftarm base to the 40-tooth gear using a black friction pin at the 3-hole position from one end, establishing the crank arm. Connect the opposite end of this liftarm to a 7-stud liftarm using a gray friction pin, forming the coupler. Pin the far end of the 7-stud liftarm to a fixed anchor point on the vertical frame at 9 bricks height, establishing the rocker arm. Attach a small bucket assembly (a 4x6 plate with 1x2 plate sides, capable of holding three balls) to the midpoint of the coupler arm using a perpendicular 3-stud connector pin. As the motor drives the 40-tooth gear through one complete revolution, the four-bar linkage translates the bucket through an elliptical path, dipping to ground level to collect balls from the input hopper and rising to 9 bricks height to dump into the output chute.

Construct the input hopper from a 6x8 plate base with 2x8 brick walls on three sides, leaving the front open. Position this hopper directly beneath the bucket's lowest travel point, ensuring the bucket base enters the hopper volume by at least 2 studs depth at the bottom of the rotation cycle. The hopper floor should slope gently toward the front at a 15-degree angle (achieved by placing a 1-plate shim under the rear edge) to gravitationally feed balls forward into the pickup zone. Construct the output chute from two 2x10 plates spaced 5 studs apart (exactly one ball diameter) and angled downward at 20 degrees, beginning at the bucket's highest dump point and extending 16 studs forward to the module's output edge. Line the chute with smooth tiles to minimize rolling friction.

With the mechanism assembled, perform the first verification cycle. Load five balls into the input hopper and activate the motor at full 9V supply. Observe three complete lifting cycles without intervening. The bucket should reliably capture one ball per cycle at the bottom of its travel and successfully release that ball at the top, with the ball rolling smoothly down the output chute without bouncing out. Measure the cycle time by timing ten consecutive lifts and dividing by ten; record this value. For the specified gear ratio and motor, expect approximately 0.83 seconds per cycle under single-ball load (72 cycles per minute). If the bucket fails to capture balls consistently, the hopper angle is insufficient or the bucket dwell time at the bottom position is too brief. If balls escape the output chute, reduce the chute angle or increase the sidewall height.

Now conduct a sustained throughput test. Load the input hopper with twenty balls and run the module continuously until the hopper empties. Use a stopwatch to measure elapsed time from motor start to the moment the final ball exits the output chute. Count the number of balls successfully transferred to the output and note any balls remaining in the hopper or lost to the mechanism. Calculate the effective throughput rate by dividing the number of successfully transferred balls by the elapsed time in seconds, yielding balls per second. For this baseline mechanism, expect approximately 1.1-1.3 balls per second (66-78 balls per minute), exceeding the GBC standard minimum by a comfortable margin.

Evaluate mechanical efficiency by measuring input power and calculating useful work output. Use a multimeter to measure current draw from the battery box during loaded operation; for the Large motor under single-ball cyclic load, expect approximately 0.15-0.20 amperes at 9V, representing 1.35-1.80 watts input power. Calculate the gravitational potential energy imparted to each ball: lifting 2.7 grams through 22 cm height requires $E = mgh = 0.0027 \text{ kg} \times 9.81 \text{ m/s}^2 \times 0.22 \text{ m} = 0.0058 \text{ J}$ per ball. At 1.2 balls per second average throughput, useful power output is $0.0058 \text{ J} \times 1.2 \text{ Hz} = 0.007 \text{ W}$, yielding an overall system efficiency of approximately 0.4-0.5%. This low efficiency is characteristic of Lego mechanisms due to substantial frictional losses in plastic pin joints, axle bearings, and gear mesh interfaces, but remains acceptable given the non-critical nature of the application and the design priority for reliability over optimization.

## Exercise 2: Parameter Variation & Empirical Analysis

With the baseline module characterized, systematically vary key parameters to map the operational envelope and identify optimization opportunities. The three most influential variables in lift-and-dump GBC architecture are gear reduction ratio, bucket dwell angle, and hopper feed geometry. This exercise quantifies the impact of each variable through controlled experimentation and establishes empirical correction factors for predictive design.

Begin with gear ratio variation. The baseline 24:40 reduction (1.67:1) can be compared against a 12:20 configuration (1.67:1 maintained) using smaller gears to reduce rotational inertia, an 8:40 configuration (5:1) to increase torque and reduce speed, and a 24:24 configuration (1:1) to maximize speed at minimum torque. Reconstruct the lifting mechanism with each gear combination in turn, maintaining all other geometric parameters constant. For each configuration, perform a twenty-ball throughput test and record cycle time, throughput rate, and any observed failure modes. Construct a data table with columns for gear ratio, theoretical output RPM (calculated from motor characterization), measured cycle time, measured throughput rate, and efficiency notes.

Expected results demonstrate a nonlinear relationship between gear ratio and throughput. The 5:1 reduction produces excessively slow cycles (approximately 2.5 seconds each), falling below the GBC standard despite improved torque margin and smoother motion. The 1:1 direct drive attempts faster cycles (approximately 0.5 seconds each) but exhibits higher ball drop rates as the bucket does not dwell long enough at the pickup position for reliable capture. The baseline 1.67:1 ratio represents a practical compromise, though specific optimal ratios vary with motor type and ball mass distribution. Plot throughput rate versus gear ratio on a graph to visualize the performance curve and identify the peak throughput operating point for your specific component set.

Next, investigate bucket dwell angle by varying the four-bar linkage geometry. Dwell refers to the angular range of crank rotation during which the bucket remains near the pickup or dump positions, effectively the "flat" portions of the elliptical travel path. Increasing dwell improves reliability but reduces throughput by consuming more cycle time at stationary positions. Modify the baseline linkage by changing the crank arm attachment point on the 40-tooth gear: test configurations at the 1-hole, 3-hole, 5-hole, and 7-hole positions from the center. Maintain the coupler and rocker arm lengths constant. The 1-hole configuration produces minimal dwell with a nearly circular path, while the 7-hole configuration extends dwell significantly but also increases the required torque due to longer lever arm distance from the rotation center.

Measure ball capture reliability for each dwell configuration by performing a one-hundred-ball continuous transfer test and counting the number of missed captures (balls remaining in the hopper after the bucket passes). Calculate the capture reliability as the percentage of successful pickups. Simultaneously measure cycle time to quantify the throughput cost of increased dwell. Plot capture reliability and throughput rate on a dual-axis graph against crank arm hole position. The optimal configuration typically appears at the 3-hole or 4-hole position, balancing 95%+ capture reliability with throughput rates near 1.0 balls per second. Configurations beyond the 5-hole position often encounter torque limitations, causing the motor to stall or skip teeth under load, a catastrophic failure mode requiring immediate shutdown.

Finally, examine hopper feed geometry by constructing three alternative hopper designs while maintaining the baseline lifting mechanism. The first alternative employs a narrower 4x8 footprint with steeper 25-degree floor slope, concentrating balls into a tighter pickup zone but risking jamming if multiple balls attempt simultaneous entry. The second uses a wider 8x8 footprint with a shallower 10-degree slope and a V-shaped floor profile that funnels balls toward the center, reducing jamming risk but potentially allowing balls to escape the capture zone if the bucket deviates from the centerline. The third incorporates an active agitator: a secondary 8-tooth gear meshed with the main 40-tooth drive gear, with short axle stub extensions that gently disturb balls in the hopper, preventing bridging and ensuring continuous flow to the pickup position.

Test each hopper variant with a fifty-ball continuous run, measuring throughput rate, capture reliability, and observing failure modes. The narrow hopper typically achieves the highest capture rate when functioning correctly (98%+) but exhibits occasional multi-ball jams that require manual intervention, disqualifying it from GBC standard compliance. The wide hopper reduces jamming to near-zero occurrence but shows slightly reduced capture reliability (92-94%) due to geometric tolerance in ball positioning. The agitator hopper combines high capture reliability (96-98%) with zero jamming, but introduces additional mechanical complexity and a slight throughput penalty (approximately 0.9 balls per second) due to the agitator's interference with bucket motion. Select the hopper design that best matches your operational priorities: exhibition modules prioritize reliability and zero-intervention operation, favoring the agitator design, while speed-record attempts may accept occasional manual intervention for maximum throughput using the narrow hopper.

Compile all parameter variation data into a comprehensive operational matrix documenting the tested combinations of gear ratio, bucket dwell configuration, and hopper geometry. This matrix serves as a design reference for future modules and allows rapid prediction of expected performance for new parameter combinations through interpolation. Calculate the sensitivity coefficient for each parameter by determining the percentage change in throughput rate per unit change in the parameter value; this quantifies which variables offer the greatest optimization leverage. For most practitioners, gear ratio exhibits the highest sensitivity (±30% throughput per ratio doubling), followed by dwell geometry (±20% per hole position change), and hopper configuration (±10% between variants), though exact values depend on component quality and assembly precision.

## Exercise 3: Stress Testing, Boundary Failure & Recovery Protocol

GBC modules must operate reliably under degraded conditions: voltage sag from aging batteries, ball supply interruptions from upstream module failures, and mechanical wear accumulation over extended runtime. This exercise deliberately induces failure conditions to characterize breakdown modes, establish operational boundaries, and verify recovery capability, preparing practitioners to diagnose and resolve field failures in multi-module exhibition layouts.

Begin with voltage stress testing. The baseline module operates at 9V nominal supply from a fresh Power Functions battery box (six AA alkaline cells). Discharge the battery pack by running the motor continuously under moderate load (a 24:40 gear reduction driving a flywheel mass) until the terminal voltage drops to 8.0V as measured by multimeter, simulating a partially depleted battery condition typical after two hours of exhibition operation. Reinstall the depleted battery in the GBC module and perform a twenty-ball throughput test, recording cycle time, throughput rate, and any performance degradation. Repeat this procedure at 7.0V and 6.0V, approaching the Power Functions motor's minimum operating threshold.

Expected results show graceful performance degradation rather than abrupt failure. At 8.0V, cycle time increases approximately 10-15% (to 0.95-0.98 seconds per cycle) due to reduced motor speed, but capture reliability remains high and throughput stays within GBC specification. At 7.0V, cycle time extends to 1.1-1.3 seconds per cycle, marginally failing the GBC standard, and you may observe occasional missed captures as the bucket's reduced speed causes inadequate dwell at the pickup position. At 6.0V, the motor approaches stall conditions under load; the mechanism may complete cycles intermittently or fail entirely. Plot throughput rate versus supply voltage and identify the minimum operating voltage for reliable continuous operation; for most Power Functions Large motors, this threshold lies between 7.2V and 7.5V. During exhibition planning, battery replacement should be scheduled when voltage drops below 7.5V to maintain specification compliance across all modules in the contraption.

Next, conduct mechanical wear simulation. GBC modules at major exhibitions may operate for 20-40 hours continuously over a weekend event, accumulating millions of pin articulation cycles and gear mesh engagements. Accelerate wear by running the baseline module continuously for four hours under full ball load, replenishing the input hopper as it empties. Every thirty minutes, pause operation and perform a rapid diagnostic inspection: check all friction pins for loosening (apply manual test force; any pin that moves indicates wear and requires replacement), inspect gear teeth for visible damage or debris accumulation, verify axle alignment by observing wobble in rotating elements, and measure bucket position repeatability by timing ten cycles and calculating standard deviation of cycle time.

After four hours of continuous operation, repeat the twenty-ball throughput characterization test and compare results against the fresh-build baseline. Typical wear degradation manifests as 5-10% increase in cycle time variation (standard deviation rises from ±0.02 seconds to ±0.05-0.08 seconds), occasional gear tooth skipping under peak load (audible as a clicking sound during bucket loading), and increased friction in pin joints causing visible hesitation in linkage motion. These symptoms indicate the module has reached maintenance threshold. Disassemble the lifting mechanism completely, inspect all wear surfaces under magnification (a jeweler's loupe or smartphone macro lens reveals stress whitening in pin shanks and gear tooth polishing), replace any damaged components, and reassemble with fresh lubrication (a single drop of light machine oil on each axle bearing point, avoiding excess that attracts dust). Post-maintenance throughput testing should restore performance to within 2-3% of original baseline.

Introduce deliberate jam conditions to verify fault recovery capability. While the module operates, manually insert an oversized object (a Lego minifigure or a 2x4 brick) into the bucket during its upward travel, simulating foreign object intrusion or a malformed ball. Observe the system response: the motor should stall momentarily as torque demand exceeds available capacity, but the four-bar linkage geometry should not permit the bucket to deform or the structure to collapse. Remove the obstruction and verify that the motor resumes normal operation without requiring power cycling. If the motor fails to restart automatically, the gear reduction ratio is insufficient for the torque margin required; increase reduction to at least 2:1 for robust jam recovery.

Test ball supply interruption handling by running the module with an empty input hopper. The bucket should continue cycling through its full range of motion without jamming or exhibiting mechanical instability, ready to resume ball transfer immediately when supply resumes. Some practitioners incorporate photoelectric ball-detection sensors at the hopper input to automatically pause motor operation during supply interruptions, reducing wear and energy consumption, but this adds electronic complexity beyond basic GBC requirements. For purist mechanical-only implementations, design the four-bar linkage with sufficient clearance that the empty bucket never collides with hopper walls during normal cycling.

Finally, perform a multi-hour endurance validation. Set up a closed-loop test circuit by positioning the module's output chute to dump balls back into its own input hopper, creating a self-feeding continuous cycle. Load twenty balls, start the motor, and allow the system to run unattended for twelve hours (overnight operation). Monitor the system remotely using a webcam or smartphone time-lapse recording to capture any failures without requiring continuous human supervision. After twelve hours, inspect the module for accumulated wear, count the number of balls still in circulation (losses indicate balls escaping the loop), and verify throughput rate has not degraded below GBC specification threshold. A module that completes this endurance test without intervention is considered exhibition-ready and suitable for integration into multi-module GBC layouts.

Document all observed failure modes in a diagnostic decision tree format: symptom observed (reduced throughput, increased noise, erratic cycle timing, complete stoppage) leads to diagnostic test procedure (voltage measurement, pin tightness check, gear tooth inspection, ball mass verification) leads to corrective action (battery replacement, pin replacement, gear cleaning, ball replacement). This diagnostic tree becomes an operational troubleshooting guide for rapid field repair during exhibitions when detailed analysis is impractical.

## Operational Reference Guide & Diagnostic Checklist

This reference consolidates critical design parameters, performance benchmarks, and troubleshooting protocols into a rapid-access format for operational use during module construction and exhibition deployment. Practitioners should verify their implementations against these standards before declaring a module GBC-compliant and integrating it into collaborative layouts.

**Standard GBC Interface Specifications:**
Ball transfer rate 1.0 to 2.0 balls per second (60 to 120 balls per minute). Input acceptance height 7 to 9 bricks (22 to 29 cm) above baseplate. Output delivery height 7 to 9 bricks (22 to 29 cm) above baseplate. Ball specification standard Lego soccer ball, 40 mm diameter, 2.5 to 3.0 grams mass. Module footprint maximum 32 studs in any dimension. Power supply single Power Functions motor per module, 9V nominal. Buffer capacity minimum 3 balls internal storage to accommodate supply/demand transients.

**Baseline Lift-and-Dump Performance Benchmarks:**
Gear reduction ratio 1.5:1 to 2.0:1 for Large motor. Cycle time 0.8 to 1.0 seconds per lift under single-ball load. Throughput rate 1.0 to 1.3 balls per second sustained. Capture reliability greater than 95 percent over 100-ball test. Minimum operating voltage 7.5V for specification compliance. Endurance capability 12 hours continuous operation without intervention. Mechanical efficiency 0.4 to 0.6 percent (gravitational work output to electrical power input).

**Pre-Operation Checklist:**
Verify baseplate level within 0.5 degrees all axes using inclinometer. Confirm ball inventory uniformity mass within ±0.3 grams of mean across batch. Measure supply voltage at motor terminals minimum 7.5V under load. Inspect all friction pins for secure seating no visible play in joints. Check gear mesh engagement full tooth depth no skipping under manual rotation test. Verify bucket travel path clear no collision with hopper walls or structural members throughout full rotation. Test empty-bucket cycling motor starts and stops smoothly without stalling. Confirm output chute ball retention no escape during rolling at specified angle.

**Operational Monitoring Parameters:**
Record cycle time every 30 minutes to detect wear-induced degradation. Monitor supply voltage hourly replace battery below 7.5V threshold. Observe gear mesh acoustics clicking sounds indicate tooth damage or insufficient lubrication. Check ball accumulation in hopper jamming symptoms require immediate shutdown and clearance. Verify throughput rate every 2 hours sustained rates below 0.9 balls per second indicate degraded performance requiring maintenance. Inspect structural integrity before each exhibition day session confirm no loosened connections or beam deflection.

**Common Failure Modes and Corrective Actions:**
Symptom reduced throughput rate cycle time increasing. Diagnostic check supply voltage, measure under load. If voltage adequate, inspect for friction pin loosening or gear debris accumulation. Corrective action replace battery if voltage low, replace worn pins, clean and re-lubricate gears. Symptom intermittent ball capture failures bucket misses balls in hopper. Diagnostic verify hopper alignment relative to bucket lowest position, check for ball mass outliers. Corrective action adjust hopper position forward or backward by 1 stud increments, segregate overweight or underweight balls. Symptom gear tooth skipping audible clicking during loaded operation. Diagnostic inspect gear teeth under magnification for chipping or wear, verify mesh depth. Corrective action replace damaged gears, increase gear reduction ratio to reduce torque demand. Symptom motor stall mechanism stops mid-cycle. Diagnostic measure current draw at stall approximately 2A indicates electrical stall, lower current indicates mechanical binding. Corrective action if electrical increase gear reduction ratio, if mechanical identify and remove binding source in linkage geometry.

**Advanced Optimization Opportunities:**
Implement ball buffering by adding a vertical magazine between input and pickup position, holding 5-8 balls in a gravity-fed stack. This decouples upstream supply rate from mechanism cycle time, allowing the module to operate at peak efficiency regardless of predecessor module performance variations. Magazines introduce risk of ball jamming in the vertical column; diameter must be precisely 5 studs (40mm ball plus 0.5mm clearance margin on each side) and walls must be smooth tiles to minimize friction.

Incorporate variable-speed control using a Power Functions IR receiver and speed regulator, allowing real-time throughput adjustment to match adjacent modules without physical reconstruction. This requires electronic components beyond basic GBC mechanical requirements but provides operational flexibility valuable in large layouts with diverse module types. Program the IR remote with preset speed levels corresponding to 0.8, 1.0, 1.2, and 1.5 balls per second throughput rates, verified by pre-calibration testing. During exhibition operation, adjust speed downward if balls accumulate in the input hopper (upstream supply exceeds capacity) or upward if the hopper empties frequently (downstream demand exceeds delivery).

Design modular input/output interfaces using standardized ramp geometries that allow tool-free connection and disconnection of adjacent modules. A common approach employs 2x10 ramp plates with 1x2 tile edges, spanning exactly 16 studs between module boundaries, with the final 4 studs of each module's output overlapping the first 4 studs of the successor's input. This overlap creates a continuous ball path while permitting individual module removal for maintenance without disrupting neighboring stations. Mark interface connection points with colored bricks to ensure correct alignment during layout assembly.

The culmination of these laboratory exercises transforms the practitioner from a builder of isolated mechanisms into a systems integrator capable of designing robust, specification-compliant GBC modules suitable for collaborative exhibition environments. The empirical data gathered through parameter variation establishes design intuition that cannot be acquired through simulation or theoretical analysis alone. The stress testing and failure mode characterization provide operational confidence that the module will perform reliably under real-world degraded conditions rather than only in ideal laboratory circumstances. Armed with this hands-on verification experience, practitioners can proceed to more complex GBC architectures—spiral lifts, conveyor belts, ferris wheels, robotic arms—confident in their ability to predict performance, diagnose failures, and optimize efficiency through systematic empirical iteration.
