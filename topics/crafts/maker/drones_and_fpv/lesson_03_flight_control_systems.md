# Lesson 3: Flight Control Systems, PID Tuning, Stabilization Algorithms

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The heart of every quadcopter, from a 65mm Tiny Whoop to a 7-inch long-range rig, is a closed-loop control system that transforms pilot stick commands and sensor measurements into motor output signals fast enough to counteract aerodynamic disturbances before the airframe tumbles out of control. Unlike fixed-wing aircraft, which possess inherent aerodynamic stability from swept wings and tail surfaces, multirotor platforms are fundamentally unstable in open-loop operation. The slightest gust, propeller thrust imbalance, or motor response asymmetry will induce uncontrolled roll, pitch, or yaw divergence within tens of milliseconds. The flight controller—a microcontroller running firmware like Betaflight, INAV, or ArduCopter—must therefore sample inertial sensors at kilohertz rates, compute corrective motor commands through proportional-integral-derivative feedback loops, and transmit pulse-width-modulated (PWM) or digital shot (DShot) signals to electronic speed controllers (ESCs) that modulate brushless motor RPM. This entire sensor-compute-actuate cycle, known as the control loop frequency, typically operates at 4 kHz to 8 kHz for the gyroscope PID controller and 500 Hz to 1 kHz for the lower-bandwidth accelerometer and barometer fusion stages.

The gyroscope measures angular velocity in degrees per second along three orthogonal axes (roll, pitch, yaw), providing the instantaneous rate of rotation. The accelerometer measures linear acceleration including gravitational force, yielding attitude reference over longer timescales but suffering from vibration noise and inability to distinguish centripetal acceleration from gravity during rapid maneuvers. The barometer measures atmospheric pressure to infer altitude, though subject to thermal drift and pressure waves from propeller downwash. Sensor fusion algorithms, most commonly a complementary filter or Kalman filter, merge these modalities: the gyroscope provides high-frequency, low-drift rate information, while the accelerometer corrects gyroscope integration error over seconds to prevent attitude drift, and the barometer stabilizes altitude hold when GPS is unavailable or unreliable indoors. The PID controller then compares the desired setpoint—whether a target angular rate commanded by the pilot in rate mode or a target angle in self-leveling angle mode—against the current state estimate, computes an error term, and applies three correction components: proportional gain scales directly with instantaneous error, integral gain accumulates error over time to eliminate steady-state offset, and derivative gain responds to the rate of error change to dampen oscillations and overshoot.

The fundamental invariant of multirotor flight control is that motor thrust modulation must counteract disturbance torques within the mechanical bandwidth of the airframe, typically 10 to 30 Hz for a 5-inch quadcopter. If the control loop frequency is too low, the controller cannot respond to high-frequency disturbances like propwash turbulence or propeller resonance, leading to oscillations visible as mid-throttle bouncing or rapid yaw washout in hard turns. If PID gains are too high, the controller over-corrects, injecting energy into the airframe's natural resonant modes and causing self-sustaining oscillations that burn motor bearings and overheat ESCs. If gains are too low, the quad becomes sluggish, unable to reject wind gusts or track aggressive stick inputs. The tuning process is therefore an exercise in finding the highest gains the airframe can tolerate without oscillation, maximizing disturbance rejection and stick responsiveness while maintaining stability across throttle ranges, airspeeds, and environmental conditions. Betaflight's blackbox logging system records gyroscope traces, PID setpoint and measurement signals, and motor outputs at full loop rate, enabling post-flight frequency-domain analysis to identify resonant peaks, propwash coupling, and inadequate damping.

The stabilization mode hierarchy begins with rate mode, also called acro or manual mode, where stick deflection commands angular velocity rather than angle. A centered stick commands zero rate, and the PID controller drives gyroscope measurement to match the commanded rate setpoint. This mode provides no self-leveling: release the sticks mid-flip and the quad maintains its current orientation, requiring continuous pilot correction to hold level flight. Angle mode, also called self-level or horizon mode, adds an outer control loop where stick deflection commands a target angle, and an additional PID controller computes the rate setpoint required to reach that angle. The gyroscope PID then drives motor outputs to achieve that rate. When sticks are centered, angle mode commands zero degrees on roll and pitch axes, causing the quad to return to level flight automatically. A third mode, horizon mode in Betaflight terminology, blends rate and angle behavior: small stick deflections behave like angle mode for stability, while large deflections transition to rate mode for aerobatics, with the blend ratio adjustable via the angle_strength parameter. For altitude hold and GPS position hold, additional outer loops run at lower frequency (typically 50 to 100 Hz) and feed corrections into the angle or rate setpoint, creating a cascaded control hierarchy.

The physical meaning of each PID term illuminates tuning intuition. Proportional gain determines immediate responsiveness: doubling P-gain halves the time to reach the setpoint but also doubles the overshoot if the system has inertia. For a quadcopter's roll axis, the inertia comes from the airframe's moment of inertia, propeller gyroscopic precession, and motor acceleration lag. Integral gain eliminates steady-state error caused by asymmetric propeller thrust, motor timing skew, or persistent side wind, but accumulates error during rapid maneuvers, leading to I-term windup where the integrator saturates and causes overshoot when the maneuver ends. Betaflight's `iterm_relax` feature pauses integral accumulation during rapid setpoint changes to prevent this. Derivative gain acts as a brake, resisting rapid changes in gyroscope rate regardless of position error. In the frequency domain, D-gain attenuates high-frequency oscillations and provides phase lead to counteract the phase lag inherent in the motor and propeller system. However, derivative action amplifies sensor noise, requiring aggressive low-pass filtering on the gyroscope signal before D-term calculation. Betaflight defaults to a biquad low-pass filter at 100 to 150 Hz for the D-term input, trading some phase lag for noise rejection.

The relationship between motor thrust and angular acceleration is mediated by the mixer matrix, which converts roll, pitch, yaw, and throttle commands into individual motor outputs. For a standard quadcopter X-configuration, roll torque comes from differential thrust between left and right motor pairs, pitch from front and rear pairs, and yaw from the aerodynamic reaction torque of clockwise versus counterclockwise propellers. The mixer is a linear 4×4 matrix multiplication, but the actual system is nonlinear: thrust scales with the square of motor RPM, and propeller efficiency decreases at high advance ratios during forward flight. These nonlinearities, combined with propeller slipstream interactions, frame flex, and ESC response delays, mean that a single set of PID gains cannot achieve optimal performance across all flight regimes. Betaflight's dynamic notch filters track motor RPM via bidirectional DShot telemetry and place adaptive notch filters at integer multiples of blade-pass frequency to reject propeller-induced vibrations, allowing higher D-gain without amplifying noise at propeller resonance.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The signal flow from sensor to motor begins with the inertial measurement unit (IMU), typically a six-axis gyroscope-accelerometer combo chip like the MPU6000, ICM20602, or BMI270. These micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS) devices contain microscopic vibrating proof masses suspended by silicon cantilevers. When the chip rotates, Coriolis force deflects the proof mass, generating a capacitance change proportional to angular velocity. The accelerometer uses the same proof mass principle but measures static deflection from gravitational or linear acceleration. The gyroscope outputs three 16-bit signed integers representing roll, pitch, and yaw rates at a sample rate of 8 kHz, with full-scale ranges of ±2000 degrees per second. The accelerometer outputs three axes at ±16 g full-scale, sampled at 1 kHz. The flight controller reads these values over SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface) at 8 kHz, applies a calibration offset to remove bias (the gyroscope zero-point offset measured during pre-arm calibration), and scales the raw ADC counts into engineering units of degrees per second.

The gyroscope calibration procedure, initiated during power-on or via CLI command `calibrate`, computes the mean gyroscope reading over one to two seconds while the quad sits motionless. This mean becomes the offset subtracted from all subsequent readings. Thermal drift causes gyroscope bias to shift by 0.1 to 0.5 degrees per second per 10°C temperature change, so Betaflight's gyroscope calibration on first arm feature repeats calibration before each flight to minimize drift. If the quad is moved during calibration, the offset will be incorrect, causing slow uncommanded rotation in flight. The accelerometer calibration is more complex because it must account for the fact that at rest, one axis always reads +1 g or -1 g depending on orientation. A six-position calibration procedure places the quad in all six orthogonal orientations (top, bottom, left, right, front, back) and computes scale and offset to produce ±1 g on the appropriate axis. Poorly calibrated accelerometers cause drift in angle mode, where the quad slowly tilts in one direction even with centered sticks.

The raw gyroscope data passes through a filtering cascade before reaching the PID controller. Betaflight's default filter chain as of version 4.5 includes a first-stage low-pass filter at 500 Hz to remove aliasing artifacts, a dynamic notch filter bank that tracks motor RPM harmonics, and a second-stage low-pass filter at 150 to 200 Hz for the D-term path. Each biquad filter stage introduces 10 to 30 microseconds of processing delay and approximately 20 degrees of phase lag at the cutoff frequency. The total filter delay, combined with the ESC response delay (80 to 150 microseconds for DShot600 plus motor inductance lag), and propeller acceleration lag, creates a phase margin that limits the maximum stable D-gain. Reducing filter cutoff frequencies increases phase lag and reduces maximum D-gain but improves noise rejection, allowing smoother motor outputs and reduced electrical noise in the ESC and video transmission system. The tradeoff is tuned by analyzing blackbox logs: if motor outputs show high-frequency noise riding on top of the PID command, filters are too loose; if the gyroscope trace shows the quad lagging behind setpoint in fast maneuvers, filters are too tight.

The PID controller executes at the gyroscope sample rate of 4 kHz to 8 kHz, though some flight controllers run PID at half the gyroscope rate to reduce CPU load. Each axis—roll, pitch, yaw—has independent P, I, and D gains. The PID calculation proceeds as follows for each axis each iteration:

```text
error = setpoint_rate - gyro_rate_filtered
P_term = Kp * error
I_term += Ki * error * dt
I_term = constrain(I_term, -iterm_limit, iterm_limit)
D_term = Kd * (gyro_rate_filtered - gyro_rate_previous) / dt
output = P_term + I_term + D_term
output = constrain(output, -max_output, max_output)
motor_command = throttle + mixer_matrix * [roll_output, pitch_output, yaw_output]
```

The setpoint rate comes from the pilot's stick input scaled by the rate profile: a full stick deflection of 1.0 might command 670 degrees per second on the roll axis for an aggressive freestyle tune, or 200 degrees per second for a smooth cinematic rig. The derivative term uses rate of change of the gyroscope measurement rather than the error signal to avoid derivative kick, a sharp spike that occurs when setpoint changes instantaneously but measurement has not yet responded. The integral term is clamped to prevent windup during sustained errors, such as when the quad is held against a wall or flipped upside-down on the ground. The `iterm_relax` feature detects rapid setpoint changes by comparing current setpoint rate to average setpoint over the last 20 to 50 milliseconds; if the change exceeds a threshold, integral accumulation pauses to prevent I-term from winding up during flips and rolls.

The motor mixer takes the PID outputs for roll, pitch, and yaw, plus the throttle command, and distributes them across the four motors. For an X-frame quadcopter with motors numbered 1 (front-right), 2 (rear-right), 3 (rear-left), 4 (front-left), and clockwise propellers on 1 and 3, counterclockwise on 2 and 4, the mixer is:

```text
M1 = throttle + roll_output - pitch_output - yaw_output
M2 = throttle + roll_output + pitch_output + yaw_output
M3 = throttle - roll_output + pitch_output - yaw_output
M4 = throttle - roll_output - pitch_output + yaw_output
```

Each motor command is then constrained to the range [1000, 2000] microseconds (standard PWM pulse width) or [48, 2047] for DShot protocol, and transmitted to the ESC. If any motor command would exceed the maximum, the entire output vector is scaled down proportionally to prevent saturation while maintaining the requested torque direction. This saturation behavior causes loss of control authority at full throttle, since the PID controller can no longer increase motor differential to correct errors. Betaflight's throttle boost feature increases throttle automatically during aggressive maneuvers to keep average motor command below 80% of maximum, preserving correction headroom.

The angle mode outer loop runs at 500 Hz to 1 kHz and compares the desired angle (from stick input) to the current attitude estimate (from sensor fusion). The attitude estimate comes from a complementary filter that blends gyroscope integration with accelerometer tilt measurement. Gyroscope integration is performed by numerically integrating gyroscope rate to update the attitude quaternion or rotation matrix each loop iteration. Over time, gyroscope bias and integration error cause the attitude estimate to drift. The accelerometer measures the direction of the gravity vector relative to the airframe; assuming the quad is not accelerating linearly, the accelerometer directly indicates pitch and roll angles. The complementary filter computes:

```text
attitude_estimate = 0.98 * (attitude_previous + gyro_rate * dt) + 0.02 * accelerometer_angle
```

This time-constant weighting (98% gyroscope, 2% accelerometer) means the gyroscope dominates short-term response while the accelerometer corrects long-term drift. The Kalman filter, used in some flight stacks, is a more sophisticated sensor fusion that also estimates gyroscope bias and can incorporate GPS velocity to correct accelerometer errors during maneuvering flight. Once the attitude estimate is available, the angle mode controller computes the rate setpoint required to drive the attitude error to zero:

```text
angle_error = setpoint_angle - attitude_estimate
rate_setpoint = Kp_angle * angle_error
```

This rate setpoint feeds into the gyroscope PID controller as before. The angle mode P-gain determines how aggressively the quad snaps to the commanded angle; too high causes overshoot and bobbling, too low causes sluggish self-leveling. Angle mode has no integral or derivative terms in most implementations because the underlying rate controller already provides I and D action.

The barometer and GPS, when present, provide altitude and position outer loops at 10 to 50 Hz. The barometer measures air pressure via a piezoelectric or capacitive sensor and converts pressure to altitude using the barometric formula, assuming standard atmospheric lapse rate. Since pressure changes by approximately 12 Pa per meter at sea level, a barometer with 1 Pa resolution provides roughly 8 cm altitude precision, but thermal drift and propeller downwash create errors of several meters. Betaflight's altitude hold mode uses a PID controller to compute vertical velocity setpoint from altitude error, then feeds that into a velocity controller that adjusts throttle. GPS position hold computes desired velocity vector from position error, converts that to attitude setpoints, and drives the angle mode controller. These slow outer loops decouple timescales: the fast gyroscope PID stabilizes the airframe at 4 kHz, the intermediate angle controller maintains attitude at 1 kHz, and the slow navigation controller adjusts attitude setpoints at 50 Hz.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Tuning begins with a conservative baseline: Betaflight's default PID values for a 5-inch quad typically set roll and pitch P-gain to 45 to 50, I-gain to 80 to 90, and D-gain to 35 to 40, with yaw P at 70, I at 100, D at 0. This tune flies but lacks responsiveness and propwash handling. The first tuning pass increases D-gain on roll and pitch axes in increments of 5 to 10 points, test-flying after each increment. The goal is to reach the highest D-gain before oscillations appear. Oscillations manifest as high-frequency jitter (8 to 15 Hz typically) in the blackbox gyroscope trace, audible as a whine from the motors at mid-throttle, and visible as twitchy corrections in onboard video. When oscillations emerge, reduce D-gain by 10% and verify smooth flight. D-gain should be within 80% to 100% of the oscillation threshold for maximum performance.

Next, increase P-gain in increments of 5. P-gain oscillations are slower (3 to 8 Hz) and appear as wallowing or bobbing during throttle changes and hard turns, particularly in propwash (the turbulent air below the quad). The blackbox trace shows underdamped ringing after stick inputs: the gyroscope rate overshoots the setpoint, rings down over several oscillations, and settles. Push P-gain until this behavior appears, then reduce by 10%. The relationship between P and D is coupled: increasing D allows higher P by damping the oscillations P creates. If P cannot be raised without oscillations despite high D, the frame is likely too flexible, motors have excessive bearing play, or the ESC-motor response has excessive delay. Frame flex can be diagnosed by grasping the flight controller and attempting to twist it relative to the arms; any perceptible twist indicates inadequate stiffness. Carbon fiber thickness under 4 mm or arm span over 250 mm without cross-bracing typically requires reduced PID gains. Motor bearing play is checked by wiggling the motor bell; 0.2 mm of radial play is acceptable, more than 0.5 mm requires bearing replacement.

Integral gain is tuned last. Start at 1.5 to 2.0 times the P-gain value. If the quad drifts slowly during sustained flight or requires constant stick correction to maintain level, I-gain is too low. If the quad overshoots and oscillates slowly when exiting a flip or fast turn, I-term windup is occurring despite `iterm_relax`. Reducing I-gain or increasing the `iterm_relax` cutoff frequency resolves this. The blackbox I-term trace should accumulate smoothly during steady flight and decay to near zero within one second after a maneuver ends. Persistent I-term bias indicates motor or propeller asymmetry: unequal propeller pitch, motor timing misalignment, or bent motor shaft. Swapping motors between positions or replacing propellers may be required.

Dynamic notch filter configuration requires bidirectional DShot telemetry, where the ESC reports current motor eRPM (electrical RPM) back to the flight controller. The flight controller computes the propeller blade-pass frequency as `eRPM / 60 * poles / 2 * blades`, places notch filters at this frequency and its first harmonic, and tracks RPM in real-time. The dynamic notch filter typically has a Q-factor (filter sharpness) of 70 to 150, removing 20 to 40 dB at the notch center while preserving phase and amplitude on either side. If the gyroscope spectrum shows residual peaks at motor frequencies despite the dynamic notch, verify that ESC telemetry is functioning via `status` in Betaflight CLI; the `ESC` field should show `DSHOT`, and the `get rpm_filter` command should display current motor RPMs when the motors are spinning. Some ESCs have poor telemetry precision or add latency that causes the notch to lag the actual motor frequency; switching to a different ESC firmware or hardware may be necessary.

Filter tuning trades latency against noise. Reducing low-pass filter cutoff from 150 Hz to 100 Hz increases D-term phase lag by roughly 15 degrees at 50 Hz, reducing maximum D-gain by 15% to 20% but eliminating motor noise that causes mid-throttle oscillations and ESC overheating. The `gyro_lowpass_hz` and `dterm_lowpass_hz` parameters control these filters. For racing quads prioritizing responsiveness, filters may be pushed to 200 Hz or higher with aggressive dynamic notch filtering to handle the resulting motor noise. For cinematic rigs prioritizing smooth video, filters at 80 to 100 Hz and conservative D-gain produce the lowest propwash and smoothest stick feel at the cost of slower disturbance rejection. Blackbox analysis guides this choice: compute the frequency spectrum of the gyroscope signal via fast Fourier transform (FFT) in Betaflight Blackbox Explorer's spectrum analyzer. Peaks below 100 Hz indicate frame resonance or propwash; peaks above 200 Hz indicate motor noise or propeller imbalance. The filter cutoff should sit between the low-frequency dynamics you want to control and the high-frequency noise you want to reject.

Common failure modes and diagnostics include oscillation at hover but smooth at speed, indicating propwash sensitivity; reduce P-gain by 10% and increase D-gain by 10%. Oscillation at speed but smooth at hover suggests filter phase lag or motor overheating; reduce filter cutoffs or verify motor and ESC temperatures remain below 70°C during sustained flight. If the quad feels locked-in on one axis but floppy on another, PID gains are imbalanced; each axis should have similar P/D ratios even if absolute values differ due to airframe inertia. If the quad drifts in angle mode but is stable in rate mode, accelerometer calibration is incorrect; redo six-position calibration or check that the flight controller is mounted truly level. If altitude hold oscillates, the barometer is reading propeller pressure waves; move the barometer sensor away from the center of the frame or add foam shielding. If GPS position hold causes abrupt jerks, GPS dilution of precision (DOP) is poor or the position controller gains are too high; reduce navigation P-gain and verify GPS HDOP is below 2.0 before arming.

Blackbox logging is mandatory for advanced tuning. Set `debug_mode = GYRO_SCALED` to log raw gyroscope, PID setpoint, and motor outputs at full 8 kHz rate. Post-flight, load the log into Betaflight Blackbox Explorer, navigate to the PID traces for roll or pitch, and inspect the gyroscope response to setpoint steps. Ideal response shows the gyroscope tracking setpoint within 10 to 20 milliseconds with zero overshoot and no ringing. Overshoot indicates excessive P or insufficient D. Ringing indicates insufficient D or too much P. Lag between setpoint change and gyroscope response indicates filter delay, ESC lag, or motor inductance; reduce filter cutoffs if lag exceeds 30 milliseconds. The spectrum analyzer reveals resonance peaks: any peak above 100 Hz with amplitude greater than 50% of the fundamental indicates noise that should be filtered. Use the `resource` command in Betaflight CLI to verify motor mapping if one motor behaves inconsistently; incorrect motor order causes inverted or crossed control axes.

For freestyle and racing, stick rates and expo curves shape feel independent of PID tuning. Betaflight's rate profiles define the relationship between stick deflection and commanded angular rate. The `rates` command displays current settings: `roll_rate`, `pitch_rate`, and `yaw_rate` define the center sensitivity in degrees per second per unit stick deflection, while `roll_expo`, `pitch_expo`, and `yaw_expo` add a cubic term that reduces sensitivity near center stick and increases it near full deflection. Typical freestyle rates use center sensitivity of 200 to 300 deg/s with 40% to 60% expo, giving fine control for proximity flying while allowing 700+ deg/s flips with full stick. Racing rates use lower center sensitivity (100 to 150 deg/s) and minimal expo for predictable linear response. Yaw rate is usually 60% to 80% of roll rate to avoid overwhelming the aerodynamic yaw authority from propeller torque.

The transition from simulator to real hardware demands respect for safety protocols. Before first flight with new PID gains, verify propeller direction matches motor rotation (clockwise props on clockwise motors), arm without props to check motor spin-up is smooth and simultaneous, and use a smoke stopper (a current-limited power supply or automotive incandescent bulb in series with the battery lead) to detect short circuits during the first bench power-up. First test hover in a large open area at 20% to 30% throttle for five seconds, land, and check that motors and ESCs are not overheating. If the quad is unstable or oscillates, disarm immediately and reduce all PID gains by 30% before reattempting. Only after stable hover is confirmed should rolls, flips, and high-speed flight be attempted. Propeller guards or flying over soft grass minimize damage from inevitable crashes during tuning. The iterative tuning cycle—fly, log, analyze, adjust—converges within five to ten flights for most airframes, producing a quad that tracks stick commands with precision, rejects propwash with authority, and delivers the locked-in feel that distinguishes a well-tuned machine from a barely-controllable toy.
