# Lesson 2: Electrical Power Systems, Battery Chemistry, Motor Theory

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Every gram of thrust a quadcopter produces is borrowed against a finite electrochemical debt. Understanding the nature of that debt — how it accumulates, how quickly it can be repaid, and at what cost to system longevity — is the foundational literacy that separates pilots who tune by feel from engineers who tune by physics. Before a single prop spins, the electrical power system has already determined the ceiling of everything: top speed, hover endurance, motor temperature, and the exact millisecond the frame drops from the sky when the battery finally surrenders.

The modern FPV quadcopter runs on lithium polymer chemistry, and the reasons for its dominance are not arbitrary. LiPo cells occupy a unique position in the energy density landscape. A fully charged cell sits at 4.20 V and discharges through a relatively flat plateau — typically 3.7 V nominal — before collapsing steeply below 3.5 V under load. That plateau is the working range where flight happens. The steep cliff below it is where cells die permanently. The flatness of the discharge curve is a double-edged property: it means consistent motor performance across most of a pack's capacity, but it also means the pilot receives almost no warning before voltage collapse occurs. This is why pilots who ignore telemetry and fly by feel alone routinely puff or kill packs — the voltage held steady, then vanished.

The chemistry behind this behavior lies in the lithium cobalt oxide cathode paired with a graphite anode, separated by a polymer electrolyte film (hence "polymer"). During discharge, lithium ions migrate from anode to cathode through the electrolyte, while electrons travel through the external circuit — your ESC, motor, and camera — doing useful work. The rate at which ions can migrate is a physical limit that no amount of marketing can circumvent. Push current demand beyond the ion migration ceiling and the electrolyte heats, the internal resistance climbs, and voltage sag becomes severe. Push harder still and you get thermal runaway: the separator melts, cathode and anode make direct contact, and the exothermic reaction becomes self-sustaining. This is why a puffed LiPo — a pack whose electrolyte has begun decomposing and generating gas — must never be charged again. The gas is lithium fluoride and organic carbonates; the structural integrity of the separator is already compromised.

Understanding C-rating requires grounding it in real numbers rather than the inflated marketing figures that have plagued the industry for over a decade. The C-rating expresses the multiple of the pack's capacity at which it can be discharged continuously. A 1500 mAh pack rated at 100C is theoretically capable of delivering 150 A continuously — 0.1 Ah × 1000 mA/A × 100 = 150,000 mA = 150 A. In practice, virtually no consumer LiPo achieves its rated C figure; a pack advertised at 100C will typically sustain 60–75 A before its terminal voltage sags meaningfully. The more reliable diagnostic is internal resistance, measured in milliohms per cell. A fresh 4S 1500 mAh race pack from a reputable manufacturer (Tattu R-Line, Cnhl Black Series, GNB) will show 3–6 mΩ per cell. As the pack ages, internal resistance climbs, and that number is the single most honest indicator of a cell's remaining useful life. At 15–20 mΩ per cell, the pack is effectively retired for FPV use; it can no longer deliver high current without catastrophic voltage sag.

The relationship between voltage sag and motor performance is direct and mechanical. A brushless motor produces torque proportional to current, and RPM proportional to voltage — these are not independent variables. When voltage sags under load, the motor slows, the ESC demands more current to compensate, and the increased current accelerates the sag further. This positive feedback loop, if the pack is weak enough, causes a cascade: the flight controller detects the attitude divergence, increases motor demand, pulls more current, sags harder, loses more authority, and the craft tumbles. Experienced pilots learn to read the OSD voltage readout in conjunction with flight feel — a slight mushing in authority at half throttle, particularly in tight maneuvers, is the early signature of a pack approaching its current delivery limit.

The KV rating of a brushless motor is perhaps the most misunderstood number in the hobby. KV does not stand for kilovolts — it is a velocity constant expressing RPM per volt of applied voltage, measured at no load. A motor rated 2400KV spun on 4S (nominally 16.8 V at full charge) will spin at approximately 40,320 RPM unloaded. Load it with a propeller, and the actual RPM under power will be substantially lower — typically 10–20% lower depending on the aerodynamic torque demand of the specific prop. The practical consequence is that KV selection is inseparable from prop selection and cell count. High KV motors (2400–2700 KV) on 4S with 5-inch props produce peak RPM in an efficient efficiency band. The same high KV motor on 6S would over-speed the motor and prop beyond the structural and aerodynamic optimum, leading to prop strikes, motor failures, and efficiency losses from compressibility at the blade tips. Dropping to a lower KV (1700–1900 KV) on 6S re-centers the operating RPM into the target range while taking advantage of 6S's superior current delivery efficiency at a given power level.

The underlying physics connecting KV to motor construction is the back-EMF constant. As the rotor spins, the permanent magnets sweeping past the stator windings induce a voltage opposing the applied voltage — the back-EMF. At equilibrium, back-EMF equals applied voltage minus the resistive voltage drop across the winding resistance. More turns of thinner wire produce a stronger magnetic field per amp (higher torque per amp, lower KV); fewer turns of thicker wire produce weaker field per amp but allow higher RPM before back-EMF equilibrates with the supply (higher KV, lower torque per amp). This is the fundamental engineering trade-off baked into every motor's winding spec, and it explains why high KV motors run hotter at equivalent power levels — thinner wire has higher resistance, and thinner wire is exactly what more turns requires.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The electrical architecture of a quadcopter is a radial power distribution topology with hierarchical voltage domains. High current flows at battery voltage from the pack to the ESCs; regulated lower voltages branch off for the flight controller, video transmitter, camera, and receiver. Every connection in the high-current path is a potential point of resistance, heat, and failure.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    4S / 6S LiPo Pack                             │
│          (+) ─────────────────────── (-)                         │
└────────────────┬──────────────────────┬─────────────────────────┘
                 │  High-Current Bus     │
         ┌───────┴───────┐       ┌───────┴───────┐
         │   XT60 Plug   │       │  PDB / ESC    │
         │  (Connector)  │       │  Capacitor    │
         └───────┬───────┘       └───────┬───────┘
                 │                       │
   ┌─────────────┼───────────────────────┼─────────────┐
   │             │     Power Distribution│Bus (PDB)    │
   │      ┌──────┴─────┐          ┌──────┴─────┐       │
   │      │  ESC 1     │          │  ESC 2     │  ...  │
   │      │  (Motor 1) │          │  (Motor 2) │       │
   │      └────────────┘          └────────────┘       │
   │                                                    │
   │  5V BEC ────► Flight Controller (FC)               │
   │  5V BEC ────► Receiver (Rx)                        │
   │  9V / 10V ──► Video Transmitter (VTx)              │
   │  Battery V ─► Camera (direct or via FC)            │
   └────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Each ESC in this architecture is both a power converter and a signal interpreter. It receives a digital throttle command over DShot — a protocol encoding 11 bits of throttle position plus a telemetry request bit, transmitted as a stream of high/low pulses timed to distinguish 0 from 1 — and uses that command to drive a three-phase bridge of MOSFET pairs. The three motor wires connect to the midpoints of these pairs; by switching the MOSFETs in a precisely sequenced pattern, the ESC synthesizes a rotating magnetic field that the rotor's permanent magnets chase. This is the heart of brushless commutation: the ESC is constantly re-creating an artificial "brush" position in software, polling the back-EMF zero crossings or using a dedicated encoder to know rotor position, then advancing the energized phase 120° ahead of the rotor to maximize torque production.

The stator and rotor geometry of a typical freestyle or race motor — take the ubiquitous 2207 as a reference — describes a 22 mm stator diameter and 7 mm stator height. These two numbers encode the motor's thermal mass, magnetic flux path cross-section, and the lever arm available for torque generation. A wider stator (2306, 2306) increases the air gap radius, amplifying torque at the cost of increased rotational inertia — the motor spools up and down more slowly, which matters enormously for the sharp throttle impulses of freestyle flight. Stator height adds copper volume, which reduces winding resistance and increases peak current handling, but adds weight and axial length. The 2207 sits at a point of maturation: it offers enough torque for 5-inch props on 4S or 6S while remaining light enough that four of them don't overwhelm a 30×30 stack frame.

The magnetic circuit within the motor is worth understanding physically. The stator is laminated electrical steel — thin sheets bonded together — to suppress eddy current losses that would otherwise heat the core at high commutation frequencies. The rotor carries N52-grade sintered neodymium magnets arranged in alternating polarity. For a 12-pole (12-magnet) rotor paired with a 9-stator-tooth arrangement (9N12P), the motor completes one electrical cycle for every two mechanical degrees of rotation — meaning at 40,000 RPM, the ESC is commutating at frequencies above 3 kHz. At these frequencies, stray inductance in the motor windings and the ESC's power loop becomes significant; this is why low-ESR electrolytic or MLCC bulk capacitors soldered directly across the ESC power pads are not optional. Without them, the switching transients — voltage spikes from the collapsing magnetic field in the motor windings — can exceed the MOSFET's drain-source breakdown voltage and destroy the ESC in milliseconds.

The relationship between voltage, current, power, and thrust connects these abstract circuit properties to the physical experience of flight. Total electrical power consumed by the system is simply $P = V \times I$, where $V$ is the battery terminal voltage under load and $I$ is the total draw from all four motors plus ancillary systems. For a typical 5-inch freestyle quad drawing 60 A at full throttle on 4S (nominally 15.0 V under moderate sag):

$$P = 15.0\,\text{V} \times 60\,\text{A} = 900\,\text{W}$$

That 900 W produces approximately 1,800–2,000 g of total thrust in a well-matched build (thrust-to-weight ratio of 10:1 on a 180 g airframe with a 250 g pack is aggressive but achievable). The efficiency of converting electrical power to mechanical thrust — measured in grams per watt — varies from roughly 3.5 g/W at hover throttle to 2.0 g/W at full throttle, because propeller aerodynamic efficiency degrades as advance ratio increases. This is why hover-optimized long-range builds use large-diameter, low-pitch, low-KV motors: they operate on the efficient left side of the power curve. Race and freestyle builds accept the inefficiency because they spend most of their time in dynamic maneuvering, where peak acceleration matters more than endurance.

Voltage sag can be modeled directly with the battery's internal resistance. If a 4S 1500 mAh pack has 4 mΩ per cell (total series resistance of 16 mΩ for the pack), and the quadcopter draws 60 A:

$$V_\text{sag} = I \times R_\text{internal} = 60\,\text{A} \times 0.016\,\Omega = 0.96\,\text{V}$$

A pack resting at 15.6 V will deliver 14.64 V to the ESCs under that load — a sag of roughly 0.96 V. As the pack ages and internal resistance climbs to 20 mΩ per cell (80 mΩ total), the same 60 A draw produces:

$$V_\text{sag} = 60 \times 0.080 = 4.8\,\text{V}$$

The motor now sees 10.8 V instead of 15.6 V — a 31% voltage deficit, with a proportional reduction in RPM and thrust. This is the moment a 6-month-old pack that "shows full voltage on the charger" suddenly feels dead in the air.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

The gap between a mediocre power system and a high-performance one rarely comes from component selection alone. It comes from understanding where resistance hides, where heat accumulates, and how the system degrades under repeated stress — then designing and maintaining it with those vectors in mind.

Connector selection is one of the highest-leverage decisions in the power chain, yet it's treated as an afterthought by beginners who buy whatever battery happens to ship with an XT30. The XT60 connector, rated to 60 A continuous and 90 A burst, is the correct choice for 5-inch and larger builds. Its gold-plated bullet contacts provide contact resistance below 0.5 mΩ per pair when new — negligible. But XT60 contacts that have been mated and unmated hundreds of times, exposed to prop wash vibration, or soldered with insufficient heat (causing cold joints in the barrel) can climb to 2–5 mΩ or higher. Multiply that by peak current and you're generating heat at the connector itself. The diagnostic is simple: after a hard throttle flight, touch the XT60 body. Warm is acceptable. Hot enough to notice is a problem. Hot enough to cause discomfort means you have a resistance point that needs resoldering or connector replacement.

Bulk capacitance on the ESC power inputs deserves careful attention. The industry standard guidance is to add one or two 1000 µF, 35 V electrolytic capacitors in parallel across the battery leads, as close to the ESC pads as the frame allows. The purpose is to provide a local charge reservoir that absorbs the switching transients before they reach the battery leads, which act as inductors at high frequencies. Without adequate bulk capacitance, every hard throttle punch generates a ringing spike that stresses the MOSFET gate oxides cumulatively. The correct soldering technique is to pre-tin the capacitor leads and the ESC pads, apply the capacitor with just enough solder to make a solid fillet, and verify polarity obsessively — a reversed electrolytic across a charged 6S pack will vent immediately and potentially cause a fire. Low-ESR aluminum electrolytics (Panasonic FR or similar) are preferred over generic bulk electrolytics because their impedance remains low at the switching frequencies (10–50 kHz) where they need to perform.

Motor failure in FPV builds follows predictable patterns, and recognizing them early prevents crashes. The first is bearing failure: a motor that makes a grinding or gritty sound when spun by hand, or that shows radial play when you grip the bell and wiggle it, has compromised bearings. Bearings in FPV motors live a brutal life — continuous high-RPM operation, vibration, grass and dirt ingestion from low-to-ground flights, and the occasional hard crash. Replacement bearings (typically MR1150ZZ or MR850ZZ depending on the stator diameter) can be pressed in with a bench vise and an appropriately sized socket in under ten minutes. The second failure mode is winding damage from prop strikes. When a prop strikes and the motor is abruptly halted at speed, the kinetic energy of the rotor spikes the motor current instantaneously. This often destroys a phase of the ESC rather than the motor itself, but repeated impacts will eventually crack the lacquer insulation on the windings. A motor with winding shorts draws asymmetric current across its three phases, produces a characteristic growling noise under power, and runs hot on one side of the bell. The diagnostic is resistance measurement across all three motor lead pairs with a good multimeter: they should all read within 0.1 Ω of each other. Any pair showing significantly higher or lower resistance indicates a shorted or open winding.

Thermal management of the ESC stack is a function of airframe design that is neglected in most off-the-shelf frames. The ESC MOSFETs generate heat proportional to $I^2 R_\text{DS(on)}$, where $R_\text{DS(on)}$ is the drain-source on-resistance of the MOSFET — typically 0.5–1.5 mΩ for modern FPV ESC MOSFETs. At 60 A total draw split across three phases, the peak phase current during full commutation approaches 40 A. MOSFET dissipation at that current with a 1 mΩ device is $P = 40^2 \times 0.001 = 1.6$ W per device, and a typical 4-in-1 ESC has 12 MOSFETs — 19 W of heat in a component the size of a matchbook. Stack builds that sandwhich the ESC between two carbon plates with no airflow are thermal disasters waiting to happen. Orienting the ESC with its underside exposed to the inlet airflow from below, or leaving 3–5 mm of clearance between the ESC and the bottom plate for convective airflow, can reduce steady-state ESC temperature by 20–30°C under sustained full-throttle operation.

Battery storage and cycling discipline directly controls the total investment lifespan of your LiPo fleet. A pack charged to 4.20 V per cell and left for a week loses measurable capacity from cathode lattice strain. The same pack stored at 3.80–3.85 V per cell — storage voltage — retains its capacity through hundreds of storage cycles. Every modern smart charger (ISDT Q6 Plus, Hota D6 Pro) has a "storage charge" mode that will bring packs to this voltage whether currently above or below it. Beyond storage practice, never discharge LiPo packs below 3.5 V per cell under load, and treat 3.3 V under load as an absolute floor that terminates flight immediately. Consistently flying to 3.5 V per cell under load is the threshold at which copper lithium plating begins occurring on the anode — a one-way structural change that permanently increases internal resistance and eventual thermal runaway risk.

Matching the entire power system — battery C-rating, ESC continuous current rating, and motor maximum current — requires working the numbers from the motor outward. Measure the maximum current draw of your motor-prop combination on a thrust stand (or look up manufacturer data for the specific combination). A 2207 2450KV motor on a Gemfan 5152 prop at 4S full throttle draws approximately 38–42 A. Four motors draw 152–168 A peak, though quad full throttle simultaneously is rare outside of the first 0.5 seconds of a launch. Derate by 80% for practical continuous design: plan your ESC for 120 A continuous (30 A per ESC on a 4-in-1, selecting an ESC rated 40 A continuous for thermal headroom). Select a battery whose C-rating yields 150 A: a 1500 mAh pack at 100C true-rating would require a pack delivering 150 A, but since rated figures are optimistic, select a pack from a manufacturer whose independently tested internal resistance supports the actual current delivery. BenchTopp and RCGroups current load tests are more trustworthy than the C-ratings printed on the shrink wrap.

The final discipline is systematic load testing before any new build flies. Connect the quad to a watt meter (the Runcam Power Meter or any inline XT60 watt meter) and mount it securely to a thrust stand or hard surface. Advance throttle to 50% for ten seconds and read the current draw and pack voltage. Then advance to full throttle for two to three seconds and read peak current and minimum voltage. This single bench test tells you everything: whether your power system is matched, whether voltage sag is within acceptable limits, whether the ESC or motor runs hot, and whether the OSD voltage alarms you've configured will trigger at the right points in real flight. It costs ten minutes and prevents a catastrophic first flight. The electromechanical foundation of your quad is exactly as reliable as the rigor you apply before the props ever touch the air.
