# Lesson 2: Plant Physiology & Propagation Mechanisms

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Every plant you cultivate is running a continuous biochemical negotiation between the energy available in its environment and the metabolic cost of staying alive. Understanding this negotiation at a mechanistic level transforms gardening from a collection of folk heuristics into a precision discipline. When a seedling fails to germinate, when a cutting refuses to root, when a mature plant suddenly chloroses despite adequate watering — the cause is always traceable to one or more of the core physiological systems that govern plant life. Mastery begins by internalizing these systems not as biology-class abstractions but as mechanical subsystems with inputs, outputs, feedback loops, and failure modes you can observe and correct at the bench.

The first invariant is this: a plant is fundamentally an energy-capture and carbon-fixation engine. Everything else — root architecture, vascular plumbing, reproductive strategy, dormancy behavior — is infrastructure built to serve that engine. Photosynthesis in the mesophyll cells of a leaf converts photons, water, and carbon dioxide into glucose and oxygen via two coupled stages. The light-dependent reactions, occurring in the thylakoid membranes of chloroplasts, use photon energy to split water molecules, releasing oxygen and driving the production of ATP and NADPH. These energy carriers then power the Calvin cycle in the stroma, which fixes atmospheric CO₂ into the three-carbon sugar glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, the precursor to glucose. The net equation — 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ — is deceptively compact. Each arrow hides a cascade of enzyme-catalyzed reactions whose efficiency is exquisitely sensitive to temperature, light intensity, CO₂ concentration, and the availability of mineral co-factors such as magnesium (the central atom in the chlorophyll porphyrin ring), manganese, and iron.

Respiration is the other half of the energy ledger, and unlike photosynthesis it runs continuously — day and night, in every living cell of the plant. Cellular respiration oxidizes glucose back to CO₂ and water, releasing the ATP that powers growth, ion uptake, protein synthesis, and cell division. The critical ratio to internalize is the light compensation point: the irradiance level at which a leaf's photosynthetic output exactly equals its respiratory consumption. Below this point, the plant is running a net energy deficit. A seedling kept on a dimly lit windowsill in winter is not slowly growing — it is slowly dying, burning its seed reserves without sufficient photosynthetic income to replace them. For most vegetable crops, the light compensation point sits somewhere between 1,000 and 2,000 lux, while the photosynthetic saturation point — above which additional light produces no marginal gain — typically falls between 25,000 and 50,000 lux depending on species. This is why a 4000K full-spectrum LED panel positioned 15–20 cm above seedling trays at 200–300 µmol/m²/s PPFD dramatically outperforms a south-facing window for indoor propagation in winter latitudes.

Transpiration is frequently taught as a passive side effect of gas exchange, but that framing undersells its mechanical role in the plant's hydraulic system. As CO₂ diffuses inward through stomatal pores for use in the Calvin cycle, water vapor diffuses outward along a vapor-pressure gradient — a gradient maintained by the constant evaporative demand of the atmosphere. This flux creates a tension, a negative-pressure gradient in the water column of the xylem, which is the fundamental force that pulls water from roots to canopy. The cohesion-tension mechanism works because water molecules are hydrogen-bonded to each other and to the hydrophilic walls of xylem conduits, allowing the column to sustain tension without cavitating under normal conditions. When atmospheric vapor-pressure deficit spikes on a hot, dry afternoon, transpiration rate surges, xylem tension increases, and if root water uptake cannot keep pace, stomata close to protect the hydraulic column from runaway cavitation. This is the mechanistic explanation for midday wilt in transplants — not heat damage to leaves, but hydraulic failure in an insufficiently established root system.

The second invariant is that the vascular system is a bidirectional transport network with physically distinct channels for different cargo. Xylem moves water and dissolved minerals upward from roots to shoots and is composed largely of dead cells — tracheids and vessel elements — whose lignified walls form rigid, continuous tubes. Phloem, by contrast, moves photosynthate (primarily sucrose) in both directions along source-to-sink gradients, and is composed of living sieve tube elements paired with companion cells that actively load and unload sugars. A source is any tissue producing more sugar than it consumes — a mature, sun-exposed leaf. A sink is any tissue consuming more sugar than it produces — a developing fruit, a root tip, a germinating seed. The pressure-flow hypothesis describes phloem transport as driven by an osmotic pressure gradient: active sucrose loading at source regions draws water into the phloem by osmosis, raising hydrostatic pressure, while sucrose unloading at sink regions reduces pressure, creating bulk flow toward the sink. This distinction matters enormously in practice. Girdling — removing a ring of bark (which contains the phloem) from a fruit tree branch — starves roots of photosynthate while leaving the xylem intact, and was historically used to increase fruit size by concentrating sugars above the girdle. Knowing which layer of tissue you are cutting when you take a T-bud or a bark graft determines the graft's survival.

The third invariant governs reproduction: plants have evolved two fundamentally different strategies for creating new individuals, and each strategy makes different physical and genetic demands on the propagator. Sexual reproduction via seed introduces genetic recombination, generates dormancy mechanisms timed to seasonal cues, and produces an embryo with its own root-shoot polarity established during seed development. Vegetative (asexual) reproduction via cuttings, divisions, layering, or grafting produces a genetic clone of the parent, bypasses the embryonic phase entirely, and relies on the plant's capacity for de novo organogenesis — specifically, the induction of adventitious roots from non-root tissue. These are not interchangeable processes. They require different substrates, different hormonal environments, and different environmental controls.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

### The Vascular Network: Structure and Signal Paths

The internal architecture of a plant stem, viewed in cross-section, reveals its transport logic directly. In a dicotyledonous plant — the broad-leaf species that make up most garden vegetables, ornamentals, and woody shrubs — the vascular bundles are arranged in a ring, with xylem toward the pith and phloem toward the outer bark, separated by a meristematic layer called the vascular cambium. In monocots (grasses, corn, lilies), the bundles are scattered throughout the cross-section without a continuous cambium ring, which is why monocots cannot produce secondary (radial) growth and you cannot graft them using cambium-alignment techniques.

```
        Dicot Stem Cross-Section
        ─────────────────────────
              [Epidermis]
            [Cortex / Bark]
          [Phloem]  ← sugars, bidirectional
         [Cambium]  ← meristematic, divides to form new xylem/phloem
          [Xylem]   ← water & minerals, upward
              [Pith]
```

The cambium is the key structure for all grafting operations. When you align the cambium of a rootstock to the cambium of a scion and exclude air and desiccation with grafting tape or parafilm, the cells at the cut faces begin dividing within days, forming a callus bridge. Vascular differentiation within the callus eventually re-establishes xylem-to-xylem and phloem-to-phloem continuity. If cambium layers are offset by more than a millimeter, callus forms but functional vascular union is delayed or incomplete, and the graft union will be mechanically weak even if the scion appears to leaf out initially.

The stomatal aperture control system is a hydraulic servo mechanism worth dissecting precisely because it is the primary lever you manipulate when managing seedling and cutting environments. Guard cells surrounding each stomate are specialized epidermal cells with unevenly thickened walls: the inner wall (facing the pore) is thick and inextensible, while the outer wall is thin and elastic. When guard cells accumulate potassium ions and water via active transport, turgor pressure increases and the asymmetric wall geometry causes the cell to bow outward, opening the pore. The signal chain that triggers potassium uptake involves blue-light photoreceptors (phototropins), which activate H⁺-ATPase proton pumps in the guard cell membrane — meaning light itself directly opens stomata independent of CO₂ concentration. Abscisic acid (ABA), synthesized in roots under drought stress and transported to leaves via the xylem, triggers stomatal closure via a separate phosphorylation cascade. This dual control is why overhead misting of cuttings reduces wilt: by raising the vapor-pressure gradient on the leaf surface, you reduce transpirational demand without altering the light-driven stomatal opening signal, buying time for root initiation to establish hydraulic supply.

### Germination: The Trigger Sequence

Germination is not simply a matter of providing water and warmth. It is a precisely ordered developmental program gated by multiple environmental signals, any one of which can act as a bottleneck. The sequence, from a practitioner standpoint, runs as follows.

Water imbibition is the physical trigger: dry seeds typically contain 5–15% moisture by weight, and the seed coat must absorb enough water to rehydrate and activate the embryo's metabolic machinery. Some seeds — notably those with hard, water-impermeable seed coats like morning glory (*Ipomoea*) or scarlet runner bean — require scarification before imbibition can occur. Mechanical scarification with 80-grit sandpaper on a folded piece of sandpaper pinched between fingers, rubbing the seed coat until it appears dull, is reliably effective. Soaking in near-boiling water (80–90°C, allowed to cool for 24 hours) achieves the same end for smaller seed lots. The goal is simply to breach the waxy or stony testa layer without damaging the embryo beneath it.

Temperature then controls enzyme activation. Each species has a base temperature below which germination enzymes are essentially inactive, an optimal range, and an upper threshold above which heat denaturation of proteins begins. Cool-season crops like lettuce (*Lactuca sativa*) and spinach (*Spinacia oleracea*) have base temperatures near 2°C and optimal germination at 15–20°C, which is why they germinate poorly on a heat mat set to the standard 25°C used for peppers and tomatoes. Heat-loving crops like basil (*Ocimum basilicum*) require substrate temperatures above 20°C and perform best at 25–28°C. A dual-zone heat mat with an infrared thermometer probe at root depth — not air temperature — is the right tool for this measurement. The 10–15 cm above the mat surface can be 5–8°C cooler than the mat surface, so measuring ambient air temperature gives you a meaningless number.

Light and photoperiod requirements form a third gate for many species. Photoblastic seeds require a specific light signal to break dormancy: positively photoblastic seeds (lettuce, many wildflowers) germinate only when exposed to red light (660 nm) which activates the phytochrome Pr → Pfr conversion, while negatively photoblastic seeds (some alliums, phacelia) are inhibited by light. This is a practical issue: pressing lettuce seed into a propagation tray and covering with perlite will suppress germination; pressing it gently onto the surface under a transparent dome will not.

Stratification addresses physiological dormancy in many woody perennials and cold-climate annuals. The embryo contains growth-inhibiting hormones (primarily ABA) at a concentration that suppresses germination even under ideal moisture and temperature conditions. Cold, moist stratification — typically 4–10°C for 4–12 weeks — allows slow enzymatic degradation of ABA and accumulation of growth-promoting gibberellins, shifting the hormonal balance toward germination. The practical protocol is to mix seeds with moist (but not wet) vermiculite in a zip-lock bag and refrigerate it at 4°C for the species-appropriate duration. For woody species like Japanese maple (*Acer palmatum*), 90 days of cold stratification followed by warm stratification replicates the autumn-winter-spring progression the seed would experience naturally.

### Vegetative Propagation: Hormonal Architecture of Rooting

When you take a stem cutting, you are asking non-root tissue to generate an entirely new root system through adventitious root organogenesis. The competence of a stem to do this depends on its concentration of auxin (primarily indole-3-acetic acid, IAA), synthesized in actively growing shoot tips and transported basipetally (tip to base) through the phloem and parenchyma. Stem bases exposed to this auxin flux accumulate sufficient IAA to trigger the dedifferentiation of procambial or parenchyma cells into root initials.

The practical implication is that the basal cut of a cutting should be made with a clean, sharp blade — either a scalpel or a freshly honed grafting knife — immediately below a node, where auxin flux from the stem above is highest. Crushing the basal tissue with a dull blade destroys the cells most likely to form root initials. The basal wound should be made at a slight angle to maximize surface area exposed to the rooting medium. For semi-hardwood and hardwood cuttings, wounding the base by scraping a 2–3 cm strip of bark from one or both sides with the blade back exposes the cambium directly to the medium and rooting hormone, further accelerating root initiation.

Exogenous auxin application in the form of indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) powder or gel is not a substitute for the plant's own auxin flux — it is an amplifier. IBA is a synthetic auxin analogue that is more stable and less mobile than IAA, meaning it stays concentrated at the application site and maintains a local high-auxin environment for days rather than hours. Standard formulations are:

| Cutting Type | IBA Concentration | Form |
|---|---|---|
| Softwood (early season, herbaceous) | 1,000–3,000 ppm | Talc powder or liquid dip (5 sec) |
| Semi-hardwood (summer, partially lignified) | 3,000–8,000 ppm | Talc powder or gel |
| Hardwood (dormant, fully lignified) | 10,000–20,000 ppm | Gel or concentrated liquid dip |

The rooting substrate must simultaneously provide moisture retention, adequate aeration, and near-zero fertility. High fertility encourages top growth at the expense of root initiation — the cutting can sustain leaf growth by drawing down reserves before roots have formed, depleting those reserves without achieving a hydraulic connection. A 50:50 mix of coarse perlite (4 mm grade) and coir fiber, or a 1:1:1 mix of perlite, vermiculite, and coir, achieves a substrate-moisture tension that keeps cuttings hydrated without waterlogging the base. Pure perlite works well for high-humidity tent propagation because aeration is maximized, but it demands more frequent misting as it dries quickly.

### Dormancy and Hardiness Zones: Environmental Calibration

Dormancy is a genetically encoded response to photoperiod and temperature that allows perennial plants to survive seasonally hostile conditions. It is not simply the absence of growth — it is an actively regulated state maintained by hormonal and epigenetic mechanisms that require specific environmental signals to both enter and exit. Practitioners manipulate dormancy constantly: forcing bulbs, chilling bare-root perennials, cold-stratifying seeds, and scheduling rootstock preparation for grafting all depend on understanding the depth and release conditions of dormancy.

Endodormancy — the deepest phase — is maintained by high ABA concentrations and must be broken by a chilling requirement measured in chill hours: hours at temperatures between 0°C and 7.2°C (some models use 0–10°C). Apple varieties like 'Bramley' require 1,000+ chill hours; low-chill cultivars like 'Anna' need only 200–300 hours. A tree pulled out of dormancy prematurely by a mid-winter warm spell will often fail to leaf out uniformly in spring, because only the most accessible buds received sufficient chilling. This is not a cultural failure — it is a mechanistic consequence of uneven chilling accumulation in a year with unusual temperature patterns.

USDA Hardiness Zone maps divide the North American continent (and are now published for the UK, Europe, and Australia) into 13 zones based solely on average annual extreme minimum temperature, in 5°F (2.8°C) increments. Zone 6a has an average minimum of -23.3°C to -20.6°C; Zone 9b has an average minimum of -3.9°C to -1.1°C. The zones define the lower survival threshold for perennial tissue — specifically, the temperature at which cellular ice crystal formation causes irreversible plasma membrane damage. Plants in zone-appropriate climates have evolved or been selected for sufficient antifreeze protein production, membrane lipid unsaturation, and osmotic adjustment in their cells to survive minimum temperatures within their zone. The critical practitioner invariant: hardiness zones define cold-hardiness only. They say nothing about heat tolerance, humidity requirements, soil chemistry, or summer high temperature ranges, all of which must be sourced from supplementary data like the AHS Plant Heat Zone Map.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

### Propagation Environment Control

The single highest-leverage intervention in a cutting propagation operation is managing the vapor-pressure deficit (VPD) of the air surrounding the cuttings. VPD is the difference between the amount of water vapor the air can hold at a given temperature and the amount it actually holds, expressed in kilopascals. At high VPD, the driving force for leaf transpiration is large; at low VPD, it is small. Cuttings without roots have no hydraulic supply to meet transpirational demand, so they must be held at a VPD low enough that transpiration is effectively arrested without introducing the fungal conditions associated with 100% relative humidity.

The target range for most softwood and semi-hardwood cuttings is 0.3–0.6 kPa, which at 22°C corresponds to roughly 85–90% relative humidity. A simple digital hygrometer placed inside the propagation dome gives you real-time VPD-adjacent data (you can compute exact VPD from temperature and RH using the August-Roche-Magnus approximation), but a more practical approach is to observe the cuttings directly. If leaves are showing turgid, upright growth and no visible condensation is pooling on the dome interior, the humidity balance is approximately right. If leaves are wilting despite full dome closure, the substrate temperature may be too high relative to air temperature, increasing xylem tension. If condensation is streaming down the dome interior constantly, ventilate briefly each morning to exchange air and reduce fungal pressure.

Basal temperature management deserves particular emphasis. The enzymatic processes of root initiation have optimal temperatures between 21°C and 27°C for most temperate species, but the air temperature around the cuttings should be kept lower — 18–22°C — to reduce transpiration demand. This is the logic behind bottom-heat propagation: a heat mat warms the rooting zone to the enzymatic optimum while cooler air above the substrate reduces leaf-level transpiration. Thermally controlling these two zones independently is the practice that separates a 20% strike rate from an 80% strike rate on difficult-to-root subjects like magnolias, camellias, and large-flowered rhododendrons.

Lighting during propagation requires restraint. Cuttings not yet capable of sustaining themselves through photosynthesis are not helped by high light intensity — they are stressed by it. Stomata open in response to blue light, increasing transpiration, while photosynthetic capacity is limited without a functional root system to supply the water and minerals the Calvin cycle requires. During the first seven to ten days of propagation, ambient light at 50–100 µmol/m²/s PPFD on a 16-hour photoperiod provides enough signal to maintain chlorophyll synthesis and low-level photosynthesis without imposing significant transpirational stress. As root primordia develop and begin to extend into the medium — typically visible as white nodules at the wound site between days 10 and 21 for softwood cuttings — light intensity can be gradually increased to 150–200 µmol/m²/s.

### Failure Mode Analysis and Remediation

The most common failure in seed germination is damping-off, a fungal collapse of seedling stems at or just below the substrate surface caused by *Pythium*, *Rhizoctonia*, or *Fusarium* species. The mechanism is straightforward: these pathogens thrive in anaerobic, moisture-saturated conditions at the substrate surface. Damping-off is not primarily a contamination problem — the spores are ubiquitous in the environment. It is a substrate hydrology problem. Overwatering eliminates the air-filled pore space in the substrate that provides the oxygen the seedling root needs and that suppresses anaerobic pathogen activity. Correcting it requires switching to bottom-watering (allowing the substrate to draw water upward by capillary action from a reservoir below, leaving the surface relatively dry), increasing substrate drainage with added perlite, and reducing dome time once cotyledons have fully expanded.

Etiolation — the pale, spindly, elongated growth of seedlings in insufficient light — is irreversible in affected tissue. The hypocotyl cells have already expanded well beyond their optimal length, and the structural carbohydrate allocation to those cells cannot be reallocated. The only intervention is to prevent it in future sowings with adequate light from the moment of emergence. If seedlings are already mildly etiolated, burying the hypocotyl deeper in the substrate at first potting will stabilize the stem and direct adventitious root production from the buried section — tomatoes, in particular, root readily from buried stem tissue, turning a liability into a productive root system.

Callus formation without root initiation is a frustrating failure mode in difficult-to-root woody cuttings. The basal end produces a white or cream-colored callus mass but no root primordia emerge. This typically indicates one of three conditions: auxin concentration at the base is sufficient to trigger cell division (callus) but not high enough or geometrically distributed correctly for root polarity to establish; rooting substrate temperature is below 20°C, insufficient to sustain the metabolic rate required for organogenesis; or the cutting was taken from tissue that is too mature (fully dormant, non-juvenile hardwood) for the current IBA formulation. The remediation protocol is to increase IBA concentration by one tier, verify substrate temperature at root-zone depth with a probe thermometer, and if tissue maturity is the suspected issue, source cuttings from epicormic regrowth (water shoots) or coppiced stools, which produce physiologically juvenile tissue with higher rooting competence even on old specimens.

Graft union failure after apparent initial take — the scion leafs out, then collapses three to six weeks later — indicates that vascular continuity was achieved only superficially. The callus bridge formed, allowing water transport to sustain initial leaf expansion, but the xylem-to-xylem differentiation within the union was incomplete, and as the scion's initial demand exceeded the union's hydraulic conductance, the union failed under load. Causes include cambium misalignment by more than a millimeter, desiccation of the union before callus formation completed (grafting tape applied too loosely or removed too early), or a thermal incompatibility where union temperature fluctuated above the callus formation optimum of 22–27°C. Re-grafting with parafilm-wrapped unions, shading to stabilize union temperature, and verifying rootstock compatibility with the scion species at the cultivar level (not just the genus level) addresses all three causes systematically.

### Scaling Up: Managing Multiple Propagation Streams

A serious propagation operation running simultaneous seed germination, softwood cuttings, and hardwood graft work in a single bench space must segregate these streams by environmental requirements rather than by physical proximity. Germination trays need high basal heat (25°C substrate) and covered domes. Softwood cuttings need high humidity, moderate light, and 22–24°C base temperature. Dormant hardwood grafts need union-zone temperatures of 22–27°C with the rootstock crown kept cold to prevent premature break. These are incompatible environments running simultaneously in many propagation cycles, and attempting to compromise on a single intermediate environment degrades performance across all streams.

The practical resolution is bench zoning: heat mats on separate controllers, independent humidity domes, and a programmable LED panel (using a controller like the Mars Hydro FC-E series or Photontek X controller) with individually addressable zones that can deliver different DLI targets across the bench. A notebook-format log tracking substrate temperature, RH, cutting date, rooting hormone concentration, and first root emergence date per batch gives you the dataset to continuously refine your strike rates. Without these records, you are simply repeating anecdotal practice. With them, you are running a replicable protocol that will compress the learning curve on difficult subjects from seasons to batches.

The physiology does not change. The plant's machinery runs on the same chemical logic whether you understand it or not. The practitioner advantage lies in knowing exactly which variable you are adjusting when you change substrate temperature, light intensity, humidity, or auxin concentration — and being able to read the plant's response as confirmation or correction of your model. That mechanistic fluency, applied with consistent observation, is what separates propagation that reliably produces at scale from propagation that works occasionally and mysteriously.
