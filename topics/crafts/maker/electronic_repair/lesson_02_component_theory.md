# Lesson 2: Core Components, Failure Modes, Testing

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Electronic repair begins not with schematics or test equipment, but with an intimate understanding of how electrons move through matter under varying conditions of voltage, current, temperature, and time. Every component failure tells a story written in the language of material physics: electromigration carving voids through aluminum traces, dielectric breakdown punching through oxide layers molecule by molecule, or thermal fatigue cycling a solder joint until crystalline grain boundaries fracture. The skilled repair technician reads these stories by observing symptoms—a darkened resistor body, a bulging electrolytic capacitor, a transistor that runs scorching hot—and translates them backward through the component's failure mode to identify the root electrical cause.

The fundamental invariant underlying all electronic component behavior is Ohm's law expressed in its most general form: voltage across a component drives current through it according to the component's impedance, which itself varies with frequency, temperature, and aging. A resistor presents purely real impedance that dissipates power as heat following $P = I^2 R$. A capacitor stores energy in an electric field, presenting reactive impedance that falls with rising frequency according to $Z_C = 1/(2\pi f C)$. An inductor stores energy in a magnetic field with impedance rising with frequency as $Z_L = 2\pi f L$. Semiconductors—diodes, transistors, and integrated circuits—manipulate these relationships through doped silicon junctions that control current flow based on applied voltages, creating nonlinear transfer characteristics that enable amplification, switching, and logic operations.

Understanding failure modes requires recognizing that every component has physical limits defined by its material composition and geometry. A carbon film resistor's power rating reflects how much heat its ceramic substrate can conduct away before the carbon film oxidizes and increases in resistance. An electrolytic capacitor's voltage rating marks the threshold where the aluminum oxide dielectric layer begins to break down under electric field stress. A transistor's maximum collector current reflects the point where current density through the silicon die causes excessive heating that degrades the semiconductor junctions. These limits are not abstract numbers but physical boundaries where material phase changes, chemical reactions, or structural damage become inevitable.

The repair technician's task involves three sequential diagnostic layers: first, identify which component has failed through visual inspection and circuit behavior analysis; second, determine the failure mode by examining the component's physical condition and measuring its electrical characteristics; third, trace the failure backward to discover what upstream circuit condition—overvoltage, overcurrent, thermal stress, or aging—caused that particular failure mode. This backward tracing is crucial because replacing a failed component without addressing the root cause simply creates a countdown to the next failure.

Component testing methodology follows a principle of escalating invasiveness. Non-contact measurements come first: infrared thermal imaging to identify abnormally hot components, oscilloscope probing to observe signal distortion, or visual inspection under magnification. In-circuit resistance and capacitance measurements follow, performed with power removed and large capacitors discharged. Finally, if in-circuit testing proves inconclusive, the suspect component must be desoldered and tested in isolation using precision instrumentation. This escalation reflects a practical reality: every time you heat a circuit board with a soldering iron, you risk damaging adjacent components through thermal shock or mechanical stress on fragile traces.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

### Resistor Construction and Failure Signatures

Carbon film resistors consist of a thin carbon-resin compound helically cut into a ceramic rod to achieve the target resistance value, then encapsulated in an epoxy coating with color-coded bands indicating resistance and tolerance. The helical cut path creates a physically long, narrow conductor that dissipates power uniformly across its length. When operated within rating, carbon film resistors exhibit stable resistance across temperature with typical temperature coefficients around 100 parts per million per degree Celsius. Metal film resistors use vacuum-deposited nickel-chromium alloy instead of carbon, achieving temperature coefficients below 25 ppm/°C and tighter tolerance but at higher cost.

The dominant failure mode for resistors is thermal runaway caused by exceeding the power dissipation rating. As current increases, resistor temperature rises according to its thermal resistance to ambient. Higher temperature causes slight resistance increase in carbon film (positive temperature coefficient), which increases power dissipation further if voltage remains constant, creating a feedback loop. Eventually the resistor body reaches temperatures where the carbon film oxidizes, permanently increasing resistance and often causing the epoxy coating to crack, blacken, or completely char. Visually, failed resistors show darkened bands, cracked bodies, or in extreme cases, complete destruction with the carbon film burned away leaving only the ceramic core.

Metal film resistors fail through a different mechanism: electromigration. When current density through the metal film exceeds roughly 10⁴ amperes per square centimeter, metal atoms migrate along the direction of electron flow, gradually opening voids in the film. This failure mode appears as a gradual resistance increase over time rather than catastrophic burnout, though eventually the film opens completely. Wire-wound power resistors, constructed by winding resistance wire around a ceramic bobbin, fail through solder joint fracture at the termination points or wire oxidation at elevated temperatures. These failures typically manifest as intermittent open circuits that come and go with mechanical vibration or thermal cycling.

Testing resistors requires measuring resistance accurately across a wide range. For in-circuit testing, use a digital multimeter in resistance mode with the circuit powered down and all large capacitors discharged. Be aware that parallel circuit paths will cause the measured resistance to read lower than the resistor's actual value—you are measuring the parallel combination of the resistor and all other circuit paths. For precision work, desolder one leg of the resistor to isolate it from parallel paths. Four-wire (Kelvin) resistance measurement eliminates lead resistance error for low-value resistors by using separate current-carrying and voltage-sensing connections, though this technique requires specialized meters or LCR meters rather than standard multimeters.

### Capacitor Physics and Degradation Mechanisms

Capacitors store charge by accumulating opposite polarities on two conductive plates separated by an insulating dielectric material. Capacitance scales with plate area and inversely with separation distance following $C = \epsilon_0 \epsilon_r A / d$, where $\epsilon_r$ is the dielectric material's relative permittivity. Ceramic capacitors use barium titanate or similar high-permittivity ceramics as the dielectric, achieving capacitances from picofarads to tens of microfarads in small surface-mount packages. Film capacitors sandwich polyester or polypropylene dielectric between metallized films, trading lower capacitance density for superior stability and lower loss. Electrolytic capacitors achieve large capacitance by using a microscopically thin aluminum oxide layer as the dielectric, formed electrochemically on an etched aluminum foil, but this construction introduces critical polarity and voltage constraints.

Electrolytic capacitor failure dominates electronic repair work because these components have the shortest operational lifetime and the most dramatic failure modes. The aluminum oxide dielectric exists only because constant DC voltage maintains it through electrochemical equilibrium; if voltage polarity reverses or the capacitor sits unused for extended periods, the oxide layer deteriorates. The liquid electrolyte slowly evaporates through the rubber seal even under normal operation, with evaporation rate doubling approximately every 10°C temperature rise following Arrhenius kinetics. As electrolyte evaporates, equivalent series resistance (ESR) increases because less conductive fluid remains between the foil plates. High ESR causes excessive heating when ripple current flows through the capacitor, accelerating further electrolyte loss in a runaway process. Eventually the capacitor fails to provide adequate filtering, leading to power supply ripple, logic circuit malfunction, or audio amplifier instability.

Visible symptoms of electrolytic capacitor failure include bulging tops where internal pressure from evaporating electrolyte deforms the aluminum can, visible electrolyte leakage around the rubber seal, or in catastrophic cases, complete rupture where the pressure relief vent fails. Before visible failure, rising ESR manifests as circuit symptoms: power supplies with excessive ripple voltage on their outputs, audio amplifiers with distortion or low-frequency oscillation, or motor drives with erratic speed control. Ceramic capacitors fail through cracking of the brittle ceramic dielectric caused by mechanical stress during board flexing or thermal shock during soldering. Cracks create partial short circuits that increase leakage current and eventually lead to complete dielectric breakdown. Film capacitors rarely fail except under overvoltage conditions where the dielectric punctures, creating a permanent short circuit.

Testing capacitors demands multiple measurement approaches because different failure modes require different tests. Capacitance measurement using an LCR meter operated at the capacitor's rated frequency (typically 100 Hz or 1 kHz for electrolytics, 1 kHz to 100 kHz for ceramics) confirms whether the device still provides its rated capacitance within tolerance. However, capacitance alone misses the critical ESR measurement. ESR meters inject a high-frequency AC current (typically 100 kHz) through the capacitor while measuring the in-phase voltage drop, directly calculating series resistance. Specialized ESR meters can perform this test in-circuit without removing the capacitor because the test frequency causes inductive reactance in parallel circuit paths to become very high, effectively isolating the capacitor. Leakage current testing applies rated DC voltage through a current-limiting resistor and measures the steady-state current after full charging. Good capacitors show leakage currents in the nanoampere to microampere range; failed ceramics with cracked dielectrics may leak milliamperes, and shorted capacitors draw whatever current the limiting resistor allows.

### Diode Junction Behavior and Breakdown Modes

Silicon diodes function by joining P-type and N-type doped silicon to create a depletion region at the junction where mobile charge carriers are swept away by the built-in electric field. When forward-biased with P-side positive, the external voltage reduces the depletion region width, allowing majority carriers to diffuse across the junction and conduct current. The relationship between forward voltage and current follows the Shockley diode equation $I = I_S (e^{V/(nV_T)} - 1)$, where $I_S$ is reverse saturation current, $n$ is the ideality factor (typically 1 to 2), and $V_T$ is thermal voltage (approximately 26 mV at room temperature). Practical silicon diodes exhibit forward voltage drops around 0.6 to 0.7 volts, with fast-recovery and Schottky diodes using metal-semiconductor junctions to achieve lower forward drops (0.3 to 0.5 V) and faster switching speeds.

Reverse-biased diodes block current flow because the external voltage widens the depletion region, creating a high-impedance barrier. However, small reverse leakage current flows due to thermally generated minority carriers and surface leakage paths, typically in the nanoampere to microampere range. As reverse voltage approaches the breakdown voltage, two mechanisms can cause current to suddenly increase: avalanche breakdown at voltages typically above 5 volts occurs when accelerated carriers collide with the crystal lattice with enough energy to liberate additional carriers, creating a multiplicative avalanche effect; Zener breakdown at lower voltages results from quantum mechanical tunneling through the narrow depletion region. Avalanche diodes and Zener diodes intentionally operate in breakdown for voltage regulation, but standard rectifier and signal diodes permanently damage when driven into breakdown at excessive current.

Diode failure modes divide into shorts, opens, and excessive leakage. Shorted diodes conduct in both directions with low resistance, typically caused by transient overvoltage that punched through the junction, leaving a conductive path of fused silicon. Once shorted, the damage is permanent and irreversible. Open diodes fail to conduct in either direction, usually resulting from thermal cycling that fractured the bond wire connecting the silicon die to the package leads or mechanical stress that cracked the die itself. Excessive leakage appears as reverse current in the microampere to milliampere range rather than nanoamperes, caused by surface contamination creating leakage paths or junction degradation from repeated thermal stress. Power rectifier diodes in switch-mode power supplies frequently fail shorted due to voltage transients during switching transitions, while signal diodes exposed to static discharge may develop leakage that causes circuit malfunction without complete failure.

Testing diodes uses the multimeter's diode test mode, which sources a small constant current (typically 1 mA) through the diode while measuring the resulting voltage drop. A good silicon diode shows 0.5 to 0.8 volts forward drop when the positive probe contacts the anode and negative probe contacts the cathode. Reversing the probes should show overload or very high resistance, indicating negligible reverse conduction. Schottky diodes show lower forward drops (0.2 to 0.4 V) and slightly higher reverse leakage than silicon PN junction diodes. Testing in-circuit requires awareness that parallel resistances or semiconductor junctions can create alternate current paths that distort measurements. Suspect readings require desoldering one lead for isolated testing. For power rectifiers, observe the diode under infrared thermal imaging during circuit operation—excessive reverse leakage causes heating even when the diode should be blocking, indicating junction degradation. Oscilloscope measurement of rectifier voltage waveforms reveals reverse recovery behavior: slow-recovery diodes show significant reverse current spikes during turn-off, while fast diodes snap off cleanly.

### Transistor Operation and Thermal Failure

Bipolar junction transistors (BJTs) use two back-to-back PN junctions to create a three-terminal amplifier where small base current controls large collector current. In an NPN transistor, the base-emitter junction forward-biases during normal operation, injecting electrons from the heavily-doped emitter into the thin base region. Most electrons diffuse across the base without recombining and are swept into the collector by the reverse-biased base-collector junction. The ratio of collector current to base current defines the current gain $\beta$ (or $h_{FE}$), typically ranging from 50 to 500 depending on device design and operating current. For the transistor to remain in its linear amplification region, the collector-base junction must stay reverse-biased; if collector voltage drops below base voltage, the transistor saturates and both junctions become forward-biased, collapsing the voltage gain.

Metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors (MOSFETs) control current through an electrically different mechanism: voltage applied to the gate terminal (separated from the channel by a thin silicon dioxide insulator) creates or depletes a conductive channel between source and drain. N-channel enhancement-mode MOSFETs require positive gate-source voltage above a threshold (typically 2 to 4 V) to create an electron channel; P-channel devices require negative gate-source voltage. Unlike BJTs that require continuous base current, MOSFETs draw essentially zero DC gate current because the gate is isolated by oxide, though charging and discharging the gate capacitance during switching requires transient current. This difference makes MOSFETs preferable for switching applications where gate drive power must be minimized, while BJTs excel in analog amplifiers where their exponential transfer characteristic provides logarithmic compression.

Transistor failure almost always traces back to thermal stress exceeding the silicon junction's maximum temperature, typically around 150 to 175°C. Current flowing through the collector-emitter or drain-source path dissipates power as heat proportional to the product of voltage and current. This heat must conduct through the transistor die, package, heatsink interface, and ultimately into ambient air. Each interface contributes thermal resistance measured in degrees Celsius per watt, and the sum of these resistances determines junction temperature rise above ambient for a given power dissipation. Inadequate heatsinking, thermal interface material degradation, or excessive ambient temperature drives junction temperature into the failure zone. At elevated temperature, several destructive processes accelerate: carrier mobility decreases causing increased on-state resistance and more heat generation; reverse leakage current increases exponentially, doubling roughly every 10°C; and repeated thermal cycling causes mechanical stress at the die-attach interface or bond wires.

Secondary breakdown represents a particularly insidious failure mode in BJTs where current concentrates in a small region of the die rather than distributing uniformly, creating a localized hot spot. As temperature increases in the hot spot, current gain increases and resistance decreases, drawing more current into the already-hot region in a positive feedback loop. Within microseconds, the hot spot reaches temperatures exceeding 1000°C, melting silicon and creating a permanent short circuit. Secondary breakdown occurs most readily when the transistor operates simultaneously at high voltage and moderate current, conditions common in inductive switching circuits where the transistor must withstand voltage transients while turning off. Discrete transistors specify a safe operating area (SOA) curve showing the boundary of voltage-current combinations that avoid secondary breakdown; exceeding SOA even briefly risks catastrophic failure.

Testing transistors begins with diode-junction measurements using the multimeter's diode mode. An NPN transistor should show forward diode drops (0.6 to 0.8 V) from base to emitter and base to collector when the base is positive, and open circuits with reversed polarity. The collector-emitter junction with base floating should read open in both directions because the back-to-back base-collector and base-emitter junctions block current regardless of polarity. PNP transistors show the inverse pattern. MOSFETs often include an intrinsic body diode between source and drain that will conduct in the reverse direction, showing a normal diode drop. For in-circuit testing, note that surrounding components may provide parallel conduction paths. More comprehensive testing requires measuring DC current gain: apply a known base current through a resistor while measuring collector current at a specified collector-emitter voltage, typically using a transistor curve tracer or a custom test jig with adjustable power supplies and current meters.

### Integrated Circuit Architecture and Diagnostic Complexity

Integrated circuits pack thousands to billions of transistors onto a single silicon die, creating complete functional blocks ranging from operational amplifiers and voltage regulators to microcontrollers and application-specific circuits. At the most basic level, linear ICs (operational amplifiers, voltage references, analog switches) use BJT or MOSFET differential pairs, current mirrors, and gain stages arranged in topologies designed for specific transfer characteristics. Digital ICs build complex state machines from fundamental logic gates—NAND, NOR, flip-flops—with CMOS technology now dominating due to its near-zero static power consumption. Mixed-signal ICs integrate analog and digital circuitry on the same die, requiring careful partitioning to prevent digital switching noise from corrupting sensitive analog signals.

IC failure modes broadly divide into dead devices showing no functionality, partial failures where some circuit blocks work but others fail, and parametric degradation where the device still functions but outside specifications. Complete failures typically result from electrostatic discharge (ESD) that punches through gate oxides or junction damage from latchup (a parasitic thyristor effect in CMOS devices where excessive current flows between power and ground, potentially melting bond wires). Partial failures often indicate damage to specific internal functional blocks, perhaps from an overcurrent condition that destroyed output driver transistors while leaving logic core intact. Parametric failures manifest as increased current consumption, reduced output drive capability, or timing specifications that drift outside tolerance, usually caused by hot carrier injection or other gradual degradation mechanisms.

Logic ICs require testing that verifies correct truth table operation across all input combinations. For simple gates, manually apply input signals using pull-up/pull-down resistors and verify output states with a logic probe. Complex ICs—microcontrollers, FPGAs, memory devices—require specialized test equipment or in-system programming to load test firmware and verify functionality through known-good patterns. Linear ICs need parameter measurement: operational amplifiers must demonstrate correct gain, bandwidth, and output swing; voltage regulators must maintain output voltage within tolerance across load current range; comparators must show clean switching thresholds without hysteresis or oscillation. These measurements require oscilloscopes for dynamic signals, precision voltmeters for DC measurements, and sometimes spectrum analyzers to characterize noise or frequency response.

Power supply ICs deserve special attention because their failure often cascades damage to other components. Buck converters, boost converters, and linear regulators experience high switching currents and voltage stresses that accelerate wear-out. The output MOSFET or pass transistor represents the primary failure point, with excessive on-state resistance causing overheating or complete short-circuit failure that dumps unregulated voltage onto downstream circuits. Testing power supply ICs requires measuring efficiency (output power divided by input power), load regulation (output voltage change versus load current), and transient response (how quickly output recovers from sudden load steps). Thermal imaging during operation identifies overheating components, while oscilloscope current probe measurements reveal excessive switching losses or parasitic ringing.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

### Systematic Fault Isolation Methodology

Efficient component-level repair begins not with random part replacement but with structured fault isolation that narrows the suspect component pool before committing to desoldering work. The initial symptom analysis establishes whether the failure is catastrophic (complete loss of function), intermittent (operation that comes and goes), or parametric (degraded performance). Catastrophic failures often leave visible evidence: burned resistors, exploded capacitors, or cracked PCB traces. Intermittent failures typically indicate cold solder joints, cracked components, or thermal-dependent failures that appear only at temperature. Parametric failures—reduced audio power output, unstable switching regulator operation, or logic timing violations—point toward component aging or marginal operating conditions.

Power supply analysis forms the first diagnostic step for any circuit because improper supply voltage cascades effects throughout the system. Measure all DC rails with a precision multimeter, comparing against schematic specifications. Out-of-tolerance voltages immediately focus attention on the power supply circuit feeding that rail. Use oscilloscope AC-coupling to examine ripple voltage on DC supplies; excessive ripple (typically above 100 mV peak-to-peak on logic supplies, 50 mV on analog rails) indicates failing filter capacitors or switching regulator instability. For switching converters, capture the switch node waveform: it should show clean transitions between on-state and off-state voltages without significant ringing or shoot-through conduction where high-side and low-side switches conduct simultaneously.

Thermal imaging provides non-invasive fault detection by revealing components operating outside normal temperature ranges. Powered circuits in steady-state reach thermal equilibrium where heat generation equals heat dissipation. Abnormally hot components—those exceeding expected temperature based on rated power dissipation—indicate excessive current draw, increased resistance, or inadequate thermal management. Conversely, components that should dissipate power but remain cold suggest open circuits or failed gate drive. Infrared cameras visualize temperature distributions across entire PCBs; thermal image overlays compared against known-good boards highlight anomalies. For targeted spot checks, non-contact infrared thermometers provide sufficient precision at lower cost than thermal cameras.

Signal tracing with an oscilloscope follows logical signal flow from input to output, identifying the stage where expected signals disappear or become distorted. In digital circuits, verify clock signals first—missing or distorted clocks prevent everything downstream from functioning. Check reset signals to confirm they properly initialize state machines. Trace data signals through logic gates and flip-flops, comparing captured waveforms against expected timing diagrams. In analog circuits, inject a known test signal at the input and follow amplification through each gain stage. Measure bias voltages at transistor bases and FET gates, confirming they sit within the correct range to establish proper operating point. Distorted signals at a stage output but correct signals at the input localize the failure to components in that stage.

### Advanced Test Equipment Techniques

Time-domain reflectometry (TDR) extends component testing beyond simple DC measurements by exploiting transmission line effects to locate impedance discontinuities along cables or PCB traces. A TDR instrument launches a fast-edge step voltage into the transmission line and displays the reflected waveform. The round-trip propagation delay to a reflection identifies the physical distance to an impedance change, calculated from signal velocity in the medium (typically 60% to 80% of light speed in PCB dielectrics). Sudden impedance increases (open circuits, damaged traces, cracked vias) create positive reflections; impedance decreases (short circuits, excessive loading) produce negative reflections. TDR proves invaluable for locating intermittent PCB faults buried in multi-layer boards where visual inspection fails.

Vector network analyzers (VNAs) characterize component impedance across frequency by measuring incident and reflected RF signals at test ports. Sweeping frequency while monitoring return loss (S11 parameter) reveals resonances, parasitic reactances, and impedance mismatches that affect high-frequency circuit operation. Trace inductance in power distribution networks creates impedance peaks at specific frequencies, potentially causing power integrity problems. Capacitor self-resonance—the frequency where parasitic inductance cancels capacitance—limits effectiveness at high frequency. VNA measurements guide component selection for bypass capacitors, RF matching networks, and transmission line terminations. While professional VNAs cost thousands of dollars, USB-based nanoVNA devices provide serviceable frequency-domain measurement up to several gigahertz at hobby-friendly prices.

Curve tracers apply swept voltage to semiconductor junctions while displaying current on an X-Y oscilloscope or computer screen, generating characteristic curves that reveal subtle parametric failures invisible to simple pass-fail tests. Transistor curve tracers step base current through a series of values while sweeping collector-emitter voltage, displaying the family of collector current curves that define operating characteristics. The curves reveal current gain uniformity, saturation voltage, and breakdown behavior. Deviations from expected curves indicate damaged junctions, excessive leakage, or shifts in doping profiles. Diode curves plotted on log scale expose forward voltage shifts and reverse leakage increases that compromise rectifier or protection diode performance. Modern semiconductor analyzers integrate curve tracing with parametric measurement, providing comprehensive component characterization in a single instrument.

Logic analyzers capture multi-channel digital signals with timing resolution sufficient to debug high-speed buses and state machine sequences. Unlike oscilloscopes optimized for analog signal fidelity, logic analyzers sacrifice voltage resolution (threshold comparator produces binary high/low) to achieve dozens or hundreds of simultaneous channels with deep memory buffers storing millions of samples. Trigger conditions combine state machines, pattern matching, and timing constraints to capture specific events in complex digital systems. Protocol analyzers decode captured data according to standards like SPI, I2C, UART, CAN, or USB, presenting human-readable transaction logs instead of raw bit streams. For FPGA and microcontroller debugging, logic analyzers verify that state machines progress through expected sequences and that communication protocols conform to timing specifications.

### Reliability-Centered Maintenance and Predictive Replacement

Proactive component replacement before failure prevents system downtime in critical applications. Electrolytic capacitors represent the prime candidates for predictive replacement because their gradual ESR increase provides measurable warning before catastrophic failure. Establish baseline ESR measurements on critical capacitors in new equipment, then periodically re-measure. Rising ESR trends, particularly when ESR exceeds twice the original value, signal imminent failure and justify preemptive replacement. Similarly, monitor power supply output ripple voltage; increasing ripple correlates with filter capacitor degradation. Operating hour counters combined with manufacturer-specified mean time between failures (MTBF) provide statistical replacement schedules for cooling fans, batteries, and electromechanical components.

Stress testing accelerates latent failures that would eventually appear in field use. Thermal cycling between temperature extremes reveals solder joints and components vulnerable to thermal expansion mismatch. Power cycling stresses capacitors and causes transient voltage spikes that expose marginal semiconductor junctions. Extended operation at elevated voltage within component absolute maximum ratings (stress voltage testing) precipitates dielectric breakdown and junction degradation in weak devices. Mechanical vibration reveals loose connectors, inadequately staked components, and resonant structures. While stress testing intentionally pushes components toward failure, it identifies weaknesses during controlled validation rather than in uncontrolled field failures.

Redesigning failure-prone circuits prevents recurring failures and improves overall reliability. If a particular component repeatedly fails in the same circuit position across multiple units, the root cause lies in circuit design rather than component defect. Analyze power dissipation using Kirchhoff's laws and thermal models to verify the component is not chronically overstressed. Check voltage transients during switching or load changes using oscilloscope capture to reveal overvoltage spikes exceeding ratings. Inadequate bypass capacitance near switching loads causes voltage droop and ringing; adding local capacitors stabilizes supply rails. Parasitic inductance in PCB traces carrying high di/dt currents generates voltage spikes following $V = L \cdot di/dt$; reducing trace length or width lowers inductance and mitigates spikes.

Documentation of failure patterns across a population of equipment builds institutional knowledge that guides future repairs and design improvements. Maintain a failure log recording component type, failure mode, circuit position, operating hours, and environmental conditions. Statistical analysis reveals whether failures cluster around specific manufacturers, date codes, or operating conditions. Bathtub curves plotting failure rate versus time distinguish infant mortality (manufacturing defects appearing early), random failures during useful life, and wear-out failures as components approach end of life. This data informs spare parts stocking levels, preventive maintenance schedules, and warranty reserve forecasting. For original equipment manufacturers, failure data feeds design-for-reliability improvements in next-generation products, closing the loop between field experience and engineering development.
