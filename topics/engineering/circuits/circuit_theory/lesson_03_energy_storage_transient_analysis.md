# Lesson 3: Energy Storage Elements, Transient Analysis, Time Constants

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The resistor, which we examined in prior lessons, operates as a memoryless element: its voltage-current relationship obeys Ohm's law instantaneously, with no dependence on past or future states. Energy dissipated as heat in a resistor vanishes irretrievably into the thermal bath of the surrounding environment. But practical circuits demand elements capable of storing energy temporarily and releasing it on demand, enabling functions as diverse as filtering, timing, signal shaping, and power conversion. Two passive components fulfill this role: the capacitor, which stores energy in an electric field, and the inductor, which stores energy in a magnetic field. Unlike resistors, capacitors and inductors exhibit memory—their present state depends on their entire voltage or current history—and this memory manifests as transient behavior whenever circuit conditions change.

When a switch closes, a battery is connected, or a signal step arrives, circuits containing energy storage elements do not transition instantaneously to their new steady state. Instead, they undergo a transient phase during which voltages and currents evolve exponentially or oscillate toward equilibrium. The rate of this evolution is governed by the time constant, a parameter combining resistance and reactance that encapsulates the circuit's dynamic timescale. Understanding transient analysis is not merely an academic exercise; it determines switching regulator ripple, filter settling time, oscillator startup behavior, and the stability of feedback control loops. Engineers who master the differential equations and initial condition algebra governing capacitors and inductors gain the ability to predict, measure, and control circuit dynamics with precision.

The capacitor's defining relationship links its current to the time rate of change of voltage across its terminals. In mathematical form, the constitutive relation is given by $i(t) = C \frac{dv(t)}{dt}$, where $C$ denotes capacitance measured in farads. This equation reveals several invariants. First, if the voltage across a capacitor remains constant, the current must be zero; capacitors block DC in steady state. Second, an instantaneous voltage jump would demand infinite current, which physical circuits cannot supply. Therefore, the voltage across a capacitor cannot change discontinuously, establishing the continuity condition $v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-)$ at switching instants. Third, integrating the current over time yields the stored charge $Q = C v$, and the energy stored in the electric field between the capacitor's plates is $W = \frac{1}{2} C v^2$. This quadratic energy dependence explains why doubling the voltage quadruples the stored energy, a critical consideration in high-voltage capacitor bank design and electrostatic discharge protection.

The inductor operates as the magnetic dual of the capacitor. Its voltage is proportional to the time rate of change of current: $v(t) = L \frac{di(t)}{dt}$, where $L$ denotes inductance measured in henries. An inductor in steady state with constant current behaves as a short circuit, passing DC freely. Any attempt to change the current instantaneously would require infinite voltage, so the current through an inductor cannot jump discontinuously: $i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)$. The energy stored in the magnetic field surrounding the inductor is $W = \frac{1}{2} L i^2$. Practical inductors consist of wire coils, and the magnetic field intensity scales with the number of turns and the core material's permeability. Air-core inductors avoid saturation but exhibit lower inductance per unit volume; ferrite or iron-core inductors achieve high inductance but saturate when the core flux density exceeds the material's limit, causing inductance to collapse nonlinearly and current to spike.

These constitutive relations are first-order differential equations, and solving them requires specifying initial conditions—the voltage across a capacitor or current through an inductor at time $t = 0$. Initial conditions arise from the circuit's history before the switching event or external disturbance. In a circuit that has been in steady state for a long time before $t = 0$, capacitors behave as open circuits and inductors as short circuits, allowing straightforward calculation of initial stored energy. At $t = 0^+$, immediately after a switch closes or opens, these stored energies cannot vanish or appear instantaneously, enforcing continuity of $v_C$ and $i_L$. However, other voltages and currents may jump discontinuously, and recognizing which quantities are continuous and which are not is the first step in every transient analysis.

The interplay between energy storage elements and resistive dissipation determines the transient timescale. A resistor drains energy from a capacitor or inductor, converting stored field energy into heat. The faster this dissipation occurs, the shorter the transient. Conversely, large capacitance or inductance increases stored energy, prolonging the transient. This trade-off crystallizes in the time constant $\tau$, measured in seconds, which characterizes the exponential rate of decay or growth in first-order circuits. For RC circuits, $\tau = RC$; for RL circuits, $\tau = L/R$. After one time constant, a decaying exponential signal reaches approximately 37% of its initial value; after five time constants, it has decayed to less than 1%, effectively reaching steady state. Engineers exploit this predictability to set filter cutoff frequencies, design timer circuits, and synchronize digital logic with analog sensor outputs.

Second-order circuits, containing both inductors and capacitors, introduce richer dynamics. The differential equation governing an RLC circuit is second-order, admitting solutions that decay exponentially, oscillate with exponential decay, or oscillate indefinitely depending on the damping ratio. The natural frequency $\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$ sets the oscillation rate in the undamped case, while the damping factor $\zeta = \frac{R}{2} \sqrt{\frac{C}{L}}$ determines whether the response is overdamped, critically damped, or underdamped. Underdamped circuits ring at a frequency slightly below $\omega_0$, exhibiting overshoot and settling time that must be carefully controlled in high-speed analog signal paths and resonant power converters. Overdamped circuits settle slowly without ringing, suitable for applications where overshoot is forbidden. Critical damping, the boundary between these regimes, achieves the fastest settling without overshoot, a condition often targeted in control system design.

The principle of superposition, valid for linear circuits, decomposes the total response into natural response and forced response. The natural response arises from initial stored energy with all independent sources zeroed; it decays exponentially in stable circuits, revealing the circuit's inherent dynamics. The forced response is driven by external sources and persists in steady state, representing the circuit's final equilibrium. Summing these components and adjusting constants to satisfy initial conditions yields the complete solution. This decomposition is not merely a mathematical convenience; it mirrors the physical process by which a circuit's memory (initial conditions) gradually fades while external driving signals take over.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

Analyzing a first-order RC circuit begins with Kirchhoff's voltage law and the capacitor's constitutive relation. Consider a series RC circuit driven by a voltage step: a resistor $R$ in series with a capacitor $C$, connected to a voltage source $V_s$ through a switch that closes at $t = 0$. Before the switch closes, assume the capacitor is uncharged, so $v_C(0^-) = 0$. By continuity, $v_C(0^+) = 0$. Applying KVL around the loop yields $V_s = v_R(t) + v_C(t) = R i(t) + v_C(t)$. Substituting the capacitor's relation $i(t) = C \frac{dv_C(t)}{dt}$ transforms this into a first-order linear ordinary differential equation:

$$V_s = RC \frac{dv_C(t)}{dt} + v_C(t)$$

Rearranging, we obtain the canonical form:

$$\frac{dv_C(t)}{dt} + \frac{1}{RC} v_C(t) = \frac{V_s}{RC}$$

This equation expresses the capacitor voltage's rate of change as a combination of the driving term $\frac{V_s}{RC}$ and a decay term proportional to $v_C(t)$ itself. The coefficient $\frac{1}{RC}$ has units of inverse time and equals $\frac{1}{\tau}$, where $\tau = RC$ is the circuit's time constant. The homogeneous solution, found by setting the right-hand side to zero, is $v_{C,natural}(t) = A e^{-t/\tau}$, representing exponential decay. The particular solution, representing the forced response, is constant in steady state: $v_{C,forced} = V_s$. The complete solution combines these:

$$v_C(t) = V_s + A e^{-t/\tau}$$

Applying the initial condition $v_C(0) = 0$ determines the constant $A$:

$$0 = V_s + A \quad \Rightarrow \quad A = -V_s$$

Thus, the capacitor voltage evolves as:

$$v_C(t) = V_s \left( 1 - e^{-t/\tau} \right)$$

This equation describes exponential charging. At $t = 0$, the exponential term equals unity, making $v_C(0) = 0$. As $t \to \infty$, the exponential decays to zero, and $v_C(\infty) = V_s$, the final steady-state value. The current through the resistor and capacitor is found by differentiating:

$$i(t) = C \frac{dv_C(t)}{dt} = C \frac{V_s}{\tau} e^{-t/\tau} = \frac{V_s}{R} e^{-t/\tau}$$

At $t = 0^+$, the current jumps discontinuously to $\frac{V_s}{R}$, the maximum value dictated by Ohm's law with the capacitor acting momentarily as a short circuit. The current then decays exponentially with the same time constant $\tau$, approaching zero as the capacitor fully charges.

Practical verification of this behavior requires an oscilloscope, a function generator, and discrete components. Select a resistor $R = 10 \, \text{k}\Omega$ and a capacitor $C = 0.1 \, \mu\text{F}$, yielding a time constant $\tau = 1 \, \text{ms}$. Set the function generator to produce a 1 kHz square wave with 5 V amplitude, ensuring a half-period of 0.5 ms, shorter than $5\tau = 5 \, \text{ms}$ to observe incomplete charging and discharging. Connect the oscilloscope probe across the capacitor, referencing ground to the circuit's common node. The observed waveform will show exponential rise toward 5 V and exponential decay toward 0 V, with curvature governed by $\tau$. Measure the time required for the voltage to reach 63.2% of its final value (approximately 3.16 V on the rising edge); this interval equals $\tau$ and should match the calculated 1 ms within component tolerances, typically 5–10% for general-purpose resistors and capacitors.

Discharging a capacitor through a resistor follows a symmetric process. If the capacitor is initially charged to $V_0$, and at $t = 0$ the voltage source is replaced by a short circuit, KVL gives $0 = R i(t) + v_C(t)$. Substituting $i(t) = C \frac{dv_C(t)}{dt}$ yields:

$$RC \frac{dv_C(t)}{dt} + v_C(t) = 0$$

The solution is purely natural response:

$$v_C(t) = V_0 e^{-t/\tau}$$

The capacitor voltage decays exponentially from $V_0$ to zero, and the current is $i(t) = -\frac{V_0}{R} e^{-t/\tau}$, with the negative sign indicating discharge current flows opposite to the charging direction.

First-order RL circuits exhibit analogous behavior with inductor current replacing capacitor voltage as the state variable. Consider a series RL circuit where a resistor $R$ and inductor $L$ are connected to a voltage source $V_s$ through a switch closing at $t = 0$. Assume the inductor current is initially zero: $i_L(0^-) = 0$, hence $i_L(0^+) = 0$ by continuity. Applying KVL yields:

$$V_s = R i(t) + L \frac{di(t)}{dt}$$

Rearranging into standard form:

$$\frac{di(t)}{dt} + \frac{R}{L} i(t) = \frac{V_s}{L}$$

The time constant is $\tau = \frac{L}{R}$, and the solution follows the same exponential structure:

$$i(t) = \frac{V_s}{R} \left( 1 - e^{-t/\tau} \right)$$

The current rises exponentially from zero toward the steady-state value $\frac{V_s}{R}$, behaving as if the inductor were a short circuit in steady state. The voltage across the inductor is:

$$v_L(t) = L \frac{di(t)}{dt} = V_s e^{-t/\tau}$$

At $t = 0^+$, the inductor voltage equals $V_s$, appearing momentarily as an open circuit to oppose the sudden current change. As $t \to \infty$, $v_L$ decays to zero.

To measure RL transient response on the bench, construct a circuit with $R = 100 \, \Omega$ and $L = 10 \, \text{mH}$, giving $\tau = 100 \, \mu\text{s}$. Drive the circuit with a square wave at 1 kHz (1 ms period) from a function generator, and monitor the inductor voltage with an oscilloscope. The waveform will show sharp voltage spikes at each switching edge, decaying exponentially with time constant $\tau$. Beware of parasitic ringing due to stray capacitance; adding a small series resistor or using a low-capacitance probe minimizes this artifact. The inductor's DC resistance (DCR), typically a few ohms for small inductors, adds to $R$ and slightly reduces $\tau$; include DCR in calculations for accurate predictions.

Second-order RLC circuits combine the dynamics of both energy storage elements, producing differential equations of the form:

$$\frac{d^2x(t)}{dt^2} + 2\zeta\omega_0 \frac{dx(t)}{dt} + \omega_0^2 x(t) = f(t)$$

where $x(t)$ represents voltage or current, $\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$ is the natural frequency in radians per second, and $\zeta = \frac{R}{2} \sqrt{\frac{C}{L}}$ is the damping ratio. The characteristic equation $s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_0 s + \omega_0^2 = 0$ has roots:

$$s_{1,2} = -\zeta\omega_0 \pm \omega_0\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}$$

Three distinct regimes emerge based on $\zeta$. When $\zeta > 1$ (overdamped), both roots are real and negative, yielding a solution of the form $x(t) = A_1 e^{s_1 t} + A_2 e^{s_2 t}$, where both exponentials decay without oscillation. The system sluggishly approaches steady state. When $\zeta = 1$ (critically damped), the roots are repeated: $s_1 = s_2 = -\omega_0$, and the solution is $x(t) = (A_1 + A_2 t) e^{-\omega_0 t}$, representing the fastest settling without overshoot. When $\zeta < 1$ (underdamped), the roots are complex conjugates: $s_{1,2} = -\zeta\omega_0 \pm j\omega_0\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$, producing damped sinusoidal oscillations:

$$x(t) = e^{-\zeta\omega_0 t} \left( A_1 \cos(\omega_d t) + A_2 \sin(\omega_d t) \right)$$

where $\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$ is the damped natural frequency. The envelope decays exponentially with time constant $\tau = \frac{1}{\zeta\omega_0}$, while oscillations occur at $\omega_d$.

Consider a series RLC circuit driven by a voltage step $V_s$ with initial conditions $v_C(0) = 0$ and $i_L(0) = 0$. Applying KVL:

$$V_s = R i(t) + L \frac{di(t)}{dt} + v_C(t)$$

Expressing $v_C(t) = \frac{1}{C} \int i(t) dt$ and differentiating the KVL equation yields the second-order differential equation for current:

$$L \frac{d^2i(t)}{dt^2} + R \frac{di(t)}{dt} + \frac{1}{C} i(t) = 0$$

for $t > 0$ with a step input. The solution depends on the damping regime. For example, with $R = 10 \, \Omega$, $L = 1 \, \text{mH}$, and $C = 1 \, \mu\text{F}$, we compute $\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10^{-3} \cdot 10^{-6}}} = 10^{4.5} \approx 31623 \, \text{rad/s}$ and $\zeta = \frac{10}{2} \sqrt{\frac{10^{-6}}{10^{-3}}} = 5 \cdot 10^{-1.5} \approx 0.158$. Since $\zeta < 1$, the circuit is underdamped and will oscillate. The damped frequency is $\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1 - 0.158^2} \approx 31220 \, \text{rad/s}$, corresponding to approximately 4.97 kHz. The current will ring at this frequency with exponentially decaying amplitude.

To observe this on the bench, construct the RLC circuit and drive it with a pulse generator producing a single voltage step. Use a current-sense resistor (e.g., 1 $\Omega$) in series and measure the voltage drop across it with an oscilloscope to infer current. The waveform will show damped sinusoidal ringing. Increase $R$ to 100 $\Omega$ while holding $L$ and $C$ constant, recalculating $\zeta \approx 1.58 > 1$; the circuit becomes overdamped, and the current waveform transitions to a smooth exponential approach with no overshoot. Experimentally sweeping $R$ while observing the transient shape provides intuitive understanding of damping's effect.

Initial conditions play a decisive role in determining transient amplitudes and phases. When a capacitor holds charge $Q_0 = C V_0$ or an inductor carries current $I_0$ at $t = 0$, this stored energy sources or sinks current during the transient. For example, if a charged capacitor $v_C(0) = V_0$ is suddenly connected across a resistor, the discharge current at $t = 0^+$ is $i(0^+) = \frac{V_0}{R}$, and the energy $\frac{1}{2} C V_0^2$ is dissipated entirely in $R$ over time. In second-order circuits, both $x(0)$ and $\frac{dx(0)}{dt}$ must be specified to solve for the two arbitrary constants in the general solution. Calculating $\frac{dx(0)}{dt}$ often requires differentiating KVL or KCL equations and evaluating them at $t = 0^+$ using continuity conditions.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Transient analysis in high-performance circuits demands attention to parasitics, nonlinearities, and measurement artifacts that textbook idealizations neglect. Real capacitors exhibit equivalent series resistance (ESR) and equivalent series inductance (ESL), transforming a simple capacitor into an RLC network. At high frequencies, ESL dominates, causing the impedance to rise inductive rather than fall capacitive, and rendering the capacitor ineffective for decoupling or filtering. Ceramic capacitors minimize ESL but suffer from voltage-dependent capacitance; a Y5V dielectric may lose 80% of its nominal capacitance at rated voltage, drastically altering the time constant. Film capacitors maintain stable capacitance but are larger and more expensive. Electrolytic capacitors provide high capacitance but exhibit high ESR and limited high-frequency performance. Selecting the right capacitor type requires balancing capacitance, voltage rating, ESR, ESL, temperature coefficient, and physical size against the application's transient bandwidth and ripple current requirements.

Inductors face analogous challenges. Core saturation limits the maximum current an inductor can handle linearly; beyond saturation, inductance collapses, and the inductor behaves as a resistor, causing current to spike uncontrollably. Switched-mode power supplies exploit this by operating inductors in saturation for controlled energy transfer, but unintentional saturation causes failure. Core loss, comprising hysteresis and eddy current losses, dissipates energy as heat, reducing efficiency and necessitating thermal management. High-frequency inductors use ferrite cores with low permeability to minimize core loss, while power inductors use powdered iron or laminated steel cores to handle high currents. The quality factor $Q = \frac{\omega L}{R}$ quantifies inductor efficiency; low-Q inductors waste energy as heat, while high-Q inductors preserve oscillation amplitude in resonant circuits.

Nonlinear capacitance and inductance introduce distortion and unpredictability. Capacitors with ceramic dielectrics exhibit piezoelectric effects, generating voltage when mechanically stressed, and microphonics, where vibration modulates capacitance. Varactor diodes exploit voltage-dependent capacitance for tuning, but this same nonlinearity degrades filter performance in audio and RF circuits. Inductors with ferrite cores exhibit permeability that varies with flux density, temperature, and frequency, complicating analysis. Engineers mitigate nonlinearity by operating components well below their maximum ratings, selecting temperature-stable materials (C0G/NP0 dielectrics for capacitors, powdered iron for inductors), and designing circuits with feedback to linearize behavior.

Measurement of transient waveforms requires oscilloscope probes with bandwidth exceeding the fastest transient frequency components by at least 3×. A 10× passive probe typically provides 100 MHz bandwidth, adequate for microsecond-scale transients but insufficient for nanosecond switching edges. Active probes extend bandwidth to several gigahertz but introduce input capacitance and cost significantly more. Ground lead inductance in standard probe setups creates ringing artifacts on fast edges; using spring-tip ground adapters or soldering the probe tip directly to the PCB minimizes this inductance, revealing true signal fidelity. Differential probes eliminate common-mode noise and ground loops, critical when measuring floating nodes or high-side switching signals in power converters.

Triggering and acquisition settings determine whether the oscilloscope captures the transient accurately. Single-shot triggering captures non-repetitive events, but requires sufficient sampling rate; at least 10 samples per rise time ensures reconstruction fidelity. Aliasing occurs when the transient bandwidth exceeds half the sampling rate, folding high-frequency content into the measured signal and producing spurious waveforms. Bandwidth limiting in the oscilloscope's input amplifier, set to just above the expected signal bandwidth, suppresses high-frequency noise without attenuating the signal. Persistence mode overlays multiple acquisitions, revealing jitter and noise statistics obscured in single-sweep captures.

Simulation complements measurement but introduces numerical errors if not configured properly. SPICE transient analysis requires specifying maximum timestep to resolve the fastest circuit dynamics; a timestep larger than one-tenth the smallest time constant produces inaccurate results. Convergence failures occur when the solver cannot satisfy Kirchhoff's laws to the specified tolerance, often due to nearly ideal components or floating nodes. Adding small parasitic resistances (e.g., 1 mΩ in series with inductors, 1 MΩ across capacitors) stabilizes convergence without significantly altering results. Initial condition statements in the SPICE netlist, such as `.ic V(node)=5` or `.ic I(L1)=1A`, enforce initial capacitor voltages and inductor currents, matching physical circuit behavior at $t = 0$.

Failure modes in transient operation arise from exceeding component ratings or neglecting parasitics. Inrush current during capacitor charging can exceed a power supply's current limit or damage diodes and switches; series resistance or active current limiting mitigates this. Voltage overshoot in underdamped RLC circuits may exceed component voltage ratings, causing dielectric breakdown in capacitors or avalanche breakdown in semiconductors. Snubber circuits, comprising an RC series branch in parallel with inductive loads, clamp voltage spikes and damp ringing. Shoot-through in half-bridge converters, where both switches conduct simultaneously during transient switching, creates a direct short across the supply, destroying MOSFETs within microseconds. Dead time, a brief interval where both switches are off, prevents shoot-through but introduces voltage distortion and requires careful timing design.

Energy recovery circuits exploit transient dynamics to improve efficiency. Resonant converters operate inductors and capacitors in oscillation, transferring energy with minimal resistive loss. Zero-voltage switching (ZVS) and zero-current switching (ZCS) techniques time switch transitions to coincide with zero voltage or current instants, eliminating switching loss. These techniques require precise transient analysis to ensure resonance occurs at the correct phase and frequency. Parasitic elements, rather than being liabilities, are incorporated into resonant tank design, transforming parasitics into functional circuit elements.

Transient thermal analysis extends electrical transient concepts to heat flow. A junction-to-ambient thermal resistance $R_{thJA}$ acts like electrical resistance, while thermal capacitance of the die and package stores heat analogous to a capacitor storing charge. A power pulse dissipates energy in the junction, raising its temperature exponentially toward a steady-state value governed by the thermal time constant $\tau_{th} = R_{thJA} C_{th}$. Exceeding the junction's maximum temperature rating, even transiently, degrades semiconductor performance or causes immediate failure. Pulsed operation exploits thermal mass to permit higher peak power than steady-state ratings allow, provided the duty cycle and repetition rate keep the average junction temperature within limits. Thermal simulation tools like finite-element analysis (FEA) model heat diffusion through complex geometries, predicting hotspots and optimizing heat sink placement.

Control loop stability in circuits with energy storage depends critically on phase margin and gain margin derived from transient response. A feedback amplifier with insufficient phase margin oscillates or rings when subjected to a step input. Compensating the loop with additional RC networks introduces poles and zeros that shape the open-loop transfer function, increasing phase margin and damping transient ringing. Bode plot analysis in the frequency domain reveals phase margin, but time-domain step response provides direct validation of stability. A well-compensated loop settles within a few cycles with minimal overshoot; a marginally stable loop rings excessively, and an unstable loop oscillates indefinitely.

Transient immunity in digital circuits depends on supply decoupling capacitors placed near IC power pins. A microcontroller drawing pulsed load current during instruction fetch creates voltage transients on the supply rail; inadequate decoupling allows these transients to corrupt logic levels or reset the processor. Effective decoupling requires multiple capacitor values in parallel: bulk capacitors (10–100 µF electrolytic) store energy for low-frequency transients, ceramic capacitors (0.1–1 µF) handle mid-frequency content, and small ceramics (10–100 pF) suppress high-frequency noise. Each capacitor's series resonance should cover a different frequency band, forming a low-impedance path over the full transient spectrum. Placement matters; inductance in PCB traces adds series impedance, so capacitors must be within millimeters of the IC pins, with vias directly to ground and power planes minimizing loop inductance.

Electromagnetic interference (EMI) from transient currents couples into adjacent circuits through magnetic and electric fields. A fast current transient in an inductor generates a time-varying magnetic field, inducing voltage in nearby loops by Faraday's law. Shielding with ferrite beads, magnetic shielding enclosures, or twisted-pair wiring reduces radiated coupling. Capacitive coupling occurs when a fast voltage transient on one trace injects current into an adjacent trace through parasitic capacitance. Guard traces, ground planes, and increased trace spacing attenuate capacitive coupling. Measuring EMI requires a spectrum analyzer and near-field probes to map radiated emissions, and injecting transients via conducted immunity testing validates circuit robustness against external disturbances.

Ultimately, mastering transient analysis equips the engineer to design circuits that behave predictably, efficiently, and reliably under dynamic conditions. The exponential rise and decay of voltages and currents in first-order circuits, the ringing and damping of second-order responses, and the interplay of initial conditions with external forcing all follow from the fundamental constitutive relations of capacitors and inductors. Translating differential equations into hardware requires attention to component selection, layout, measurement technique, and failure mode mitigation. The time constant remains the central figure of merit, compressing resistance, capacitance, and inductance into a single parameter that governs circuit speed and sets the timescale for transient phenomena across analog and digital domains alike.
