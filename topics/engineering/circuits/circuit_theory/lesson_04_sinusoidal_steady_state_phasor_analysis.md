# Lesson 4: Sinusoidal Steady State, Phasor Analysis, Impedance

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

When we transition from the resistive domain—where Ohm's law reigns supreme and voltage-current relationships remain instantaneously proportional—into circuits containing inductors and capacitors driven by sinusoidal sources, we encounter a profound complication. The differential equations governing energy storage elements transform our familiar algebraic circuit analysis into a calculus problem. An inductor's voltage depends on the time derivative of current, while a capacitor's current depends on the time derivative of voltage. For a voltage source $v_s(t) = V_m \cos(\omega t + \phi)$ driving an RL or RC network, solving for the steady-state current demands integrating or differentiating trigonometric functions, managing phase shifts, and reconciling superposition of sinusoids with different phases—a tedious, error-prone process that scales poorly as circuit complexity grows.

The phasor transform, introduced by Charles Proteus Steinmetz in the 1890s during the War of Currents, provides an algebraic escape from this differential equation quagmire. Steinmetz recognized that sinusoidal steady-state analysis—where all sources oscillate at a single angular frequency $\omega$ and transients have decayed to negligibility—permits a one-to-one mapping between time-domain sinusoids and complex numbers in the frequency domain. A sinusoid $v(t) = V_m \cos(\omega t + \theta)$ with amplitude $V_m$ and phase $\theta$ transforms into the phasor $\mathbf{V} = V_m e^{j\theta} = V_m \angle \theta$, where $j = \sqrt{-1}$. This transformation converts differential equations into algebraic equations involving complex numbers, collapsing time-varying calculus into straightforward complex arithmetic. Kirchhoff's voltage and current laws, node-voltage and mesh-current methods, Thévenin and Norton theorems—all techniques from resistive circuit analysis—carry forward unchanged in form, operating now on phasors instead of real-valued DC quantities.

The fundamental invariant underpinning phasor analysis is the **uniqueness of the steady-state frequency**. Every voltage and current in the circuit oscillates at the same angular frequency $\omega$ as the driving source; only amplitudes and phases differ. This frequency-domain representation suppresses the explicit time dependence $e^{j\omega t}$, which factors out of every term in Kirchhoff's equations. When we write $\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{I} \mathbf{Z}$, we mean that the phasor voltage across an element equals the phasor current through it multiplied by a complex impedance $\mathbf{Z}$, which encodes both magnitude scaling and phase shift. For a resistor, $\mathbf{Z}_R = R$, a real number indicating voltage and current remain in phase. For an inductor, $\mathbf{Z}_L = j\omega L$, a purely imaginary quantity revealing that voltage leads current by ninety degrees. For a capacitor, $\mathbf{Z}_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C} = -\frac{j}{\omega C}$, indicating voltage lags current by ninety degrees.

These impedances emerge naturally from the element laws. Consider an inductor with $v(t) = L \frac{di(t)}{dt}$. Let $i(t) = I_m \cos(\omega t + \phi_i)$, which transforms to the phasor $\mathbf{I} = I_m e^{j\phi_i}$. The time derivative becomes $\frac{di}{dt} = -\omega I_m \sin(\omega t + \phi_i) = \omega I_m \cos(\omega t + \phi_i + 90°)$, so $v(t) = \omega L I_m \cos(\omega t + \phi_i + 90°)$, yielding the phasor $\mathbf{V} = \omega L I_m e^{j(\phi_i + 90°)} = j\omega L I_m e^{j\phi_i} = j\omega L \mathbf{I}$. The operator $j$ in the frequency domain corresponds to differentiation followed by a ninety-degree phase advance in the time domain. Similarly, for a capacitor with $i(t) = C \frac{dv(t)}{dt}$, inverting yields $\mathbf{V} = \frac{1}{j\omega C} \mathbf{I}$. These impedance expressions are frequency-dependent: inductive reactance $X_L = \omega L$ grows with frequency, making inductors appear as short circuits at DC and open circuits at infinite frequency; capacitive reactance $X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C}$ shrinks with frequency, making capacitors open circuits at DC and short circuits at high frequency.

The physical interpretation of impedance magnitude and angle provides deep insight into energy flow. Resistors dissipate power irreversibly as heat; their impedance is real, and voltage and current align in phase, so instantaneous power $p(t) = v(t)i(t)$ averages to $P_{avg} = \frac{1}{2}V_m I_m$ over a cycle. Inductors and capacitors store and release energy cyclically without net dissipation in ideal form; their impedances are purely imaginary, and voltage and current remain ninety degrees out of phase, so average power is zero despite non-zero instantaneous power oscillations. In real circuits combining resistance, inductance, and capacitance, the impedance $\mathbf{Z} = R + jX$ contains a resistive component $R$ governing average power dissipation and a reactive component $X$ (positive for net inductance, negative for net capacitance) governing energy storage oscillations. The impedance angle $\theta_Z = \arctan(X/R)$ equals the phase difference between voltage and current phasors: $\mathbf{V} = V_m \angle \theta_v$ and $\mathbf{I} = I_m \angle \theta_i$ yield $\theta_Z = \theta_v - \theta_i$.

Root-mean-square (RMS) values provide the bridge between sinusoidal AC quantities and equivalent DC power dissipation. For a sinusoid $v(t) = V_m \cos(\omega t)$, the RMS value is $V_{rms} = \frac{V_m}{\sqrt{2}}$, defined such that a resistor dissipates the same average power whether subjected to $V_m \cos(\omega t)$ or a DC voltage $V_{rms}$. This equivalence stems from the time-averaged square of the cosine: $\langle \cos^2(\omega t) \rangle = \frac{1}{2}$. Power utilities worldwide specify voltages in RMS: North American residential "120 V AC" means $V_{rms} = 120$ V, corresponding to a peak amplitude $V_m = 120\sqrt{2} \approx 170$ V. RMS arithmetic simplifies power calculations: if $V_{rms}$ appears across an impedance $|\mathbf{Z}|$ with current $I_{rms}$, then apparent power $S = V_{rms} I_{rms}$ in volt-amperes (VA), average (real) power $P = V_{rms} I_{rms} \cos\theta$ in watts (W), and reactive power $Q = V_{rms} I_{rms} \sin\theta$ in volt-amperes reactive (VAR), where $\theta$ is the impedance angle. The power factor $\mathrm{PF} = \cos\theta$ quantifies the fraction of apparent power that performs useful work; purely resistive loads achieve $\mathrm{PF} = 1$, while purely reactive loads yield $\mathrm{PF} = 0$.

Complex power $\mathbf{S} = P + jQ$ unifies these quantities into a single phasor representation, where $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{V} \mathbf{I}^*$ and the asterisk denotes complex conjugation. This conjugation flips the sign of the current's phase, aligning the computation with the IEEE standard convention that lagging currents (inductive loads) produce positive reactive power. The magnitude $|\mathbf{S}| = V_{rms} I_{rms}$ equals apparent power, the real part $\Re\{\mathbf{S}\} = P$ equals average power, and the imaginary part $\Im\{\mathbf{S}\} = Q$ equals reactive power. On the complex plane, plotting $\mathbf{S}$ produces a right triangle with hypotenuse $S$, horizontal leg $P$, and vertical leg $Q$, with angle $\theta$ measuring the impedance angle. Power factor correction—installing capacitor banks to offset inductive loads in industrial facilities—minimizes $Q$, reducing $S$ for fixed $P$ and thereby lowering transmission losses and utility penalties for poor power factor.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The phasor transformation operates as a linear, invertible mapping between the time domain and frequency domain, valid exclusively for sinusoidal steady state at a single frequency. Given a real-valued time-domain signal $v(t) = V_m \cos(\omega t + \theta)$, we construct the analytic signal $\tilde{v}(t) = V_m e^{j(\omega t + \theta)} = V_m e^{j\theta} e^{j\omega t}$ by invoking Euler's formula $e^{j\alpha} = \cos\alpha + j\sin\alpha$. The time-domain sinusoid is the real part: $v(t) = \Re\{\tilde{v}(t)\}$. The phasor $\mathbf{V}$ is defined as the complex amplitude with the time-varying carrier $e^{j\omega t}$ suppressed: $\mathbf{V} = V_m e^{j\theta} = V_m \angle \theta$. Converting back to the time domain requires restoring the carrier and taking the real part: $v(t) = \Re\{\mathbf{V} e^{j\omega t}\}$.

Phasors obey superposition and linearity. If two sinusoidal voltages at the same frequency $\omega$ combine—say, $v_1(t) = V_{m1}\cos(\omega t + \theta_1)$ and $v_2(t) = V_{m2}\cos(\omega t + \theta_2)$—then the sum $v(t) = v_1(t) + v_2(t)$ corresponds to the phasor sum $\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{V}_1 + \mathbf{V}_2 = V_{m1}e^{j\theta_1} + V_{m2}e^{j\theta_2}$. Complex addition handles amplitude and phase composition automatically, eliminating the trigonometric identities required in the time domain. Scalar multiplication and differentiation also translate cleanly: multiplying a time-domain sinusoid by a constant $k$ corresponds to multiplying the phasor by $k$; differentiating $v(t)$ with respect to time corresponds to multiplying the phasor by $j\omega$.

Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL) states that the sum of voltages around a closed loop equals zero. In the time domain, this becomes $\sum_k v_k(t) = 0$. Transforming to phasors, $\sum_k \Re\{\mathbf{V}_k e^{j\omega t}\} = 0$ implies $\Re\{e^{j\omega t} \sum_k \mathbf{V}_k\} = 0$ for all $t$, which holds if and only if $\sum_k \mathbf{V}_k = 0$. KVL thus applies directly to phasors. Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) follows identically: $\sum_k \mathbf{I}_k = 0$ at any node. These laws permit node-voltage and mesh-current analysis in the phasor domain using the same matrix formulations developed for resistive circuits, substituting complex impedances for resistances.

Consider a series RLC circuit driven by a voltage source $v_s(t) = V_m \cos(\omega t)$, corresponding to phasor $\mathbf{V}_s = V_m \angle 0°$. The series impedance is $\mathbf{Z}_{total} = R + j\omega L + \frac{1}{j\omega C} = R + j(\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}) = R + jX$, where $X = X_L - X_C$ is the net reactance. The current phasor is $\mathbf{I} = \frac{\mathbf{V}_s}{\mathbf{Z}_{total}} = \frac{V_m}{R + jX}$. Converting to polar form, $\mathbf{Z}_{total} = |\mathbf{Z}| \angle \theta_Z$, where $|\mathbf{Z}| = \sqrt{R^2 + X^2}$ and $\theta_Z = \arctan(X/R)$. Thus $\mathbf{I} = \frac{V_m}{|\mathbf{Z}|} \angle -\theta_Z$, revealing that current magnitude is $I_m = \frac{V_m}{|\mathbf{Z}|}$ and current lags voltage by $\theta_Z$ degrees if $X > 0$ (inductive) or leads if $X < 0$ (capacitive). At resonance, $\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$, the reactance vanishes ($X = 0$), impedance becomes purely resistive ($\mathbf{Z}_{total} = R$), and current reaches maximum magnitude $I_m = \frac{V_m}{R}$ in phase with voltage.

Voltage division and current division extend to the phasor domain. For impedances $\mathbf{Z}_1$ and $\mathbf{Z}_2$ in series with total voltage $\mathbf{V}$, the voltage across $\mathbf{Z}_1$ is $\mathbf{V}_1 = \mathbf{V} \frac{\mathbf{Z}_1}{\mathbf{Z}_1 + \mathbf{Z}_2}$. For impedances in parallel with total current $\mathbf{I}$, the current through $\mathbf{Z}_1$ is $\mathbf{I}_1 = \mathbf{I} \frac{\mathbf{Z}_2}{\mathbf{Z}_1 + \mathbf{Z}_2}$. These formulas involve complex division, which requires converting to polar form: $\frac{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}} = \frac{|A|}{|B|} \angle (\theta_A - \theta_B)$.

Admittance $\mathbf{Y} = \frac{1}{\mathbf{Z}} = G + jB$ provides the dual representation, where $G$ is conductance and $B$ is susceptance. For a resistor, $\mathbf{Y}_R = \frac{1}{R}$; for an inductor, $\mathbf{Y}_L = \frac{1}{j\omega L} = -\frac{j}{\omega L}$; for a capacitor, $\mathbf{Y}_C = j\omega C$. Admittances add in parallel just as impedances add in series, simplifying parallel circuit analysis. For example, a parallel RLC circuit driven by current source $\mathbf{I}_s$ has total admittance $\mathbf{Y}_{total} = \frac{1}{R} + j(\omega C - \frac{1}{\omega L})$ and voltage $\mathbf{V} = \frac{\mathbf{I}_s}{\mathbf{Y}_{total}}$.

Power calculations in the phasor domain leverage RMS phasors and complex conjugation. Define RMS phasors as $\mathbf{V}_{rms} = \frac{\mathbf{V}_m}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $\mathbf{I}_{rms} = \frac{\mathbf{I}_m}{\sqrt{2}}$, where $\mathbf{V}_m$ and $\mathbf{I}_m$ are peak-amplitude phasors. Complex power is $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{V}_{rms} \mathbf{I}_{rms}^*$. Expanding in rectangular form with $\mathbf{V}_{rms} = V_{rms} \angle \theta_v$ and $\mathbf{I}_{rms} = I_{rms} \angle \theta_i$, we obtain $\mathbf{S} = V_{rms} I_{rms} \angle (\theta_v - \theta_i) = V_{rms} I_{rms} \angle \theta_Z$, where $\theta_Z$ is the impedance angle. The real part $P = V_{rms} I_{rms} \cos\theta_Z$ represents average power dissipated in resistances, while the imaginary part $Q = V_{rms} I_{rms} \sin\theta_Z$ represents reactive power exchanged with inductances and capacitances. A positive $Q$ indicates net inductive behavior (current lags voltage), consuming reactive power; negative $Q$ indicates net capacitive behavior (current leads voltage), supplying reactive power.

Instantaneous power $p(t) = v(t) i(t)$ oscillates at twice the source frequency. For $v(t) = V_m \cos(\omega t + \theta_v)$ and $i(t) = I_m \cos(\omega t + \theta_i)$, applying the product-to-sum identity yields $p(t) = \frac{V_m I_m}{2} [\cos(\theta_v - \theta_i) + \cos(2\omega t + \theta_v + \theta_i)]$. The first term is constant, equaling average power $P = \frac{V_m I_m}{2} \cos\theta_Z = V_{rms} I_{rms} \cos\theta_Z$. The second term oscillates at $2\omega$, representing the bidirectional energy flow between source and reactive elements; its time average is zero. Oscilloscope measurements capturing voltage and current waveforms reveal this double-frequency pulsation in instantaneous power, most pronounced in circuits with poor power factor where $\theta_Z$ approaches ninety degrees.

For a parallel RC circuit driven by a current source, admittance $\mathbf{Y} = \frac{1}{R} + j\omega C$ governs the voltage response $\mathbf{V} = \frac{\mathbf{I}_s}{\mathbf{Y}}$, and the voltage leads the source current by angle $\arctan(\omega RC)$.

Node-voltage analysis in the phasor domain proceeds identically to DC analysis. Label each non-reference node with a phasor voltage $\mathbf{V}_1, \mathbf{V}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{V}_n$. Apply KCL at each node, expressing branch currents as $\mathbf{I} = \frac{\mathbf{V}_a - \mathbf{V}_b}{\mathbf{Z}}$ for impedance $\mathbf{Z}$ connecting nodes $a$ and $b$. Collect terms into a matrix equation $\mathbf{Y} \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{i}_s$, where $\mathbf{Y}$ is the node admittance matrix (complex-valued), $\mathbf{v}$ is the vector of unknown node voltages, and $\mathbf{i}_s$ is the vector of source currents. Solve via Gaussian elimination or matrix inversion, handling complex arithmetic throughout. Mesh-current analysis follows similarly, formulating $\mathbf{Z} \mathbf{i} = \mathbf{v}_s$ with the mesh impedance matrix $\mathbf{Z}$ and source voltages $\mathbf{v}_s$.

Thévenin and Norton equivalent circuits apply unchanged: any linear network of impedances and sources reduces to a single voltage source $\mathbf{V}_{th}$ in series with impedance $\mathbf{Z}_{th}$, or equivalently a current source $\mathbf{I}_{N} = \frac{\mathbf{V}_{th}}{\mathbf{Z}_{th}}$ in parallel with $\mathbf{Z}_{th}$. To compute $\mathbf{V}_{th}$, measure the open-circuit voltage phasor at the terminals. To compute $\mathbf{Z}_{th}$, deactivate independent sources (replace voltage sources with short circuits, current sources with open circuits) and calculate the impedance looking into the terminals. Maximum power transfer occurs when the load impedance equals the complex conjugate of the source impedance: $\mathbf{Z}_{load} = \mathbf{Z}_{th}^*$. This conjugate matching ensures resistive components align while reactive components cancel, achieving unity power factor and maximizing real power delivery.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Phasor analysis achieves computational efficiency by reducing differential equations to algebraic manipulations, but the practitioner must guard against systematic errors and domain violations that undermine accuracy. The most common failure mode is **multi-frequency contamination**: phasor methods assume all sources share a single angular frequency $\omega$. If a circuit contains sources at $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$, superposition requires solving two separate phasor problems—one at each frequency—and summing the time-domain results. Attempting to mix phasors at different frequencies within a single calculation produces nonsensical results because the suppressed carrier $e^{j\omega t}$ differs across terms. Nonlinear elements such as diodes, transistors, and saturable inductors generate harmonics and intermodulation products, invalidating phasor linearity assumptions; time-domain simulation or harmonic balance methods become necessary.

Numerical precision in complex arithmetic demands attention to **catastrophic cancellation** when subtracting nearly equal complex numbers or computing impedances near resonance. At series RLC resonance, $X_L = X_C$ and $X = X_L - X_C \approx 0$, so impedance magnitude $|\mathbf{Z}| = \sqrt{R^2 + X^2} \approx R$. Finite-precision arithmetic can yield spurious phase angles $\theta_Z = \arctan(X/R)$ when $X$ results from subtracting large, nearly equal reactances. Mitigate this by maintaining high precision (double-precision floating point minimum) and restructuring calculations to avoid differencing operations. For narrow-bandwidth filters, compute the quality factor $Q = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R} = \frac{1}{\omega_0 RC}$ and express impedance near resonance as $\mathbf{Z} \approx R(1 + j2Q\frac{\Delta\omega}{\omega_0})$, where $\Delta\omega = \omega - \omega_0$, which avoids direct subtraction.

Power factor correction in industrial settings requires careful **reactive power budgeting** to avoid overcompensation. Installing excessive capacitance to offset inductive loads can drive the power factor leading (capacitive), attracting utility penalties and causing voltage rise issues. The optimal correction targets $\mathrm{PF} \approx 0.95$ lagging rather than unity, accommodating load variations without crossing into leading territory. Switched capacitor banks with multiple stages provide granular control, activating or deactivating capacitor groups based on real-time power factor measurements. Tuning reactors (series inductors) prevent harmonic resonance between capacitor banks and grid inductance at frequencies coinciding with nonlinear load harmonics, which can amplify harmonic distortion and trigger protective relay tripping.

**Parasitic impedances** degrade idealized phasor models at higher frequencies. Real inductors exhibit series resistance (winding losses) and parallel capacitance (interwinding coupling), transforming the ideal $\mathbf{Z}_L = j\omega L$ into $\mathbf{Z}_L = R_s + j\omega L \parallel \frac{1}{j\omega C_p}$. This parasitic network produces self-resonance at $f_{SRF} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC_p}}$, above which the inductor behaves capacitively. Real capacitors exhibit equivalent series resistance (ESR) and equivalent series inductance (ESL), modifying $\mathbf{Z}_C$ into $\mathbf{Z}_C = R_{ESR} + j\omega L_{ESL} + \frac{1}{j\omega C}$. ESR dominates at low frequencies, causing real power dissipation and heating; ESL dominates at high frequencies, degrading decoupling effectiveness in power distribution networks. Datasheet specifications list these parasitics; accurate high-frequency circuit models incorporate them via SPICE subcircuits combining ideal components.

**Phase measurement precision** on oscilloscopes and vector network analyzers limits power factor determination accuracy. A four-channel oscilloscope measuring voltage and current with 1% amplitude error and 1° phase error propagates into power calculations as $P = V_{rms} I_{rms} \cos\theta \pm \Delta P$, where $\Delta P$ depends on both amplitude and phase uncertainties. At high power factors ($\theta_Z \approx 0°$), phase error dominates; at low power factors ($\theta_Z \approx 90°$), amplitude error dominates. Calibrate instruments against precision resistive loads where $\mathrm{PF} = 1$ is known, and use differential voltage probes to eliminate ground loop errors that introduce spurious phase shifts. For power measurements, dedicated power analyzers employing synchronous sampling of voltage and current at megasample rates achieve sub-0.1° phase accuracy and 0.1% power accuracy, essential for characterizing switch-mode power supplies and motor drives.

**Transformer equivalent circuits** in phasor analysis require careful treatment of magnetizing inductance $L_m$ and leakage inductances $L_{leak,p}$, $L_{leak,s}$. The ideal transformer model $\frac{\mathbf{V}_s}{\mathbf{V}_p} = \frac{1}{n}$ and $\frac{\mathbf{I}_s}{\mathbf{I}_p} = -n$ (where $n$ is the turns ratio) neglects these parasitics. A realistic model places $L_m$ in parallel with the primary, representing core magnetization current, and leakage inductances in series with primary and secondary windings, representing flux not linking both windings. At line frequencies (50 Hz or 60 Hz), magnetizing current remains small (1–5% of rated current), and leakage reactance remains low (3–10% of winding impedance), so the ideal model suffices. At kilohertz frequencies in switch-mode transformers, leakage inductance becomes dominant, storing energy released during switching transitions and necessitating snubber circuits or active clamp topologies to prevent voltage spikes.

**Frequency scaling and impedance normalization** streamline hand calculations and enable dimensionless filter design. Normalize impedance by a reference resistance $R_0$ (often 50 Ω or 75 Ω in RF contexts, or the load resistance in filter design), defining $\mathbf{z} = \frac{\mathbf{Z}}{R_0}$. Normalize frequency by a reference frequency $\omega_0$ (often the cutoff or resonance frequency), defining $\Omega = \frac{\omega}{\omega_0}$. In these normalized coordinates, inductor impedance becomes $\mathbf{z}_L = j\Omega \frac{\omega_0 L}{R_0} = j\Omega l$, where $l = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R_0}$ is the normalized inductance. Similarly, $\mathbf{z}_C = -\frac{j}{\Omega c}$, where $c = \omega_0 C R_0$ is the normalized capacitance. Prototype filter designs developed in normalized coordinates translate to arbitrary frequencies and impedances via denormalization: $L = \frac{l R_0}{\omega_0}$ and $C = \frac{c}{\omega_0 R_0}$. This technique, standard in RF filter synthesis (Butterworth, Chebyshev, elliptic designs), separates the mathematical filter topology from the physical implementation.

**Time-domain verification** via inverse phasor transformation confirms steady-state solutions and exposes transient behavior ignored by phasor analysis. Given phasor solution $\mathbf{I} = I_m \angle \theta_i$, reconstruct the time-domain current as $i(t) = I_m \cos(\omega t + \theta_i) = \Re\{I_m e^{j\theta_i} e^{j\omega t}\}$. Overlay oscilloscope measurements with predicted waveforms to validate model accuracy. Discrepancies indicate unmodeled parasitics, measurement errors, or departure from sinusoidal steady state (harmonic distortion, transients). SPICE transient simulations provide ground truth: apply the sinusoidal source, simulate for multiple cycles until transients decay, then extract amplitude and phase from the final steady-state cycles via Fourier analysis. Compare SPICE-derived phasors against hand calculations; agreement within 1% confirms model fidelity.

**Harmonic distortion** quantified by total harmonic distortion (THD) measures deviation from sinusoidal purity. For voltage $v(t) = V_1 \cos(\omega t + \theta_1) + \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} V_n \cos(n\omega t + \theta_n)$, THD is $\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{n=2}^{\infty} V_n^2}}{V_1}$, often expressed as a percentage. Phasor analysis applies only to the fundamental component ($n=1$); harmonics require separate phasor solutions at each harmonic frequency $n\omega$ and time-domain superposition. Switching power converters, rectifiers, and magnetic saturation generate harmonics that propagate through the power grid, causing transformer overheating, neutral conductor overloading in three-phase systems, and interference with communication circuits. IEEE Standard 519 limits harmonic current injection based on load size and grid strength, enforcing THD below 5% at the point of common coupling for most industrial installations.

**Resonance in RLC circuits** amplifies voltages and currents to dangerous levels if unrecognized. In a series RLC circuit at resonance ($\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$), impedance magnitude drops to $R$, and current magnitude peaks at $I_m = \frac{V_m}{R}$. The voltage across the inductor is $V_L = \omega_0 L I_m = \frac{V_m \omega_0 L}{R} = V_m Q$, where $Q = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R}$ is the quality factor. For high-Q circuits ($Q \geq 10$), inductor and capacitor voltages can exceed the source voltage by an order of magnitude or more, stressing insulation and triggering arcing. Parallel RLC resonance similarly amplifies current in the reactive branches. Circuit designers exploit resonance in tuned RF amplifiers, oscillators, and filters, but must derate component voltage and current ratings accordingly. Damping resistors reduce $Q$ at the cost of insertion loss, trading peak response for stability and reduced overshoot in transient response.

**Three-phase AC analysis** extends single-phase phasor techniques to balanced and unbalanced three-phase systems. In a balanced three-phase system, voltages $\mathbf{V}_a$, $\mathbf{V}_b = \mathbf{V}_a e^{-j2\pi/3}$, and $\mathbf{V}_c = \mathbf{V}_a e^{j2\pi/3}$ form a symmetric set with $\mathbf{V}_a + \mathbf{V}_b + \mathbf{V}_c = 0$. Phase currents sum to zero in a balanced wye connection, eliminating neutral current. Line-to-line voltages in delta configuration lead line-to-neutral voltages by thirty degrees and have magnitude $\sqrt{3}$ times larger. Total three-phase real power is $P_{3\phi} = 3 V_{ph} I_{ph} \cos\theta = \sqrt{3} V_{LL} I_L \cos\theta$, where $V_{ph}$ is phase voltage, $V_{LL}$ is line-to-line voltage, $I_{ph}$ is phase current, $I_L$ is line current, and $\theta$ is the impedance angle. Unbalanced loads require analyzing each phase independently, constructing a three-node admittance matrix, and solving for phase voltages and currents, accounting for neutral impedance if present. Symmetrical components (positive, negative, zero sequence) decompose unbalanced phasors into balanced sets, simplifying fault analysis in power systems.
