# Operational Verification Labs: Power Electronics

## Objective & Operational Lab Setup

Power electronics represents the practical discipline of controlling and converting electrical energy using semiconductor switching devices operating in their saturation and cutoff regions rather than linear modes. This operational verification sequence guides practitioners through the physical construction, measurement, and empirical characterization of switching converters, magnetics design, and protection circuitry. Unlike linear power supplies that dissipate excess energy as heat through series-pass transistors operating in their active region, switching topologies achieve high efficiency by rapidly toggling power semiconductors between fully-on and fully-off states, with the average output voltage or current controlled through duty cycle modulation.

The laboratory exercises demand hands-on construction of buck, boost, and flyback converter circuits on perforated prototyping board, followed by systematic measurement of switching waveforms, efficiency curves, and transient load response using oscilloscopes, electronic loads, and thermal imaging. Each exercise builds operational competence through iterative design refinement, starting with open-loop pulse-width modulation generators, progressing through closed-loop feedback compensation, and culminating in stress testing under worst-case input voltage transients and output short-circuit faults. The practitioner will physically wind toroidal inductors and transformers, select gate drive resistors and snubber networks through measured ringing suppression, and debug switching noise coupling paths using near-field probes and spectrum analyzers.

The analytical framework underlying these exercises combines time-domain switching analysis, small-signal AC modeling for loop stability assessment, and thermal circuit representations for heatsink sizing. Practitioners must become fluent in three complementary analysis domains: the large-signal averaged model that predicts steady-state conversion ratios and conduction losses; the small-signal linearized model that determines control loop bandwidth and phase margin; and the parasitic circuit model that quantifies electromagnetic interference, voltage overshoot from leakage inductance, and reverse recovery losses in rectifier diodes. This triad of modeling perspectives ensures that laboratory measurements can be predicted, explained, and optimized through engineering first principles rather than trial-and-error component substitution.

### Laboratory Equipment and Component Inventory

The power electronics workbench requires a four-channel digital oscilloscope with minimum 100 MHz bandwidth, 1 GSa/s sampling rate, and isolated differential voltage probes rated for 1000 V common-mode rejection. High-side gate drive measurements demand galvanic isolation to prevent ground loop currents from corrupting switching node waveforms. A current probe with 50 MHz bandwidth and 30 A continuous rating captures inductor current ripple and diode reverse recovery transients. The oscilloscope's FFT function provides frequency-domain analysis of switching harmonics and conducted emission spectra without requiring a dedicated spectrum analyzer for initial debugging.

Bench power supplies must deliver 0-50 VDC with 5 A continuous current and adjustable current limiting for short-circuit protection during bring-up testing. A programmable electronic load capable of constant-current, constant-resistance, and pulsed load modes enables dynamic load transient testing with rise times below 1 microsecond. Digital multimeters with true RMS measurement and 0.1% accuracy characterize input and output power for efficiency calculations. Thermal measurement employs both contact thermocouples with 0.1°C resolution and non-contact infrared cameras that reveal thermal gradients across power semiconductor junctions, magnetic cores, and PCB copper planes.

Component selection for switching converters prioritizes devices optimized for hard-switching topologies rather than linear regulators. Power MOSFETs require low on-resistance RDS(on) for conduction loss minimization and low gate charge Qg for reduced switching loss. The figure of merit RDS(on) × Qg guides optimal device selection at a given voltage rating. Schottky barrier rectifiers provide fast recovery times below 20 nanoseconds and low forward voltage drop of 0.3-0.5 V, critical for high-frequency operation above 100 kHz where diode reverse recovery would otherwise dominate switching loss. Magnetic cores utilize ferrite materials with permeability μ ranging from 2000-3000 and saturation flux density Bsat of 300-400 mT, providing the necessary inductance density while avoiding core saturation at peak currents.

Gate driver integrated circuits must source and sink 1-2 A peak current to charge and discharge MOSFET gate capacitance within 10-20 nanoseconds, ensuring clean switching transitions that minimize the overlap time when both voltage and current are simultaneously high. Optocouplers or isolated gate drivers using transformer coupling provide galvanic isolation for high-side switches in half-bridge and full-bridge topologies. Ceramic capacitors with X7R or X5R dielectric characteristics serve as low-ESR input and output filter capacitors, handling high ripple currents at switching frequencies. Aluminum electrolytic capacitors provide bulk energy storage but require parallel ceramic bypassing due to their inductive series impedance at high frequencies.

The prototyping platform consists of copper-clad perforated board with 0.1-inch hole spacing, allowing point-to-point wiring with 18-22 AWG solid copper wire for power paths and 26-30 AWG wire-wrap wire for signal connections. Power and ground planes reduce parasitic inductance through wide, short current loops. Dead bug construction, where integrated circuits are mounted upside-down with leads pointing upward, minimizes lead inductance for gate driver circuits. Twisted-pair wiring for gate drive signals rejects common-mode switching noise. Star grounding topology connects all ground returns to a single central point near the input power source, preventing high-frequency switching currents from coupling into low-level analog feedback signals.

### Analytical Models and Simulation Framework

The volt-second balance principle governs inductor current evolution over a complete switching cycle in steady-state operation. During the on-time interval when the switch conducts, the inductor experiences a positive voltage difference between input and output, causing linear current rise with slope (Vin - Vout)/L. During the off-time interval when the diode freewheels, the inductor voltage reverses to -Vout (buck topology) or -(Vout - Vin) (boost topology), producing linear current decay. Steady-state operation requires that the integral of inductor voltage over one complete period equals zero, otherwise average current would drift unbounded upward or downward. This volt-second balance immediately yields the fundamental conversion ratio relating output voltage to input voltage as a function of duty cycle D, the fraction of the switching period during which the main switch conducts.

For the buck converter, volt-second balance over one period Ts gives: (Vin - Vout)·D·Ts + (-Vout)·(1-D)·Ts = 0, which simplifies to Vout = D·Vin. The output voltage linearly scales with duty cycle from zero at D=0 to the input voltage at D=1, providing step-down voltage conversion with theoretical 100% efficiency assuming ideal lossless components. The boost converter topology yields Vout = Vin/(1-D) through similar analysis, capable of step-up voltage conversion with output voltage approaching infinity as duty cycle approaches unity. The buck-boost and flyback converters produce negative output polarity with conversion ratio Vout = -D/(1-D)·Vin, providing both step-up and step-down capability with polarity inversion.

Inductor ripple current ΔIL represents the peak-to-peak variation in inductor current during each switching cycle, superimposed on the DC average current required by the load. Smaller ripple current reduces RMS current through the inductor and output capacitor, decreasing conduction losses and output voltage ripple. The critical inductance value that maintains continuous conduction mode (CCM) where inductor current never reaches zero occurs when the minimum inductor current equals zero at the lightest load condition. Operating in discontinuous conduction mode (DCM) where inductor current returns to zero during each cycle simplifies the power stage by eliminating reverse recovery losses in the catch diode, but introduces load-dependent output voltage regulation requiring more complex control loops. The boundary between CCM and DCM occurs at the critical inductance Lcrit = (1-D)·Vout/(2·f·Iload), where f represents switching frequency and Iload the DC output current.

Small-signal AC modeling linearizes the nonlinear switching converter around its DC operating point to analyze control loop stability and transient response. The power stage transfer function from duty cycle perturbations to output voltage perturbations exhibits a dominant low-frequency pole set by the output capacitor and load resistance, typically 10-100 Hz, followed by a double pole at the LC resonant frequency where inductive and capacitive reactances cancel. A right-half-plane zero appears in boost, buck-boost, and flyback converters, creating a phase lag that complicates compensation because the zero contributes positive phase while simultaneously attenuating gain. This non-minimum phase behavior arises from the physics of boost action: increasing duty cycle initially decreases output voltage before the increased energy storage in the inductor subsequently raises it.

The voltage-mode control transfer function can be derived by perturbing the steady-state equations and applying Laplace transforms. For a buck converter with output capacitance C, load resistance R, and inductance L, the control-to-output transfer function takes the form Gvd(s) = Vin/(1 + s/(ω0·Q) + s²/ω0²), where ω0 = 1/√(LC) defines the LC resonant frequency and Q = R·√(C/L) represents the quality factor. High Q values produce underdamped peaking in the frequency response, requiring compensator zeros to cancel the LC double pole and provide adequate phase margin. The feedback compensation network, typically implemented with an error amplifier and passive RC networks, must provide sufficient gain at low frequencies to achieve tight DC regulation while crossing over the 0 dB axis at a frequency well below half the switching frequency with 45-60° phase margin to ensure stable transient response without excessive overshoot or ringing.

SPICE-level circuit simulation using LTspice or PSIM allows verification of converter operation before physical construction. The practitioner constructs the complete power stage including parasitic resistances in inductors and capacitors, MOSFET on-resistance and gate charge characteristics, and diode forward voltage and reverse recovery time. Transient analysis with 1-10 nanosecond timesteps captures switching transitions and voltage/current waveforms at individual cycle resolution. AC analysis sweeps frequency from 1 Hz to 1 MHz to extract Bode plots of the open-loop gain and phase response, enabling compensator design to achieve the target crossover frequency and phase margin. Parametric sweeps vary input voltage, load current, and component values to map the converter's operating envelope and identify worst-case stability margins.

## Exercise 1: Foundational Practical Implementation

### Buck Converter Open-Loop Construction and Characterization

This exercise constructs a synchronous buck converter operating at 100 kHz switching frequency with 12 V input, 5 V output at 2 A load current, and measures the fundamental voltage conversion ratio, efficiency, and inductor current waveforms. The synchronous topology replaces the catch diode with a second MOSFET, the low-side or synchronous rectifier, which conducts during the freewheeling interval with lower forward voltage drop than a Schottky diode, significantly improving efficiency at high output currents where conduction losses dominate.

Begin by selecting the power MOSFETs based on voltage stress and conduction loss criteria. The high-side switch must block the full input voltage of 12 V during the off-time, suggesting a 30 V rated device with 100% derating margin. The IRFZ44N N-channel MOSFET provides 55 V drain-source voltage rating, 49 A continuous drain current, and 17.5 mΩ on-resistance at 10 V gate drive, yielding conduction loss of I²·RDS(on) = (2 A)²·(17.5 mΩ) = 70 mW during the on-time. The low-side synchronous rectifier experiences identical current stress but zero voltage blocking requirement since it is source-grounded, allowing an even lower resistance device. The IRLZ44N provides 9.0 mΩ on-resistance, reducing freewheeling conduction loss to 36 mW. Total MOSFET conduction loss at 2 A output current approximates 106 mW, representing less than 1% of the 10 W output power.

Gate drive design determines switching speed and thus switching loss, which arises from the brief interval when the MOSFET transitions between on and off states while simultaneously supporting voltage and conducting current. The product of voltage and current during this crossover interval represents instantaneous power dissipation, and integrating over the transition time gives energy loss per switching event. Faster transitions reduce switching loss but increase dI/dt and dV/dt, which radiate electromagnetic interference and excite parasitic ringing. The gate drive resistor Rg sets the gate current according to Ohm's law, Ig = (Vdriver - Vgs)/Rg, where Vdriver represents the gate driver output voltage, typically 10-12 V for logic-level MOSFETs. A 10 Ω gate resistor with 12 V driver supplies 1.2 A peak gate current, charging the MOSFET's 65 nC total gate charge in approximately 54 nanoseconds, yielding switching transition times around 20-30 nanoseconds under typical conditions.

The MCP1407 gate driver integrated circuit provides 6 A peak source and sink current with 25 nanosecond propagation delay and direct TTL/CMOS logic compatibility, eliminating the need for discrete transistor drivers. The non-inverting input connects to the PWM controller output, while the high-current totem-pole output drives the MOSFET gate through the 10 Ω series resistor. A 0.1 μF ceramic capacitor placed within 5 mm of the gate driver VDD and GND pins provides local charge storage for the high transient currents during gate charge and discharge, preventing voltage droop on the driver supply rail that would slow switching transitions. The gate driver ground return must connect directly to the MOSFET source terminal using the shortest possible trace or wire to minimize source inductance, which introduces negative feedback that slows turn-on and risks oscillation.

Inductor selection balances core loss, winding loss, and physical size constraints. The required inductance value follows from the desired peak-to-peak ripple current, typically 20-40% of the DC output current to balance efficiency against filter capacitor requirements. Choosing 30% ripple gives ΔIL = 0.3·(2 A) = 0.6 A. The inductor voltage during the on-time equals Vin - Vout = 12 V - 5 V = 7 V, and this voltage acting over the on-time D·Ts produces current rise ΔIL/2. Solving L = (Vin - Vout)·D·Ts/(ΔIL/2) with duty cycle D = Vout/Vin = 5/12 = 0.417 and period Ts = 1/(100 kHz) = 10 μs yields L = 7 V · 0.417 · 10 μs / 0.3 A = 97 μH. A standard 100 μH inductor with saturation current rating above the peak current of 2 A + 0.3 A = 2.3 A suffices.

Wind the inductor using a Micrometals T106-26 iron powder toroid core with 26 μ permeability and outer diameter of 1.06 inches. The inductance formula for a toroidal core is L = (μ0·μr·N²·Ae)/le, where N represents the number of turns, Ae the effective cross-sectional area, and le the magnetic path length. For the T106-26 core, the manufacturer specifies AL = 195 nH/turn², meaning a single turn produces 195 nH of inductance. The required number of turns is N = √(L/AL) = √(100 μH / 195 nH) = √512 = 22.6 turns, rounded to 23 turns for practical winding. Use 18 AWG magnet wire with 0.040-inch bare diameter, capable of 2.3 A RMS current with minimal skin effect at 100 kHz. Space the turns evenly around the toroid circumference to distribute the magnetic field uniformly and minimize localized saturation. Measure the completed inductor with an LCR meter at 100 kHz to verify 100 ± 10 μH inductance and series resistance below 50 mΩ.

Output capacitor selection prioritizes low equivalent series resistance (ESR) to minimize output voltage ripple, which derives primarily from the capacitor ESR multiplied by the inductor ripple current rather than from capacitive charging effects at switching frequencies above 10 kHz. The output voltage ripple is ΔVout = ΔIL·ESR, so achieving 50 mV ripple with 0.6 A ripple current requires ESR below 50 mV / 0.6 A = 83 mΩ. Ceramic capacitors with X7R dielectric provide ESR in the 1-10 mΩ range, far exceeding this requirement. Parallel three 100 μF ceramic capacitors rated for 10 V with X7R dielectric, yielding 300 μF total capacitance and approximately 3 mΩ combined ESR. The capacitors must be rated for the full RMS ripple current, approximately ΔIL/(2√3) = 0.6/(2√3) = 0.173 A RMS per capacitor, well within the typical 1-2 A ripple current rating of 1210 case size ceramics.

Input capacitance provides a low-impedance source for the pulsed current drawn by the buck converter, which exhibits a square-wave profile with amplitude equal to the output current during the on-time and zero during the off-time. The RMS value of this square wave is Iin,rms = Iout·√D = 2 A · √0.417 = 1.29 A. Place four 47 μF ceramic capacitors in parallel at the input, providing 188 μF total capacitance and low ESR to absorb the pulsed current without excessive voltage ripple. Add a 1 μF ceramic capacitor immediately adjacent to the high-side MOSFET drain and source terminals to bypass high-frequency switching transients and reduce parasitic inductance in the switch node loop.

The PWM controller generates the 100 kHz square wave with adjustable duty cycle. The NE555 timer configured in astable mode provides a simple starting point, though its limited maximum frequency of 500 kHz with stable operation and lack of synchronization inputs make it suboptimal for production designs. Wire the 555 as follows: connect VCC (pin 8) to 12 V, GND (pin 1) to ground, and bypass VCC with a 0.1 μF ceramic capacitor. The timing resistor RA connects between VCC and the discharge pin (pin 7), while RB connects between the discharge pin and the threshold pin (pin 6). The timing capacitor C connects between the threshold pin and ground. The control voltage pin (pin 5) receives a 0.01 μF bypass capacitor to ground, stabilizing the internal voltage reference.

Select RA = 1 kΩ and RB = 6.8 kΩ to achieve approximately 100 kHz with duty cycle near 42%. The frequency equation for the 555 in astable mode is f = 1.44/((RA + 2·RB)·C), and the duty cycle is D = (RA + RB)/(RA + 2·RB). Solving for capacitance with f = 100 kHz, RA = 1 kΩ, and RB = 6.8 kΩ yields C = 1.44/((1 kΩ + 2·6.8 kΩ)·100 kHz) = 1.44/(14.6 kΩ · 100 kHz) = 986 pF, approximately 1 nF. Install a 1 nF C0G ceramic capacitor for low temperature coefficient and stable frequency. Verify the PWM frequency and duty cycle using the oscilloscope before connecting to the gate driver.

Assemble the complete converter on a perforated board using star grounding topology. Establish the power ground plane using wide copper foil or 18 AWG bare bus wire forming a rectangular loop around the board perimeter. Connect the input capacitor negative terminals, low-side MOSFET source, and output capacitor negative terminals to this power ground at a single star point near the input power connection. Route the high-current path from input positive through the high-side MOSFET drain, to the switch node (connection point between high-side source and low-side drain), through the low-side MOSFET to ground, completing a compact rectangular loop that minimizes area and thus parasitic inductance. Place the inductor immediately adjacent to the switch node, with the output capacitor bank connecting between the inductor output and power ground.

Separate signal ground from power ground to prevent switching currents from coupling into the PWM controller and gate drivers. Establish a signal ground plane adjacent to but isolated from the power ground, with the two grounds connected together only at the input power star point. The PWM controller, gate driver ICs, and any feedback circuitry reference signal ground. Twisted-pair wiring carries the PWM signal from the 555 output to the gate driver inputs, rejecting common-mode noise induced by the switching node dV/dt. Maintain at least 10 mm clearance between the high dV/dt switch node and any low-level signal traces to minimize capacitive coupling.

Before applying power, verify all connections with a digital multimeter in continuity mode. Confirm no short circuits exist between input positive and ground, or between the two MOSFET drain terminals. Measure the gate-to-source resistance of each MOSFET, expecting infinite resistance indicating no gate drive signal present. Check PWM controller operation independently by powering it from a separate bench supply and observing the output waveform on the oscilloscope, confirming 100 kHz frequency and 40-45% duty cycle. Verify gate driver operation by temporarily connecting a 10 kΩ resistor as a dummy load between the driver output and ground, observing 10-12 V amplitude square waves at 100 kHz.

Connect the input power supply set to current limit mode at 0.5 A and voltage limit at 12 V. Attach the electronic load set to constant current mode at 100 mA, a light load that ensures continuous conduction mode with comfortable margin above the boundary condition. Place oscilloscope probes on the switch node (high-side MOSFET source), inductor current (using current probe), and output voltage. Set the oscilloscope timebase to 10 μs/division to display a single switching cycle. Enable the power supply and observe the startup transient as the output voltage ramps from zero to the regulation point.

The switch node waveform should exhibit clean square wave transitions between ground potential during the off-time and input voltage during the on-time. Measure the on-time and off-time durations using oscilloscope cursors: the on-time should be approximately 4.17 μs and off-time 5.83 μs, yielding duty cycle of 4.17/(4.17+5.83) = 0.417 or 41.7%, consistent with the Vout/Vin ratio of 5/12. The inductor current waveform exhibits a sawtooth profile with upward slope during the on-time and downward slope during the off-time. The peak-to-peak ripple current should measure approximately 0.6 A, matching the 30% design target. The average inductor current equals the output current of 0.1 A, so the waveform oscillates between 0.1 - 0.3 = -0.2 A and 0.1 + 0.3 = 0.4 A. The negative current excursion indicates operation in discontinuous conduction mode at this light load, requiring increased load current to enter continuous conduction.

Increase the electronic load current in 100 mA increments, observing the inductor current waveform at each step. The minimum inductor current rises with increasing load, eventually remaining positive throughout the switching cycle at approximately 350 mA load current, marking the boundary between discontinuous and continuous conduction modes. Continue increasing load current to the full 2 A design point, verifying that the average inductor current matches the output current and the ripple current remains near 0.6 A. Measure the output voltage at each load step using the oscilloscope or a digital multimeter, documenting the load regulation error as the fractional change in output voltage from no-load to full-load. Ideal regulation yields zero change, but practical converters exhibit 1-5% droop due to resistive losses in the inductor, MOSFETs, and PCB traces.

Efficiency measurement requires simultaneous measurement of input and output power. Set the electronic load to 2 A constant current mode and allow thermal equilibrium for 5 minutes, ensuring all components reach steady-state temperature. Measure input voltage and current using the bench power supply's built-in meters, or preferably with separate high-accuracy digital multimeters to avoid instrument bias. Record Vin = 12.05 V and Iin = 0.875 A, yielding input power Pin = 12.05 · 0.875 = 10.54 W. Measure output voltage Vout = 4.98 V at the output capacitor terminals using a true RMS multimeter, though for DC measurements the averaging mode suffices. Output power is Pout = 4.98 V · 2.0 A = 9.96 W. Efficiency is η = Pout/Pin = 9.96/10.54 = 94.5%.

The 5.5% loss of 0.58 W distributes across conduction losses in the MOSFETs, inductor resistance, and switching losses. Verify this loss budget through individual component calculations. MOSFET conduction loss is P_FET = I²_rms,HS · RDS(on),HS · D + I²_rms,LS · RDS(on),LS · (1-D), where the high-side RMS current is approximately Iout·√D = 2·√0.417 = 1.29 A and the low-side RMS current is Iout·√(1-D) = 2·√0.583 = 1.53 A. This yields P_FET = (1.29)²·17.5mΩ·0.417 + (1.53)²·9.0mΩ·0.583 = 12.1 mW + 12.7 mW = 24.8 mW. Inductor conduction loss with 50 mΩ series resistance and 2 A DC current is P_L = I² · R = 4 · 0.05 = 0.2 W. The remaining loss of approximately 0.36 W arises from switching transitions, gate drive power, and controller quiescent current.

Thermal imaging or contact thermocouples identify hot spots requiring improved heatsinking. The high-side MOSFET dissipates approximately 140 mW total (conduction plus switching), raising junction temperature by ΔTj = P · Rθja, where Rθja represents the junction-to-ambient thermal resistance, typically 62 °C/W for the TO-220 package with no heatsink. This yields temperature rise of 140 mW · 62 °C/W = 8.7 °C above ambient, acceptable for low-power operation. At higher power levels exceeding 1 W dissipation, attach clip-on heatsinks with Rθsa = 10-20 °C/W, reducing total thermal resistance to approximately 25 °C/W and limiting temperature rise to acceptable levels below the 150 °C maximum junction temperature rating.

Measure output voltage ripple using the oscilloscope in AC-coupling mode with 10 mV/division vertical scale and 1 μs/division timebase. The ripple waveform exhibits a sawtooth profile at the 100 kHz switching frequency, created by the inductor ripple current charging and discharging the output capacitor ESR. Peak-to-peak ripple voltage should measure approximately 1.8 mV, calculated as ΔVripple = ΔIL · ESR = 0.6 A · 3 mΩ = 1.8 mV, excellent ripple performance due to the low-ESR ceramic output capacitors. Aluminum electrolytic capacitors with typical 50-100 mΩ ESR would produce 30-60 mV ripple under identical conditions, demonstrating the superiority of ceramics for switching converter applications despite their lower absolute capacitance per unit volume.

Document the complete measurement dataset in tabular form for later analysis and comparison with theoretical predictions. Record input voltage, input current, output voltage, output current, efficiency, inductor current ripple, output voltage ripple, and thermal rise for load currents ranging from 100 mA to 2 A in 100 mA steps. Plot efficiency versus load current, observing the characteristic curve that rises from low efficiency at light loads (dominated by fixed quiescent and gate drive losses) to peak efficiency in the 30-50% load range, then declining slightly at full load as conduction losses increase quadratically with current. The highest efficiency typically occurs between 1/3 and 1/2 of maximum load, representing the optimal operating point for the chosen components and switching frequency.

### Boost Converter with Right-Half-Plane Zero Characterization

This exercise constructs a 5 V to 12 V boost converter operating at 200 kHz switching frequency with 1 A output current, and empirically characterizes the right-half-plane zero that complicates control loop compensation. The boost topology transfers energy from input to output through the inductor during the on-time when the switch conducts, then delivers stored energy to the output during the off-time when the diode conducts. Unlike the buck converter where duty cycle linearly scales the voltage down, the boost converter exhibits a nonlinear hyperbolic relationship Vout = Vin/(1-D), causing output voltage to approach infinity as duty cycle approaches unity.

The duty cycle required to boost 5 V input to 12 V output follows from the ideal conversion ratio: D = 1 - Vin/Vout = 1 - 5/12 = 0.583 or 58.3%. This high duty cycle presents challenges for gate drive design, as the on-time of 5.83 μs at 200 kHz switching period leaves only 4.17 μs for the off-time energy transfer. Short off-times require low forward voltage drop diodes to minimize conduction loss during the brief freewheeling interval, making Schottky rectifiers mandatory for boost converters operating above 100 kHz.

Select a power MOSFET with drain-source voltage rating above the output voltage with margin: a 30 V device provides comfortable derating for 12 V output with 2.5× safety factor. The RFP30N06LE N-channel MOSFET offers 60 V rating, 30 A continuous current, and 35 mΩ on-resistance, yielding conduction loss of I²in · RDS(on) · D where Iin = Pout/(η·Vin) ≈ 12 W / (0.90 · 5 V) = 2.67 A assuming 90% efficiency. Conduction loss is approximately (2.67)² · 0.035 · 0.583 = 145 mW. The Schottky rectifier must block the full output voltage during the on-time and conduct the output current during the off-time. The MBRS340T3G Schottky rectifier provides 40 V reverse voltage rating, 3 A forward current, 0.5 V forward voltage at 3 A, and less than 10 nanoseconds reverse recovery time. Forward conduction loss during the off-time is Vf · Iout · (1-D) = 0.5 V · 1 A · 0.417 = 208 mW.

Inductor design for the boost converter follows similar ripple current criteria as the buck, targeting 30-40% peak-to-peak variation. The inductor voltage during the on-time equals the full input voltage Vin = 5 V, as the diode is reverse-biased and the output capacitor holds the output voltage constant. Choosing 30% ripple current based on input current gives ΔIL = 0.3 · 2.67 A = 0.8 A. The inductance is L = Vin · D · Ts / (ΔIL/2) = 5 V · 0.583 · 5 μs / 0.4 A = 36.4 μH. A 33 μH inductor with saturation current above the peak inductor current of 2.67 + 0.4 = 3.07 A provides reasonable tolerance margin. Wind the inductor on a Micrometals T94-26 core (AL = 135 nH/turn²) using N = √(L/AL) = √(33 μH / 135 nH) = 15.6 turns, rounded to 16 turns of 16 AWG magnet wire to handle the 2.67 A RMS input current with low conduction loss.

Output capacitance requirements differ from the buck converter due to the discontinuous output current profile. During the on-time, the diode blocks and no current flows to the output; the capacitor alone supplies load current. During the off-time, the diode conducts and delivers both load current and charge to replenish the capacitor. This pulsed discharge/charge cycle requires higher capacitance to limit output voltage ripple, following ΔVout ≈ Iout · D · Ts / C. For 50 mV ripple with 1 A load, 58.3% duty cycle, and 5 μs period, the minimum capacitance is C = Iout · D · Ts / ΔVout = 1 · 0.583 · 5 μs / 50 mV = 58.3 μF. Use three 47 μF ceramic capacitors in parallel for 141 μF total capacitance, providing comfortable margin and low ESR.

Input capacitance faces less stringent requirements than the buck converter, as the boost inductor draws continuous current from the input rather than the pulsed square-wave profile of the buck. A single 47 μF ceramic capacitor at the input adequately filters the residual ripple current and provides local charge storage for startup transients. Add a 1 μF ceramic bypass capacitor immediately adjacent to the MOSFET source and drain terminals to minimize parasitic inductance in the switching loop.

The PWM controller can utilize the same NE555 circuit from the previous exercise, but with adjusted timing resistors to achieve 58.3% duty cycle at 200 kHz switching frequency. Setting RA = 680 Ω and RB = 1.5 kΩ yields duty cycle D = (RA + RB)/(RA + 2·RB) = (680 + 1500)/(680 + 3000) = 0.592 or 59.2%, close to the target. The frequency is f = 1.44/((RA + 2·RB)·C) = 1.44/((680 + 3000)·C). Solving for C with f = 200 kHz gives C = 1.44/(3680 · 200 kHz) = 1.96 nF, approximately 2 nF. Install a 2.0 nF C0G ceramic timing capacitor.

Assemble the boost converter using identical layout and grounding practices as the buck converter. The critical high dI/dt loop consists of the input capacitor, MOSFET, diode, and output capacitor. Minimize the area enclosed by this loop through compact component placement, with all four components within a 2-inch square region. Route the power ground plane underneath this loop using copper foil or heavy wire, providing a low-impedance return path that minimizes radiated emissions. Connect the input capacitor negative terminal, MOSFET source, and signal ground at a single star point.

Bring up the boost converter using the same cautious procedure as the buck: verify all connections, test the PWM controller independently, set input current limit to 0.5 A, and begin with light load. Connect a 120 Ω resistor as a 100 mA load (Pload = V²/R = 144/120 = 1.2 W, verify resistor wattage rating), apply input power, and observe waveforms. The switch node voltage should swing between ground during the on-time and the output voltage of approximately 12 V during the off-time, the inverse of the buck converter where the switch node reached input voltage during on-time. Verify on-time of approximately 2.96 μs and off-time of 2.04 μs for 59.2% duty cycle at 200 kHz.

The inductor current exhibits the same sawtooth waveform as the buck converter, but the average value now equals the input current rather than output current, approximately 2.67 A at full load versus 1 A output. The current ramp-up slope during on-time is Vin/L = 5 V / 33 μH = 152 kA/s, and the ramp-down slope during off-time is (Vin - Vout)/L = (5 - 12)/33 μH = -212 kA/s. The asymmetric slopes reflect the different voltages applied across the inductor during each interval. Measure peak-to-peak ripple current, expecting approximately 0.8 A as designed.

Gradually increase load current to the full 1 A design point, monitoring output voltage regulation. Unlike the buck converter where output voltage remained relatively constant, the boost converter exhibits more pronounced droop due to the high duty cycle magnifying the effect of parasitic resistances. At 1 A load, measure output voltage and compare to the ideal 12 V. If output voltage sags to 11.5 V, the 4.2% regulation error arises from the voltage drops across MOSFET on-resistance, inductor DCR, diode forward voltage, and ESR of the output capacitor, compounded by the 1/(1-D) gain of the boost stage.

Measure efficiency using the same input/output power method as the buck converter. At 1 A output and 11.5 V output voltage, output power is 11.5 W. Set the pulse generator to create 10 ms duration input voltage sags from 12 V to 7 V at 1 Hz repetition rate.

Observe output voltage during the brown-out events. When input voltage sags below 8.5 V, the UVLO trips and disables the converter. During the 10 ms shutdown interval, the output capacitor supplies the 2 A load, decaying according to V(t) = V0 - I·t/C = 5 - 2·0.01/0.0003 = 5 - 0.067 = 4.93 V. The output voltage sags 70 mV, remaining well within tolerance. When input voltage recovers above 9.0 V, the UVLO releases and the converter restarts, recharging the output capacitor within 1-2 ms. This ride-through capability allows the converter to tolerate brief input disturbances without disrupting output load.

Increase the brown-out duration to 50 ms and observe deeper output voltage sag: ΔV = 2·0.05/0.0003 = 333 mV, dropping output voltage to 4.67 V. If the minimum output voltage specification is 4.75 V, this exceeds tolerance and requires increased output capacitance to extend ride-through time. Calculate required capacitance for 50 ms ride-through with 250 mV maximum sag: C = I·Δt/ΔV = 2·0.05/0.25 = 0.4 F or 400,000 μF, impractically large. Instead, reduce load current to 1 A or decrease ride-through requirement to 30 ms, yielding manageable 200-300 μF capacitance.

### Electromagnetic Interference Characterization and Mitigation

Switching converters generate broadband electromagnetic interference through high dI/dt current transients and high dV/dt voltage slews that couple into nearby circuits through radiated and conducted paths. Radiated emissions arise from current loops acting as loop antennas, with radiated power proportional to loop area and the square of frequency. Conducted emissions propagate back to the input power source through capacitive and inductive coupling, potentially disrupting other equipment sharing the same power bus.

This exercise measures conducted emissions on the input power line using a line impedance stabilization network (LISN) and oscilloscope-based time-domain analysis, then implements mitigation techniques including snubbers, shielding, and input filtering. The LISN presents a defined 50 Ω impedance to the converter input while blocking external power line noise from corrupting measurements, allowing repeatable conducted emission characterization.

Construct a simple LISN using a 50 μH inductor in series with the power line, shunted by a 50 Ω resistor to ground through a 0.1 μF AC coupling capacitor. Connect the power source to the LISN input, the buck converter to the LISN output, and an oscilloscope 50 Ω input to the LISN sense port (across the 50 Ω shunt resistor). Set the oscilloscope to 50 Ω input impedance mode to properly terminate the measurement port.

With the converter operating at 12 V input and 2 A output, observe the voltage waveform across the LISN sense resistor, representing the AC component of input current. The waveform exhibits high-frequency ringing superimposed on the fundamental switching frequency square wave, with peak amplitudes of 2-5 V corresponding to 40-100 mA RMS noise current. Perform FFT analysis of the time-domain waveform to convert to frequency spectrum, observing the fundamental at 100 kHz and harmonics at integer multiples (200 kHz, 300 kHz, etc.) extending to several megahertz.

The harmonics roll off at approximately -20 dB/decade above 1 MHz due to the finite rise time of the switching transitions, but discrete resonances appear at specific frequencies where parasitic inductances and capacitances form tank circuits. Measure the dominant resonance frequency, typically 5-20 MHz, and correlate with the parasitic LC resonance: f = 1/(2π√(Lpar·Cpar)). For a 20 nH PCB trace inductance and 200 pF MOSFET drain-source capacitance, the resonance is f = 1/(2π√(20nH·200pF)) = 25.2 MHz, consistent with observation.

Mitigation begins with minimizing loop area in the high dI/dt paths. Measure the area of the rectangular current loop formed by the input capacitor, high-side MOSFET, low-side MOSFET, and PCB ground plane. Use calipers or a ruler to measure the physical dimensions, calculating loop area as length × width. A 2 cm × 3 cm loop yields 6 cm² or 600 mm² area. Radiated field strength at 1 meter distance scales as E ∝ Area · I · f², so halving loop area reduces emissions by 6 dB. Relocate components to create a more compact layout with loop area below 300 mm², re-measure conducted emissions, and verify 3-6 dB reduction in harmonic amplitudes.

Install an RC snubber across the high-side MOSFET drain-source to damp the parasitic ringing. The snubber resistor dissipates energy stored in parasitic inductance during each switching transition, while the capacitor limits the rate of voltage rise across the MOSFET. Select snubber values using R = √(L/C) for critical damping, where L represents the parasitic inductance (20 nH) and C the snubber capacitance. Choosing C = 1 nF yields R = √(20nH/1nF) = 4.5 Ω, rounded to the standard value of 4.7 Ω. Install a 4.7 Ω, 0.5 W resistor in series with a 1 nF C0G capacitor directly across the MOSFET drain and source terminals using the shortest possible leads.

Re-measure conducted emissions with the snubber installed, observing significant reduction in the high-frequency ringing peaks, typically 10-15 dB suppression at the resonant frequency. The fundamental switching frequency and lower harmonics remain largely unchanged, as the snubber primarily affects transient ringing rather than steady-state switching transitions. Calculate snubber power dissipation as Psnubber = 1/2 · Csnub · Vds² · fsw = 0.5 · 1 nF · (12 V)² · 100 kHz = 7.2 mW, negligible compared to total converter losses.

Add a common-mode choke at the converter input by winding both positive and negative power lines together through a ferrite toroid core. Use a Fair-Rite 5943003801 material 43 toroid with outer diameter of 0.5 inches, winding 5 bifilar turns of 18 AWG wire. The common-mode inductance is approximately LCM = N² · AL = 25 · 700 nH = 17.5 μH, presenting high impedance to common-mode noise currents (those flowing in the same direction on both power lines) while offering near-zero impedance to differential-mode power transfer currents (flowing in opposite directions). Common-mode chokes effectively suppress radiated emissions by preventing high-frequency noise from propagating beyond the immediate converter vicinity.

Differential-mode filtering requires a series inductor and shunt capacitor forming a low-pass LC filter with cutoff frequency well below the switching frequency. Add a 10 μH inductor in series with the input power line, immediately followed by a 10 μF ceramic capacitor from the line to ground. The cutoff frequency is fc = 1/(2π√(LC)) = 1/(2π√(10μH·10μF)) = 15.9 kHz, providing -40 dB/decade attenuation above cutoff. At the 100 kHz switching frequency, attenuation is approximately -40·log₁₀(100/15.9) = -32 dB, effectively suppressing conducted emissions at the switching fundamental and harmonics.

Measure the complete system with all mitigation strategies implemented: compact layout, snubbers, common-mode choke, and differential-mode input filter. Conducted emissions should show 20-30 dB reduction across the 150 kHz - 30 MHz frequency range compared to the initial unfiltered design, demonstrating comprehensive EMI suppression through multi-faceted mitigation. Verify compliance with relevant standards (e.g., CISPR 22 Class B for residential environments, requiring conducted emissions below 60 dBμV at 150 kHz declining to 40 dBμV at 30 MHz) by converting the oscilloscope voltage measurements to dBμV: VdBμV = 20·log₁₀(Vrms/1μV).

## Operational Reference Guide & Diagnostic Checklist

### Component Selection Reference Tables

Power MOSFET selection balances drain-source voltage rating, on-resistance, gate charge, and thermal characteristics. The drain-source voltage must exceed the maximum voltage stress with 50-100% margin to account for parasitic inductance ringing and transient overvoltage. For buck converters, high-side voltage stress is Vds_max = Vin_max + Vringing, where ringing typically adds 30-50% of input voltage. Low-side voltage stress equals high-side stress in synchronous topologies. Boost and flyback converters subject the switch to output voltage stress, requiring devices rated for Vds > 1.5 · Vout_max.

On-resistance RDS(on) determines conduction loss according to Pcond = I²rms · RDS(on). The RMS current depends on topology: for buck converters, Irms_HS = Iout·√D and Irms_LS = Iout·√(1-D). For boost converters, Irms_switch = Iin·√D. Select MOSFETs with RDS(on) low enough that conduction loss remains below 1-2% of output power, balancing against device cost. The RDS(on) temperature coefficient of +0.5-0.7%/°C increases resistance by 50-70% at 125°C junction temperature compared to 25°C specification, requiring derating in thermal calculations.

Gate charge Qg directly affects switching loss through the gate drive power Pgate = Qg · Vgs · fsw and the switching transition time. Lower gate charge enables faster switching and higher frequency operation, but typically correlates with higher RDS(on) at a given voltage rating due to semiconductor physics tradeoffs. The figure of merit RDS(on) · Qg provides a single metric for comparing devices at equal voltage ratings; lower values indicate superior performance.

Schottky diodes for high-frequency rectification require forward voltage Vf below 0.5 V and reverse recovery time trr below 20 ns. Silicon carbide (SiC) Schottky diodes offer near-zero reverse recovery and low forward voltage, ideal for boost and flyback topologies where diode conduction loss dominates. Voltage rating must exceed peak reverse voltage with margin: for buck converters, Vr_max > 1.5·Vin; for boost converters, Vr_max > 1.5·Vout. Current rating should exceed peak diode current by 50% to ensure reliability under transient conditions.

Inductor selection requires balancing inductance value, DC resistance (DCR), saturation current, and physical size. Inductance value sets ripple current according to L = (Vin - Vout) · D · Ts / ΔIL for buck topology, or L = Vin · D · Ts / ΔIL for boost. Smaller inductance reduces size and cost but increases ripple current, raising conduction losses and output voltage ripple. Target 20-40% ripple current relative to DC load current for optimal balance. Saturation current rating must exceed peak inductor current by 20-30% margin, as core saturation causes inductance collapse and uncontrolled current rise.

DCR affects efficiency through conduction loss Pdcr = I²dc · DCR, typically 50-200 mΩ for 10-100 μH power inductors. Lower DCR improves efficiency but increases cost and size. Core material selection trades AC loss versus DC bias characteristics: ferrite cores offer low core loss at high frequencies (>100 kHz) but have moderate permeability (μ = 2000-3000) and saturation flux density (Bsat = 300-400 mT); powdered iron cores provide higher saturation (Bsat = 1000 mT) with distributed air gap that prevents catastrophic saturation, but exhibit higher AC core loss above 200 kHz.

Capacitor selection prioritizes low ESR, adequate RMS ripple current rating, and voltage rating with derating margin. Ceramic capacitors with X7R or X5R dielectric provide the lowest ESR (1-10 mΩ) and highest ripple current capability, mandatory for output filtering in converters above 50 kHz. Voltage rating must exceed maximum applied voltage by 50-100%; capacitance decreases significantly with applied DC bias voltage, losing 30-50% of nominal capacitance at 50% rated voltage for X7R dielectric. Specify capacitor values at the expected operating voltage rather than zero bias to account for this derating.

Aluminum electrolytic capacitors provide high capacitance density for bulk energy storage but suffer from high ESR (50-200 mΩ), limited ripple current (1-2 A), and temperature-dependent lifetime governed by the Arrhenius equation: lifetime halves for every 10°C temperature increase above rated maximum. Use electrolytics for bulk capacitance in parallel with ceramic bypass capacitors that handle high-frequency ripple current. Polymer electrolytic capacitors bridge the gap with ESR between ceramics and traditional electrolytics (10-50 mΩ) and superior ripple current ratings.

### Troubleshooting Decision Trees

**No output voltage, no switching activity:**
1. Verify input power supply voltage present at converter input terminals using multimeter DC voltage mode.
2. Check PWM controller VCC supply voltage at pin 8, expecting 10-15 V for proper operation.
3. Measure PWM controller output pin with oscilloscope, expecting square wave at switching frequency with 0-10 V amplitude.
4. If no PWM output, check controller enable/shutdown pin for logic high (enabled) or low (disabled/fault) condition.
5. If PWM present but no gate drive output, verify gate driver VCC supply and input signal connection.
6. Check for blown fuses, open traces, or disconnected power connections using continuity mode.
7. Measure MOSFET gate-source voltage during switching, expecting 10-12 V square wave indicating proper drive.
8. If gate drive present but switch node shows no activity, suspect failed MOSFET: measure drain-source resistance with power removed, expecting infinite resistance. Low resistance (< 1 Ω) indicates shorted MOSFET requiring replacement.

**Output voltage present but unregulated (too high or too low):**
1. Measure actual output voltage with multimeter and compare to expected regulation point. If >10% error, feedback loop is non-functional or misconfigured.
2. Check feedback resistor divider values, verifying ratio scales output voltage to controller reference voltage (typically 2.5 V). Measure voltages at divider midpoint with multimeter.
3. Verify error amplifier compensation network component values, ensuring no open or shorted capacitors/resistors.
4. Measure duty cycle at PWM controller output, expecting D = Vout/Vin for buck or D = 1 - Vin/Vout for boost. Duty cycle near 0% or 100% indicates controller saturation from excessive feedback error.
5. Check for noise or oscillation in feedback signal by observing error amplifier output with oscilloscope: clean DC voltage indicates proper operation, while high-frequency oscillation suggests instability requiring compensation adjustment.
6. Verify load current is within specified operating range: excessive load may cause dropout, while insufficient load (below discontinuous conduction mode boundary) may cause regulation error in voltage-mode control.

**Output voltage ripple excessive:**
1. Measure output voltage ripple using oscilloscope in AC coupling mode, 10 mV/div scale, 1 μs/div timebase for 100 kHz converters.
2. If ripple exhibits sawtooth waveform at switching frequency, dominant source is output capacitor ESR multiplied by inductor ripple current. Measure inductor current ripple with current probe; excessive ripple indicates insufficient inductance. Add parallel output capacitors to reduce ESR, or increase inductance value to reduce ΔIL.
3. If ripple shows high-frequency ringing superimposed on switching frequency, parasitic resonances are underdamped. Add RC snubber across switch node or increase gate drive resistance to slow switching transitions and reduce excitation.
4. If ripple amplitude varies with load current, check for insufficient output capacitance: ΔV = ΔI·ESR + (ΔI·Ts)/(8·C). Increase capacitance to reduce second term.
5. Verify capacitor connections are low-impedance: measure capacitor ESR with ESR meter or impedance analyzer at switching frequency. Damaged capacitors exhibit ESR 10-100× higher than specification.

**Thermal overheating of components:**
1. Identify overheating component using thermal imaging or contact thermocouple, noting steady-state temperature after 30-minute thermal soak.
2. If power MOSFET overheats, calculate expected dissipation from conduction loss I²·RDS(on)·D and switching loss 1/2·Vds·Ids·(tr+tf)·fsw. Compare calculated dissipation to measured temperature rise using thermal resistance Rθja or Rθjc. If calculated loss is excessive, reduce RDS(on) by paralleling devices, reduce switching frequency, or improve gate drive for faster transitions.
3. If heatsink is installed, verify thermal interface material is present and provides low contact resistance. Measure heatsink temperature and MOSFET case temperature; large differential (>20°C) indicates poor thermal coupling requiring TIM reapplication or improved clamping pressure.
4. If Schottky diode overheats, calculate forward conduction loss Vf·Iavg·(1-D) for buck converter. Replace with lower Vf device (SiC Schottky) or implement synchronous rectification using MOSFET.
5. If inductor overheats, measure DC resistance and verify core temperature. If DCR loss (I²·DCR) is excessive, select inductor with lower DCR or larger wire gauge. If core temperature exceeds 100°C, core loss dominates: reduce switching frequency, decrease flux density by using larger core, or select core material with lower loss tangent.

**Audible noise or whistling:**
1. Audible noise in 1-20 kHz range arises from mechanical vibration of magnetic components due to AC flux in the core creating magnetostrictive forces. Measure switching frequency; if < 20 kHz, the fundamental falls in audible range. Increase frequency above 25 kHz to shift harmonics beyond human hearing.
2. If frequency is >50 kHz but audible noise persists, subharmonic or beat frequency oscillation may be present. Observe inductor current waveform for period doubling, subharmonic instability (oscillation at fsw/2, fsw/3, etc.), or low-frequency amplitude modulation. These instabilities arise from insufficient phase margin; reduce control bandwidth by decreasing compensator gain.
3. Potted or encapsulated inductors reduce magnetostrictive noise radiation by mechanically damping core vibrations. Replace open-frame inductors with shielded, potted types.
4. Capacitor singing occurs when ceramic capacitors with piezoelectric properties vibrate under AC voltage stress. Use X7R or X5R dielectrics which exhibit less piezoelectricity than Y5V or Z5U. Mount capacitors with flexible adhesive rather than rigid solder to decouple mechanical vibrations from PCB.

**Electromagnetic interference exceeding limits:**
1. Measure conducted emissions using LISN and spectrum analyzer or oscilloscope FFT. Identify dominant frequencies and amplitudes.
2. If fundamental switching frequency harmonics dominate, implement differential-mode input filtering using series inductor and shunt capacitor with cutoff frequency fc = 0.1·fsw.
3. If broadband noise floor is elevated, common-mode currents couple through parasitic capacitances. Install common-mode choke at input with bifilar winding on ferrite core.
4. Identify high dI/dt current loops using near-field probes or simple wire loop connected to oscilloscope. Minimize loop area by relocating components and shortening PCB traces.
5. If radiated emissions from cables dominate, add ferrite beads to I/O cables near the converter. Each ferrite increases impedance by 50-500 Ω at 10-100 MHz, suppressing common-mode currents propagating along cables.
6. For emissions above 200 MHz, layout parasitics and switch node ringing dominate. Add RC snubbers across high dV/dt nodes, reduce gate drive speed by increasing gate resistor, and ensure ground plane provides continuous low-impedance return path under all high-frequency current loops.

### Measurement Procedure Best Practices

Oscilloscope probing technique critically affects measurement accuracy in switching converters due to probe ground lead inductance and loading. Standard oscilloscope probes with 6-inch ground leads exhibit 20-50 nH inductance, creating resonant circuit with probe capacitance that rings at 50-100 MHz. This ringing is measurement artifact, not actual circuit behavior, and obscures true waveforms. Minimize ground lead length to < 10 mm using spring-tip ground attachments, direct solder connections, or coaxial probe adapters. Verify probe compensation by measuring a known square wave source and adjusting probe capacitance trimmer for flat top with no overshoot or rounding.

Current measurement using oscilloscope current probes requires understanding probe bandwidth, sensitivity, and insertion impedance. AC current probes based on Hall effect or current transformer provide galvanic isolation but limited low-frequency response (typically 1 kHz minimum). DC current probes extend response to true DC but require careful zeroing procedure before measurements. Position current probe to measure inductor current (continuous, averaged DC plus AC ripple) rather than switch current (discontinuous, pulsed). Verify probe orientation matches current flow direction for correct polarity.

Efficiency measurement accuracy depends on simultaneous, synchronized voltage and current measurements at input and output. Use four-wire Kelvin connections to eliminate voltage drops in test leads: separate wires carry current to/from the device under test and sense voltage directly at terminals. Input power is Pin = Vin·Iin for DC circuits, while output power is Pout = Vout·Iout. Efficiency is η = Pout/Pin = (Vout·Iout)/(Vin·Iin). Measure voltages with digital multimeters in DC mode with ≥4½ digit resolution (0.01% accuracy). Measure currents using shunt resistors and differential voltage measurement, or use dedicated power analyzer instruments that simultaneously capture voltage and current with synchronized sampling.

Power measurement error budget includes instrument accuracy, measurement lead drops, and power factor effects if AC components exist. A 0.1% voltage error and 0.5% current error compound to approximately 0.6% power error. At 90% efficiency, a 0.6% measurement error yields ±0.6% uncertainty in the efficiency result, i.e., 90.0% ± 0.6%. To achieve better accuracy, use calibrated reference resistors for voltage and current shunts, maintain stable ambient temperature, and allow thermal equilibrium before recording measurements.

Bode plot measurement of control loop frequency response requires a network analyzer or discrete sine wave injection with swept frequency. Inject a small AC perturbation (50-100 mV peak) into the feedback loop at the error amplifier output or PWM controller input, sweeping frequency from 10 Hz to 100 kHz. At each frequency, measure the gain (output voltage ripple divided by injected perturbation) and phase shift (time delay between injected signal and output response converted to degrees: φ = 360·Δt·f). Plot magnitude in dB (20·log₁₀(Vout/Vinj)) versus frequency on logarithmic horizontal axis. Plot phase in degrees versus frequency on same axis. Identify crossover frequency where gain crosses 0 dB and measure phase at that frequency; phase margin is the difference between measured phase and -180°, typically targeting 45-60° for stable transient response.

Thermal measurement using thermocouples requires proper attachment technique to minimize measurement error. Clean component surface with isopropyl alcohol to remove oxidation and contaminants. Apply a small amount of thermal epoxy or kapton tape to secure thermocouple junction in intimate contact with component case, ensuring no air gaps. For junction temperature estimation of power semiconductors, measure case temperature Tcase and calculate junction temperature from Tj = Tcase + P·Rθjc, where P is component power dissipation and Rθjc the junction-to-case thermal resistance from datasheet (typically 0.5-1.5°C/W for TO-220 packages). Infrared thermal imaging provides non-contact temperature measurement but requires knowledge of surface emissivity (0.95 for black anodized metal, 0.1-0.3 for shiny bare aluminum, 0.7-0.85 for semiconductor plastic packages) for accurate absolute temperature readings.

### Design Optimization Procedures

Efficiency optimization balances multiple loss mechanisms: conduction losses in MOSFETs, inductor, and interconnect; switching losses during transitions; gate drive power; and controller quiescent current. Conduction losses scale as I²·R and dominate at low switching frequencies and high output currents. Switching losses scale linearly with frequency and dominate at high switching frequencies and low currents. The optimal switching frequency minimizes total loss, typically 100-500 kHz for 10-100 W converters.

To optimize efficiency, measure loss breakdown by category: calculate MOSFET conduction loss from I²rms·RDS(on), estimate switching loss from datasheet curves or oscilloscope measurement of voltage/current overlap during transitions, calculate inductor DCR loss from I²dc·DCR, and measure total input-output power difference to account for all losses. Identify the dominant loss mechanism and target improvements: if conduction loss dominates, reduce RDS(on) or DCR; if switching loss dominates, reduce frequency or improve gate drive for faster transitions; if gate drive power dominates, reduce gate charge or frequency.

Transient response optimization requires balancing control loop bandwidth against stability margins. Higher bandwidth provides faster correction of load transients but reduces phase margin and risks instability. The control loop compensation network places poles and zeros to achieve desired frequency response shape: integrator behavior at low frequencies for tight DC regulation, gain crossover at 1/10 to 1/5 of switching frequency, and sufficient phase margin (45-60°) at crossover. Type II compensation (one pole, one zero) suffices for current-mode control. Type III compensation (two poles, two zeros) is necessary for voltage-mode control of buck or boost converters to cancel the LC double pole.

Component placement optimization minimizes parasitic inductance and electromagnetic coupling. Position the input capacitor, power MOSFETs, and output capacitor to form the smallest possible current loop, ideally < 500 mm² enclosed area. Route high dI/dt traces (switch node connections) as wide copper planes or heavy wire with minimal length. Separate power ground and signal ground planes, connecting them at a single star point near the input power connection. Place gate drive components adjacent to MOSFET gates with short, direct traces to minimize gate drive inductance that slows switching transitions. Route feedback signals away from high dV/dt switch nodes to prevent capacitive coupling of switching noise into control loop.

Thermal design ensures junction temperatures remain below maximum ratings with margin for component aging and ambient temperature variation. Calculate worst-case power dissipation for each component at maximum load, maximum input voltage, and maximum ambient temperature. Determine required thermal resistance from Rθja = (Tjmax - Tamb) / P, where Tjmax is the maximum allowable junction temperature (typically 125-150°C), Tamb the maximum ambient temperature (typically 85°C for industrial applications), and P the power dissipation. If natural convection thermal resistance exceeds required value, add heatsinks or improve airflow. Heatsink thermal resistance scales inversely with surface area and airflow velocity; doubling heatsink area or doubling air velocity halves thermal resistance.

Reliability enhancement addresses failure modes through redundancy, derating, and protection. Voltage stress derating ensures component voltage ratings exceed maximum applied voltages by 50-100% margin, preventing overvoltage failure from transients or fault conditions. Power derating limits steady-state dissipation to 50-70% of component maximum rating, providing margin for temperature variation and aging. Current derating ensures peak and RMS currents remain 20-30% below component ratings. Redundancy through parallel devices improves reliability but requires careful current sharing to prevent unequal loading; use matched devices and symmetric layout to ensure equal impedances. Protection circuits detect fault conditions and disable the converter or limit damage: overcurrent protection using current sensing and hiccup mode restart; overvoltage protection using output voltage sensing and shutdown; thermal protection using temperature sensors and thermal foldback.
