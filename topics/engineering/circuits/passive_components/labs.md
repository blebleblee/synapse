# Operational Verification Labs: Passive Components

## Objective & Operational Lab Setup

The primary objective of this laboratory sequence is to develop mastery in the empirical characterization, operational measurement, and failure-mode analysis of passive electronic components—resistors, capacitors, and inductors—across temperature extremes, frequency sweeps, and power dissipation stress conditions. While the theoretical impedance relationships ($Z_R = R$, $Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C}$, $Z_L = j\omega L$) provide the mathematical foundation, real components exhibit parasitic effects, tolerance drift, and thermal runaway behaviors that can only be captured through direct benchtop measurement and thermal imaging correlation. This laboratory guide emphasizes the physical reality of component behavior under operational stress, teaching practitioners to identify non-ideal characteristics, extract parasitic element values from measured data, and construct empirical correction models that bridge the gap between datasheet specifications and deployed circuit performance.

Your operational environment consists of three primary instrument categories, each serving a distinct measurement domain. The digital multimeter (DMM) with four-wire Kelvin sensing capability provides DC resistance measurements at microohm resolution, eliminating lead and contact resistance errors that plague two-wire configurations. The precision LCR meter, operating across the frequency spectrum from 100 Hz to 10 MHz, enables impedance magnitude and phase angle extraction, revealing parasitic series resistance in capacitors (ESR), parallel capacitance in inductors, and frequency-dependent loss mechanisms. The benchtop oscilloscope with differential voltage probes and current sense transformers captures time-domain waveforms during transient events, allowing direct observation of RC charging exponentials, LC resonant ringing, and inductor current slew-rate limiting. Supplementary equipment includes a programmable DC power supply with constant-current mode for controlled thermal stress testing, a calibrated thermocouple probe with digital readout for junction temperature measurement, and a thermal infrared camera for spatial temperature distribution mapping across component bodies during high-power dissipation.

Circuit construction methodology follows professional prototyping standards to ensure measurement repeatability and minimize parasitic inductance and capacitance introduced by wiring. All test circuits shall be assembled on FR-4 solderable breadboards with copper pad spacing of 2.54 mm, using 22 AWG tinned copper wire with bend radii no tighter than 3 mm to prevent work-hardening and intermittent connections. Ground return paths must form a continuous star topology converging at a single reference node adjacent to the instrument ground terminal, preventing circulating ground loop currents that corrupt millivolt-level measurements. Component lead lengths shall be trimmed to 6 mm stub length after insertion, minimizing parasitic lead inductance (approximately 20 nH/cm for straight wire) that becomes significant above 10 MHz. All soldered joints require 63/37 tin-lead eutectic solder with rosin flux, reflowed to a smooth concave meniscus indicating proper wetting and intermetallic bond formation. Cold solder joints exhibiting grainy texture or convex profiles introduce milliohm-level series resistance and must be reflowed before measurement commencement.

Data acquisition methodology employs quantitative spreadsheet analysis with uncertainty propagation to distinguish measurement noise from genuine physical phenomena. Each impedance magnitude measurement shall be repeated across ten consecutive readings with instrument re-zeroing between samples, yielding a statistical population from which mean, standard deviation, and 95% confidence intervals are computed. Temperature-dependent measurements require thermal equilibrium confirmation via successive readings separated by 60-second intervals, with equilibrium defined as temperature drift below 0.5°C over a five-minute observation window. Frequency-dependent impedance sweeps shall employ logarithmically-spaced frequency steps (ten points per decade from 100 Hz to 10 MHz) to capture resonant peaks and parasitic breakpoints with sufficient resolution. All raw measurement data, instrument settings, ambient conditions, and component batch codes must be recorded in a laboratory notebook with entries signed and dated, establishing a forensic audit trail for anomaly investigation and measurement reproducibility verification.

Safety protocols for high-voltage capacitor discharge and inductor flyback transients are non-negotiable. Electrolytic and film capacitors above 50V working voltage must be discharged through a 10 kΩ 5W wirewound resistor held by insulated probe handles, with capacitor terminals shorted by insulated jumper wire after discharge verification via DMM voltage measurement below 1V. Inductors switching off-state in current-carrying conditions generate flyback voltage spikes reaching kilovolt amplitudes; all inductive test circuits must include parallel flyback diodes (1N4007 minimum rating 1000V 1A) with cathode oriented toward the supply rail to clamp reverse voltage transients. Never probe energized circuits with hands, body, or conductive objects within 50 mm of exposed terminals. All power supplies must incorporate current-limiting set 20% above expected operating current to arrest thermal runaway before component destruction. A fire extinguisher rated for electrical fires (Class C) shall remain within 3 meters of the workbench at all times.

## Exercise 1: Foundational Practical Implementation

This foundational exercise establishes baseline measurement competency by characterizing a carbon film resistor across DC resistance, AC impedance magnitude versus frequency, and thermal coefficient drift under controlled heating. The experimental apparatus isolates the resistor's intrinsic properties from circuit loading effects, revealing how parasitic capacitance and lead inductance manifest as frequency-dependent impedance deviations from the ideal resistive model. You will construct empirical correction curves that compensate for these non-idealities in subsequent precision measurement applications.

Select a 1 kΩ ±1% carbon film resistor (example: Yageo CFR-25JB-52-1K) with 0.25W power rating and examine its physical construction under 10× magnification. The carbon resistive element appears as a dark helical groove cut into a ceramic rod substrate, visible through the tan epoxy encapsulation. This helical geometry, necessary to achieve kilohm resistance in compact form factor, introduces parasitic series inductance on the order of 10–20 nH, negligible below 1 MHz but dominant at VHF frequencies where inductive reactance $X_L = 2\pi f L$ exceeds the 1 kΩ DC resistance. Note the axial wire leads soldered to endcap terminations; each lead contributes approximately 15 nH/cm of additional inductance plus 0.2–0.5 pF of stray capacitance to the measurement fixture.

Begin DC resistance measurement using the DMM in four-wire Kelvin mode, which drives test current through one pair of terminals while sensing voltage through a second high-impedance pair, eliminating lead resistance errors. Connect the DMM's FORCE HI and FORCE LO terminals to the resistor leads via short 10 cm twisted-pair 18 AWG wires, then connect SENSE HI and SENSE LO via separate twisted-pair runs terminating within 5 mm of the resistor body. Enable the 1 kΩ range with 6½-digit resolution, yielding 1 µΩ least-significant-bit precision. Allow a 30-second auto-zero cycle to null thermal EMF voltages in the copper-to-solder junctions, then record ten consecutive readings at one-second intervals. For a typical 1% tolerance part, expect a mean resistance between 990 Ω and 1010 Ω with standard deviation below 50 mΩ, reflecting instrument noise floor rather than genuine resistance fluctuation. Compute the mean value $\bar{R}$ and standard uncertainty $u_R = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}$ where $\sigma$ is the sample standard deviation and $N = 10$ samples. Record this as the DC reference resistance $R_0$ at ambient temperature $T_0 = 23°C \pm 2°C$.

Transition to frequency-domain impedance characterization using the precision LCR meter configured in series equivalent circuit mode, which models the component as a series combination of resistance $R_s$, inductance $L_s$, and capacitance $C_s$. This model proves more accurate than parallel equivalent circuits for low-impedance components below 10 kΩ. Set the LCR meter to 1V RMS test signal amplitude and 100 Hz initial frequency. Connect the component between the LCR meter's HCUR and LCUR terminals using 50 mm coaxial cables with BNC-to-alligator adapters, ensuring the ground return path connects to LPOT terminal. Perform open-circuit and short-circuit compensation calibration with the alligator clips touching (short) and separated by 20 mm (open), storing correction coefficients in the instrument's non-volatile memory. This compensation nullifies the 2–5 pF parasitic capacitance and 50–100 nH inductance of the test fixture cabling.

Execute a logarithmic frequency sweep from 100 Hz to 10 MHz with ten measurement points per decade, recording both impedance magnitude $|Z(f)|$ and phase angle $\theta(f)$ at each frequency step. At 100 Hz, expect $|Z| \approx 1000~\Omega$ and $\theta \approx 0°$, confirming purely resistive behavior where capacitive and inductive reactances remain negligible. As frequency increases beyond 100 kHz, parasitic lead inductance begins to manifest as positive phase angle shift, indicating that the impedance vector rotates from the real axis into the inductive quadrant. By 1 MHz, typical measurements yield $|Z| \approx 1050~\Omega$ and $\theta \approx +3°$, corresponding to parasitic series inductance $L_s \approx 15~\text{nH}$ extractable via $L_s = \frac{|Z| \sin\theta}{2\pi f}$. Plot $|Z(f)|$ and $\theta(f)$ on semi-log axes (frequency logarithmic, impedance and phase linear) to visualize the breakpoint frequency where inductive reactance equals resistive impedance, occurring at $f_L = \frac{R}{2\pi L_s} \approx 10~\text{MHz}$ for typical carbon film resistors. Above this frequency, the component behaves as an inductor rather than a resistor, rendering it unsuitable for high-frequency applications without parasitic compensation networks.

Thermal coefficient measurement requires controlled temperature elevation while monitoring resistance drift. Construct a simple heating fixture by wrapping the resistor body with 500 mm of 32 AWG nichrome heating wire (2 Ω/meter resistance) in a loose helical coil, leaving 10 mm clearance at each end to prevent lead terminal overheating. Connect the nichrome heater to the programmable DC power supply configured for constant-current mode at 200 mA, dissipating approximately 0.08W in the heater coil. Attach a K-type thermocouple probe to the resistor body using thermally conductive but electrically insulating epoxy (example: Arctic Silver thermal adhesive), positioning the junction at the geometric center of the resistor body. Connect the thermocouple to a digital thermometer with 0.1°C resolution.

Maintain the resistor in four-wire Kelvin measurement configuration throughout the thermal test, continuously monitoring resistance via DMM while the heater elevates temperature. Apply heater current and record simultaneous resistance and temperature readings at 30-second intervals until temperature stabilizes at thermal equilibrium, typically 60°C ± 2°C after 5–7 minutes. Expect resistance to increase monotonically with temperature following the linear relationship $R(T) = R_0[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)]$ where $\alpha$ is the temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR). For carbon film resistors, typical TCR ranges from −200 to −500 ppm/°C (negative coefficient), meaning resistance decreases with temperature due to thermally-activated carrier generation in the carbon matrix. Plot measured resistance versus temperature data points on Cartesian axes and fit a linear trendline via least-squares regression. Extract the slope $\frac{dR}{dT}$ and compute $\alpha = \frac{1}{R_0}\frac{dR}{dT}$ in units of ppm/°C. For a 1 kΩ resistor exhibiting 5 Ω decrease over a 37°C temperature rise, the TCR evaluates to $\alpha = \frac{-5}{1000 \times 37} \times 10^6 = -135~\text{ppm/°C}$, indicating moderate negative temperature dependence requiring compensation in precision voltage reference applications.

Power dissipation limits define the maximum safe operating current before thermal runaway initiates destructive failure. The 0.25W power rating assumes 70°C maximum ambient temperature and 25°C/W thermal resistance from the resistor body to ambient air via natural convection. Calculate the maximum steady-state current via $I_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{\frac{P_{\text{max}}}{R}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.25}{1000}} = 15.8~\text{mA}$, yielding terminal voltage $V_{\text{max}} = I_{\text{max}} R = 15.8~\text{V}$. Construct a stress test circuit by connecting the resistor in series with a current-limiting power resistor (10 Ω 5W wirewound) and the programmable power supply configured for constant-voltage mode at 20V. Measure actual current via ammeter in series topology, confirming approximately 16 mA via Kirchhoff's voltage law $I = \frac{20~\text{V}}{1000~\Omega + 10~\Omega} \approx 19.8~\text{mA}$. This slightly exceeds the rated maximum current to induce observable thermal stress.

Monitor resistor body temperature via thermocouple and thermal IR camera during the stress test, recording temperature at 60-second intervals. After 10 minutes, the resistor should stabilize near 90–110°C, glowing faintly orange-red at the hottest point visible via the IR camera's false-color temperature map. Simultaneously monitor resistance via four-wire DMM, expecting 3–5% resistance decrease relative to the cold $R_0$ value due to negative TCR. If temperature exceeds 150°C or resistance drops below 900 Ω, immediately remove power to prevent catastrophic failure where the carbon film fractures or the epoxy encapsulation outgasses. Allow the resistor to cool naturally for 15 minutes, then remeasure DC resistance at ambient temperature. Permanent resistance drift exceeding 2% indicates incipient damage; such parts must be discarded and replaced in precision applications.

## Exercise 2: Parameter Variation & Empirical Analysis

This intermediate exercise extends component characterization to capacitors and inductors, emphasizing parasitic loss mechanisms that dominate high-frequency and high-current operating regimes. You will extract equivalent series resistance (ESR) from capacitor impedance phase angles, measure inductor core saturation onset via permeability rolloff, and quantify resonant frequency shifts caused by parasitic elements. The empirical models developed here enable predictive simulation of passive component behavior in switching power supplies, RF matching networks, and signal integrity applications where datasheet parameters alone prove insufficient.

Select a 10 µF aluminum electrolytic capacitor rated 50V with radial leads (example: Nichicon UPW1H100MDD), a 100 nF ceramic multilayer capacitor rated 50V X7R dielectric (example: Murata GRM31CR71H104KA01L), and a 100 µH radial inductor with ferrite core (example: Bourns RLB0914-101KL). These three components span the capacitance and inductance ranges encountered in power supply filtering, decoupling, and energy storage applications, each exhibiting distinct parasitic characteristics. The electrolytic capacitor suffers from high ESR (0.5–2 Ω) due to electrolyte ionic conductivity limits and aluminum foil resistance. The ceramic capacitor exhibits voltage-dependent capacitance loss (−20% at rated voltage for X7R dielectric) and piezoelectric microphonic noise susceptibility. The ferrite-core inductor displays magnetic saturation at moderate DC bias currents, causing inductance collapse and waveform distortion.

Begin with electrolytic capacitor ESR extraction via LCR meter impedance phase measurement. ESR represents the real-axis component of capacitor impedance arising from electrolyte resistivity, foil ohmic losses, and lead resistance. In the series equivalent circuit model, the capacitor's complex impedance is $Z_C = R_{\text{ESR}} + \frac{1}{j\omega C}$, yielding phase angle $\theta = \arctan\left(\frac{-1/(2\pi f C)}{R_{\text{ESR}}}\right)$. At low frequencies where capacitive reactance $|X_C| = \frac{1}{2\pi f C}$ greatly exceeds ESR, phase angle approaches −90°, indicating nearly ideal capacitive behavior. As frequency increases, ESR becomes comparable to $|X_C|$, rotating the phase angle toward 0° and increasing impedance magnitude above the ideal $|Z_{\text{ideal}}| = \frac{1}{2\pi f C}$ trajectory.

Connect the 10 µF electrolytic capacitor to the LCR meter in series mode with proper polarity: positive terminal to HCUR, negative terminal to LCUR/LPOT common. Configure 1V RMS test amplitude and execute a frequency sweep from 100 Hz to 100 kHz. At 100 Hz, expect $|Z| \approx 160~\Omega$ and $\theta \approx -88°$, close to the ideal capacitive response. At 1 kHz, measure $|Z| \approx 16~\Omega$ and $\theta \approx -85°$, indicating slight ESR influence. At 10 kHz, observe $|Z| \approx 2~\Omega$ and $\theta \approx -70°$, revealing significant ESR contribution. Extract ESR at each frequency via $R_{\text{ESR}}(f) = |Z(f)| \cos[\theta(f)]$ and plot ESR versus frequency on log-log axes. For aluminum electrolytic capacitors, ESR decreases with frequency following a power-law relationship $R_{\text{ESR}} \propto f^{-0.3}$ due to frequency-dependent electrolyte conductivity and skin effect in the aluminum foil. At 10 kHz, typical ESR ranges from 0.5–1.5 Ω for 10 µF capacitors, dissipating significant power $P_{\text{ESR}} = I_{\text{RMS}}^2 R_{\text{ESR}}$ in switch-mode power supply ripple current applications.

Verify ESR via time-domain measurement by observing capacitor voltage response to square-wave current excitation. Construct a test circuit with the capacitor connected between the oscilloscope probe tip and ground, driven by a function generator configured for 1 kHz square wave at 10V peak-to-peak amplitude through a 100 Ω series resistor. The series resistor limits current to approximately 100 mA peak, safely within the capacitor's ripple current rating. Connect the oscilloscope in AC coupling mode with 10× attenuation probe, triggering on the rising edge. Observe the capacitor voltage waveform: at each square-wave transition, the voltage exhibits an instantaneous step $\Delta V_{\text{step}} = I_{\text{peak}} R_{\text{ESR}}$ followed by exponential charging with time constant $\tau = RC$. Measure the step amplitude via oscilloscope cursor delta-voltage function, yielding $R_{\text{ESR}} = \frac{\Delta V_{\text{step}}}{I_{\text{peak}}}$. For a 10V step through 100 Ω driving a 10 µF capacitor with 1 Ω ESR, expect approximately 1V instantaneous voltage step riding atop the exponential charging transient. This time-domain method validates the frequency-domain LCR extraction, confirming ESR within ±15% agreement.

Transition to ceramic capacitor voltage-dependent capacitance measurement, a critical non-ideality in decoupling applications where applied DC bias reduces effective capacitance. X7R dielectric ceramics exhibit ferroelectric domain saturation under electric field stress, reducing dielectric constant and thus capacitance according to $C(V) = C_0 \exp\left(-\frac{V}{V_c}\right)$ where $V_c \approx 25~\text{V}$ is the characteristic voltage for 50V-rated capacitors. Construct a bias voltage test fixture using the programmable DC power supply in series with the LCR meter's test terminals via a bias tee network (RF choke inductor of 1 mH in series with the DC supply, AC coupling capacitor of 10 µF in series with the LCR meter signal). This configuration superimposes DC bias voltage on the 1V AC test signal without loading the DC supply with AC current or injecting DC into the LCR meter input stage.

Configure the LCR meter for 1 kHz measurement frequency and 1V RMS amplitude. Connect the 100 nF ceramic capacitor between the bias tee output terminals, ensuring no polarity constraints exist (ceramic capacitors are non-polarized). Set the DC bias supply to 0V and record the measured capacitance $C_0$, expecting approximately 100 nF ±10% tolerance. Incrementally increase DC bias voltage in 5V steps from 0V to 50V (rated voltage limit), recording capacitance at each bias point after 10-second settling time. Plot $C(V)$ versus $V$ on linear axes, observing monotonic capacitance decrease. At 25V bias, expect approximately 80 nF (20% loss), and at 50V bias, measure approximately 65 nF (35% loss). Fit the exponential decay model via non-linear least-squares regression, extracting the characteristic voltage $V_c$. This parameter enables predictive modeling of decoupling capacitor effectiveness in circuits operating at elevated DC voltages, where reduced capacitance degrades high-frequency bypass performance.

Inductor core saturation measurement reveals the DC bias current threshold beyond which ferrite permeability collapses, causing inductance to drop precipitously and waveform distortion to appear. Ferrite cores exhibit relative permeability $\mu_r \approx 1000–3000$ at low flux density, but saturate at $B_{\text{sat}} \approx 0.3–0.5~\text{T}$, above which $\mu_r$ drops toward unity. The saturation current $I_{\text{sat}}$ is defined as the DC bias where inductance decreases by 10% relative to the zero-bias value $L_0$. Construct a DC bias test circuit by connecting the 100 µH inductor in series with a 10 Ω current-sense resistor, driven by the programmable DC power supply in constant-current mode. Connect the LCR meter across the inductor terminals, configured for 10 kHz measurement frequency and 0.5V RMS amplitude to avoid AC flux excursion comparable to DC bias flux.

Set the supply current to 0 mA and measure the zero-bias inductance $L_0$ via LCR meter, expecting approximately 100 µH ±10%. Incrementally increase DC bias current in 50 mA steps from 0 mA to 2 A, recording inductance at each step. Plot $L(I)$ versus $I$ on linear axes. Initially, inductance remains nearly constant as ferrite permeability sustains linear magnetic response. At some critical current $I_{\text{crit}}$, inductance begins declining, signaling the onset of core saturation. Continue increasing current until inductance drops to $0.9 L_0$, defining the saturation current $I_{\text{sat}}$. For typical 100 µH ferrite radial inductors, expect $I_{\text{sat}} \approx 500–800~\text{mA}$. Beyond this current, the inductor's energy storage capability degrades and ripple current waveform distortion appears in switch-mode converters, manifesting as subharmonic oscillation and increased EMI.

Verify saturation via time-domain observation of current waveform distortion. Construct a flyback converter test circuit by connecting the inductor in series with an N-channel MOSFET (example: IRF540N) and the DC power supply set to 12V. Drive the MOSFET gate with a function generator producing 10 kHz square wave at 10V amplitude, switching the inductor current on and off. Insert the current-sense resistor (0.1 Ω 2W) in series with the inductor and connect the oscilloscope in differential voltage mode across the resistor, yielding current waveform $i(t) = \frac{v_{\text{sense}}(t)}{0.1~\Omega}$. With the supply current limit set to 1 A, observe the current waveform: during MOSFET on-time, current ramps linearly with slope $\frac{di}{dt} = \frac{V_{\text{supply}}}{L}$ until reaching saturation current $I_{\text{sat}}$, at which point the slope increases sharply as inductance collapses, causing current to surge toward the supply current limit. This non-linear current ramp indicates core saturation and necessitates operating the inductor below $I_{\text{sat}}$ with 20–30% derating margin.

Parasitic resonance measurement reveals the self-resonant frequency (SRF) where parasitic capacitance and inductance cancel, causing impedance magnitude to peak and phase angle to cross zero. For inductors, interwinding capacitance $C_{\text{par}}$ (typically 5–20 pF) forms a parallel LC tank circuit resonating at $f_{\text{SRF}} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC_{\text{par}}}}$. Above SRF, the inductor behaves as a capacitor. Connect the 100 µH inductor to the LCR meter in series mode and execute a frequency sweep from 100 kHz to 30 MHz, recording impedance magnitude and phase. Plot $|Z(f)|$ and $\theta(f)$ on log-linear axes. Expect impedance to rise with inductive slope $|Z| = 2\pi f L$ until approaching SRF, where impedance peaks and phase transitions from +90° to −90°, indicating capacitive behavior dominance. For 100 µH inductors with 10 pF parasitic capacitance, expect $f_{\text{SRF}} \approx 5~\text{MHz}$. Extract $C_{\text{par}}$ via $C_{\text{par}} = \frac{1}{(2\pi f_{\text{SRF}})^2 L}$, enabling high-frequency circuit simulation with parasitic effects included.

## Exercise 3: Stress Testing, Boundary Failure & Recovery Protocol

This advanced exercise subjects passive components to deliberate overstress conditions—voltage breakdown, thermal runaway, and magnetic core saturation—to empirically map failure boundaries and develop forensic diagnostic skills. You will induce catastrophic failures in sacrificial components, analyze the failure signatures via thermal imaging and oscilloscope waveform capture, and establish conservative derating guidelines that prevent premature failure in production designs. These destructive tests require strict adherence to safety protocols, as arcing voltages, molten solder, and explosive component rupture present burn and fire hazards.

Capacitor voltage breakdown testing determines the true dielectric strength margin beyond rated working voltage. Ceramic and film capacitors exhibit voltage-dependent leakage current and eventually undergo dielectric breakdown where an ionized conduction channel forms, causing catastrophic short-circuit failure. Aluminum electrolytic capacitors may vent electrolyte gas through pressure-relief vents or rupture violently if overvoltage exceeds formation voltage limits. Construct a high-voltage stress test circuit using a variable DC power supply capable of 0–100V output with current limiting set to 10 mA to prevent excessive energy release during breakdown. Select three 100 nF ceramic capacitors rated 50V and connect one in series with a 10 kΩ current-sense resistor and the high-voltage supply. Install the circuit inside a transparent polycarbonate enclosure to contain debris from explosive failure, and wear safety glasses rated for impact protection.

Connect the oscilloscope's differential voltage probe across the capacitor terminals and a current probe around the series resistor lead. Set the voltage supply to 0V and incrementally increase voltage in 5V steps, recording leakage current via ammeter and capacitor voltage via oscilloscope at each step. Below rated voltage (50V), expect leakage current below 1 µA, indicating high dielectric resistance exceeding 50 GΩ. Above rated voltage, leakage current increases exponentially as electric field stress approaches the dielectric breakdown threshold $E_{\text{BD}} \approx 20~\text{MV/m}$ for X7R ceramics. Continue increasing voltage slowly (1V per 10 seconds) while monitoring current. At some critical voltage $V_{\text{BD}}$ between 70–100V, observe a sudden current surge to the 10 mA limit accompanied by a sharp voltage drop, indicating breakdown. The oscilloscope waveform shows a step transition from high voltage to near-zero voltage within microseconds. Immediately reduce supply voltage to 0V. Post-failure inspection via 20× magnification may reveal a pinhole breach in the ceramic body with carbon tracking or metallization burn marks radiating from the puncture site.

Repeat the test on the second and third capacitors, recording $V_{\text{BD}}$ for each sample. Compute the mean breakdown voltage $\bar{V}_{\text{BD}}$ and standard deviation, establishing an empirical safety margin. For 50V-rated ceramic capacitors, expect $\bar{V}_{\text{BD}} \approx 80~\text{V}$ with coefficient of variation 10–15%, indicating that a 60% derating (operating at 30V maximum) provides 2.5× margin against breakdown, sufficient for most applications. Conservative designs employ 50% derating (25V maximum operating voltage for 50V-rated parts) to accommodate voltage transients, aging effects, and manufacturing tolerance shifts.

Resistor thermal runaway demonstration illustrates the positive feedback loop where power dissipation increases temperature, reducing resistance (for positive TCR materials) or increasing resistance (for negative TCR materials), which further increases current or voltage, accelerating thermal runaway until catastrophic failure. Select a 100 Ω ±5% carbon composition resistor rated 0.5W (example: Ohmite OX104JE) and connect it in series with a 10 Ω current-sense resistor and the programmable power supply configured for constant-voltage mode at 24V. This configuration drives approximately $I = \frac{24~\text{V}}{110~\Omega} \approx 218~\text{mA}$ through the resistor, dissipating $P = I^2 R = (0.218)^2 \times 100 \approx 4.75~\text{W}$, nearly tenfold over rating.

Attach a K-type thermocouple to the resistor body with thermal adhesive and position the thermal IR camera to capture a continuous video feed of the component's temperature distribution. Apply power and begin recording temperature at one-second intervals while simultaneously capturing oscilloscope waveforms of voltage and current. Within 10–15 seconds, the resistor body glows dull red (500°C), visible in ambient lighting. Resistance drops due to negative TCR, increasing current slightly. After 30–60 seconds, the carbon composition matrix fractures with an audible crack, and resistance jumps discontinuously to several megohms (open circuit). Current collapses to microamp leakage levels as the power supply compliance voltage limit is reached. The thermal IR video shows localized hot spots reaching 700–800°C before failure, with thermal runaway concentrated at the resistor's geometric center where heat dissipation path length is maximum.

Post-mortem inspection reveals a longitudinal crack in the resistor body with exposed carbon element and blackened epoxy char. This failure mode—thermal stress fracture—results from differential thermal expansion between the carbon matrix and ceramic substrate. The failure signature is distinct from overcurrent fusing, where the resistive element vaporizes without body fracture. Document failure time, peak temperature, and failure mode in the laboratory notebook, establishing that sustained operation above 5× rated power leads to failure within 60 seconds, providing a quantitative basis for worst-case failure mode effects analysis (FMEA) in circuit design reviews.

Inductor saturation current destruction test pushes the component beyond its specified $I_{\text{sat}}$ rating until core heating or winding insulation breakdown occurs. Select a 47 µH ferrite core inductor rated $I_{\text{sat}} = 1~\text{A}$ and $I_{\text{RMS}} = 1.5~\text{A}$ (example: Wurth Elektronik 744771147). Connect it in series with a 0.1 Ω current-sense resistor and the programmable DC power supply in constant-current mode, initially set to 0 A. Attach a thermocouple to the inductor body and position the thermal IR camera for continuous monitoring.

Incrementally increase supply current in 250 mA steps, recording inductor temperature and inductance (measured via LCR meter at 10 kHz) at each current level after 60-second thermal equilibrium wait. At 1 A ($I_{\text{sat}}$ rating), expect temperature around 60–70°C and inductance reduced to approximately 42 µH (10% drop per specification). At 2 A, temperature rises to 100–120°C as core losses $P_{\text{core}} = k f^{1.3} B^{2.5}$ escalate due to increased flux density, and inductance collapses to 30–35 µH as the core saturates deeply. At 3 A, winding insulation begins smoking as magnet wire reaches its thermal class B limit (130°C). The thermal IR camera reveals hot spots at the winding layer interfaces where insulation breakdown is imminent. Continue increasing current cautiously; at 3.5–4 A, observe a sudden short-circuit event where inter-turn insulation fails, shunting adjacent winding turns and causing inductance to drop precipitously while current surges to the supply's current limit. Smoke and acrid odor from vaporized polyurethane insulation indicate complete failure. Immediately remove power and allow cooling for 10 minutes before handling.

Forensic analysis via destructive disassembly proceeds by carefully unwinding the failed inductor's wire turns with flush cutters and tweezers, counting the number of turns $N$ and examining the core for thermal cracks or demagnetization. Measure the core's inner diameter, outer diameter, and height with calipers, computing the effective magnetic path length $l_e$ and cross-sectional area $A_e$ from manufacturer datasheets. Apply Faraday's law $L = \frac{\mu_0 \mu_r N^2 A_e}{l_e}$ to back-calculate the pre-failure permeability $\mu_r$, confirming consistency with datasheet specifications. Document the thermal failure signature: charred insulation, discolored ferrite core, and copper oxidation at the turn-to-turn short location. This empirical evidence informs derating strategies: operate ferrite inductors below 70% of rated $I_{\text{sat}}$ to prevent thermal runaway, and limit RMS current to 60% of rated $I_{\text{RMS}}$ to maintain winding temperature below 100°C.

Recovery protocol for failed passive components focuses on rapid identification and replacement to restore circuit functionality. Construct a standardized diagnostic checklist that systematically probes resistance, capacitance, and inductance at DC and AC frequencies, comparing measurements against expected values with ±20% tolerance gates. A failed resistor exhibits open-circuit (infinite resistance) or short-circuit (near-zero resistance) rather than the nominal value. A failed capacitor may show short-circuit (electrolyte dried out or dielectric punctured), open-circuit (internal lead fracture), or grossly reduced capacitance (electrolyte leakage). A failed inductor typically exhibits open-circuit (wire fusing at turn-to-turn short) or drastically reduced inductance (core cracked or demagnetized).

Develop a three-tier severity classification for passive component derating in production designs. Tier 1 (critical path components in safety systems, medical devices, or aerospace): derate voltage to 50%, current to 60%, and power to 40% of absolute maximum ratings, ensuring operation well within the linear region with margin for worst-case environmental stress (temperature, humidity, vibration). Tier 2 (reliability-critical consumer electronics, automotive infotainment): derate voltage to 70%, current to 75%, and power to 50%. Tier 3 (cost-sensitive disposable consumer products): derate voltage to 80%, current to 85%, and power to 60%, accepting higher failure rates in exchange for reduced bill-of-materials cost. Document these derating guidelines in a formal design standards document, referencing the empirical failure data collected during this laboratory sequence as quantitative justification.

**Troubleshooting Decision Tree for Anomalous Measurements:**

- **Symptom:** Measured resistance 10× higher than expected  
  **Diagnosis:** Cold solder joint or intermittent lead connection  
  **Remedy:** Reflow solder joints; trim oxidized component leads; verify continuity with ohmmeter before measurement

- **Symptom:** Capacitor impedance phase angle near 0° at low frequency  
  **Diagnosis:** Short-circuit failure (dielectric breakdown) or incorrect polarity on electrolytic  
  **Remedy:** Remove component; measure with DMM in diode mode (should show open circuit); check polarity markings; replace if shorted

- **Symptom:** Inductor impedance magnitude decreasing with frequency  
  **Diagnosis:** Exceeding self-resonant frequency; parasitic capacitance dominates  
  **Remedy:** Reduce measurement frequency below SRF; use lower-frequency inductance value in circuit design; add external series resistance to dampen resonance

- **Symptom:** Thermal runaway initiates at 50% rated power  
  **Diagnosis:** Insufficient heatsinking or ambient temperature exceeds specification  
  **Remedy:** Increase copper pad area for thermal conduction; add forced airflow; reduce ambient temperature below 40°C; derate power by additional 20%

- **Symptom:** Electrolytic capacitor ESR increasing over time  
  **Diagnosis:** Electrolyte dry-out due to elevated temperature or ripple current stress  
  **Remedy:** Replace capacitor; review ripple current specification; ensure operating temperature below 85°C; consider low-ESR polymer or ceramic alternative

- **Symptom:** Ceramic capacitor microphonic noise audible at 20 kHz  
  **Diagnosis:** Piezoelectric resonance excited by AC voltage across X7R dielectric  
  **Remedy:** Select C0G/NP0 dielectric (non-piezoelectric); reduce AC voltage amplitude; mechanically dampen capacitor mounting with silicone adhesive; increase frequency above audible range

**Parasitic Element Extraction Formulae:**

Series resistance from phase angle: $R_s = |Z| \cos\theta$

Series inductance from phase angle: $L_s = \frac{|Z| \sin\theta}{2\pi f}$ (positive phase quadrant)

Parallel capacitance from phase angle: $C_p = \frac{1}{2\pi f |Z| |\sin\theta|}$ (negative phase quadrant)

Self-resonant frequency from LC product: $f_{\text{SRF}} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC_{\text{par}}}}$

Temperature coefficient from resistance slope: $\alpha = \frac{1}{R_0} \frac{dR}{dT}$ (units: ppm/°C)

Saturation current from inductance rolloff: $I_{\text{sat}}$ defined at $L(I_{\text{sat}}) = 0.9 L_0$

**Safety Limits and Emergency Procedures:**

- **Capacitor discharge protocol:** Always discharge capacitors above 50 V through 10 kΩ resistor before handling; verify voltage below 1 V with DMM; short terminals with insulated jumper after discharge

- **High-voltage breakdown testing:** Enclose test circuit in polycarbonate safety box; wear safety glasses; limit current to 10 mA; increment voltage no faster than 1 V per 5 seconds; immediately reduce voltage at first sign of arcing or smoke

- **Thermal runaway containment:** Conduct high-power tests inside fume hood with exhaust ventilation; keep fire extinguisher within 3 m; monitor temperature continuously; remove power immediately if temperature exceeds 150°C or smoke appears

- **Inductor flyback suppression:** Install freewheeling diode (1N4007 minimum) across all inductive loads; orient cathode toward positive supply rail; never open-circuit an inductor carrying current—flyback voltage can reach kilovolt levels causing insulation breakdown and arcing

- **Electrostatic discharge (ESD) precautions:** Although passive components are generally ESD-robust, handle all components with grounded wrist strap when working with high-impedance circuits (above 1 MΩ) to prevent measurement perturbation from body capacitance coupling
