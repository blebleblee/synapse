# Lesson 3: Sensor Fusion—Multimodal Data Integration for Robotic AR

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

Robotic augmented reality systems face a fundamental challenge that distinguishes them from consumer AR applications: they must maintain millimeter-accurate spatial registration while operating in dynamic, unstructured environments where occlusion, sensor dropout, and motion blur are not edge cases but operational norms. A warehouse picking robot equipped with an AR interface cannot afford the spatial jitter tolerated in smartphone face filters; its gripper trajectories depend on pose estimates that must converge within 2-5 millimeters across six degrees of freedom, updated at rates exceeding 100 Hz to accommodate servo control loops. This precision requirement exposes the core limitation of single-sensor localization approaches—no individual sensor modality provides both the bandwidth and the fidelity necessary for robust spatial tracking under real-world perturbations.

The mathematical heart of sensor fusion lies in complementary error characteristics across modalities. Inertial measurement units integrate angular velocity and linear acceleration to produce high-frequency pose estimates with zero latency, but their open-loop integration accumulates unbounded drift at rates governed by bias instability and random walk coefficients specified in datasheets as angle random walk (ARW) in degrees per root-hour and bias instability in degrees per hour. Visual odometry systems, conversely, provide drift-free absolute position measurements by tracking landmark features across image frames, but their update rates are constrained by exposure times, feature extraction latency, and correspondence search complexity—typically yielding 30-60 Hz stereo visual odometry or 10-20 Hz monocular SLAM. LiDAR range sensors deliver millimeter-precision distance measurements immune to lighting variation, but their mechanical scanning mirrors or MEMS oscillators impose fundamental trade-offs between field-of-view coverage, angular resolution, and update rate, with typical automotive-grade units scanning at 10-20 Hz.

Sensor fusion algorithms exploit these complementary properties through probabilistic state estimation frameworks that model each sensor as providing noisy observations of an underlying ground-truth pose trajectory. The mathematical foundation rests on Bayes' theorem applied recursively over time: given a prior belief about the robot's state encoded as a probability distribution, and given a new sensor measurement with known noise characteristics, the optimal posterior belief combines both sources of information weighted by their relative uncertainties. This Bayesian update rule exhibits a profound invariant—it is both commutative and associative across sensor modalities, meaning that fusing IMU and camera measurements in either order, or fusing all three sensors simultaneously, yields identical optimal estimates. This property emerges directly from the independence assumption: if measurement noise from different sensors is uncorrelated, then the joint likelihood factorizes into per-sensor terms that can be combined in any sequence.

The Extended Kalman Filter represents the canonical linearized approach to this Bayesian recursion, approximating nonlinear sensor models through first-order Taylor expansions around the current state estimate. For robotic AR, the state vector typically encodes 15 dimensions: three-dimensional position, three-dimensional orientation represented as unit quaternions, three-dimensional velocity, and six-dimensional IMU biases for accelerometer and gyroscope offsets. The prediction step propagates this state forward using IMU measurements integrated through kinematic differential equations, while the update step corrects the predicted state using visual or LiDAR observations. The filter maintains a 15×15 covariance matrix that quantifies uncertainty in each state dimension and their cross-correlations, providing not just a point estimate but a complete Gaussian distribution over plausible states.

Nonlinear sensor models introduce critical challenges that distinguish robotic AR from textbook filtering problems. Camera projection geometry follows the pinhole model where a three-dimensional world point transforms to a two-dimensional image pixel through a nonlinear perspective division: pixel coordinates equal focal length times world coordinates divided by depth. Orientation kinematics exhibit even more severe nonlinearity—rotating a vector by a quaternion involves a triple cross-product operation, and the quaternion itself must satisfy a unit-norm constraint that defines a three-dimensional manifold embedded in four-dimensional space. These nonlinearities violate the Gaussian assumption underlying Kalman filtering: if the prior state distribution is Gaussian, propagating it through a nonlinear function produces a non-Gaussian posterior that cannot be exactly represented by a mean and covariance matrix.

The Unscented Kalman Filter addresses this limitation through deterministic sampling rather than linearization. Instead of computing Jacobian matrices through symbolic differentiation, the UKF selects a minimal set of sigma points that exactly capture the mean and covariance of the prior distribution, propagates each sigma point through the nonlinear function without approximation, then reconstructs the posterior mean and covariance from the transformed sigma points. For a 15-dimensional state space, the UKF requires only 31 sigma points (twice the dimensionality plus one) compared to the thousands of particles needed by Monte Carlo methods, making it tractable for real-time embedded systems. The unscented transform exhibits the mathematical property that it captures third-order accuracy for Gaussian priors, compared to the second-order accuracy of EKF linearization, with the practical consequence that UKF covariances remain consistent—they neither overconfidently collapse to zero nor pessimistically explode during aggressive maneuvers.

Multi-camera calibration introduces a spatial graph problem where each camera's extrinsic transformation relative to the robot's IMU frame must be estimated with sub-millimeter translation accuracy and sub-degree rotation accuracy to prevent AR registration errors from accumulating across the sensor suite. The calibration target—typically a planar checkerboard or AprilTag grid—must be observed simultaneously by all cameras across diverse poses that excite all six degrees of freedom in the relative transformations. The calibration optimization jointly refines camera intrinsics (focal length, principal point, radial and tangential distortion coefficients), extrinsics (rotation and translation from IMU to each camera), and temporal offsets (clock synchronization delays between sensor timestamps). This joint optimization converges through nonlinear least-squares methods like Levenberg-Marquardt, iteratively adjusting parameters to minimize reprojection error—the L2 distance between detected checkerboard corners in image space and their forward-projected positions using current parameter estimates.

Temporal synchronization represents a subtle but critical invariant in high-rate sensor fusion. IMU measurements arrive asynchronously at 200-1000 Hz, camera frames at 30-60 Hz, and LiDAR scans at 10-20 Hz, each with independent clock sources subject to crystal oscillator drift that accumulates at 10-100 parts per million. A 10-millisecond synchronization error at 1 meter per second robot velocity induces 10 millimeters of spatial misalignment, directly violating augmented reality registration requirements. Hardware timestamping through GPIO interrupts or PTP (Precision Time Protocol) network synchronization can achieve sub-millisecond accuracy, but software systems must additionally compensate for transport latency—the variable delay between photon arrival at a sensor and state estimate availability to downstream control loops. The fusion algorithm addresses this through buffered message queues that hold measurements until their timestamp falls within the current propagation interval, ensuring that no out-of-order updates corrupt the filter's causality.

LiDAR-RGB integration confronts the correspondence problem: associating three-dimensional point cloud returns with two-dimensional image pixels requires solving for the relative transformation between sensors and accounting for their different sampling patterns. LiDAR provides sparse millimeter-accurate range measurements organized in spherical coordinates defined by azimuth angle, elevation angle, and radial distance, while cameras deliver dense pixel arrays with known bearing angles but unknown depth. The transformation pipeline projects LiDAR points into camera image space through the calibrated extrinsic transformation, then associates each projected point with its nearest image pixel within an association radius that accounts for both geometric projection uncertainty and motion blur during the sensor's integration time. This geometric fusion enables semantic segmentation networks trained on RGB images to label LiDAR points with object classes, while simultaneously providing depth supervision that constrains visual odometry scale—a critical degeneracy for monocular cameras that cannot infer absolute metric scale from perspective geometry alone.

Uncertainty quantification separates principled sensor fusion from ad-hoc weighted averaging. Each sensor's measurement model specifies not just the expected observation as a function of state, but also the covariance of observation noise—a matrix that encodes how measurement errors correlate across different dimensions. For a stereo camera rig, pixel disparity measurements exhibit higher uncertainty at greater distances because small pixel errors induce large depth errors through the triangulation geometry, formalized by the depth variance equation: variance in depth equals baseline times focal length squared divided by disparity cubed. IMU measurements exhibit time-correlated noise where consecutive gyroscope samples are not independent but coupled through finite analog bandwidth, modeled by continuous-time power spectral density parameters that must be discretized to discrete-time covariance matrices using Van Loan integration. The Kalman gain matrix—the central quantity computed during each filter update—directly encodes the relative trust placed in prediction versus measurement, automatically balancing sensor contributions based on their specified uncertainty models rather than requiring hand-tuned weights that inevitably degrade under off-nominal conditions.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The sensor fusion pipeline for robotic AR follows a predict-update cycle synchronized to the highest-rate sensor, typically the IMU at 200-1000 Hz. The prediction phase propagates the state estimate forward using kinematic motion models, while the update phase corrects the predicted state when new camera or LiDAR measurements arrive. This architecture maintains a single unified state estimate that continuously incorporates all available sensor data, ensuring that the AR rendering pipeline always queries the most recent fused pose rather than relying on any single sensor's delayed or corrupted output.

State representation encodes both geometric quantities and sensor error parameters in a 15-dimensional vector. Position occupies three dimensions as the IMU frame's origin expressed in world coordinates, typically initialized at the robot's starting location or a globally referenced coordinate system like a building's floor plan. Velocity spans another three dimensions, representing the time derivative of position in world coordinates—not the robot-centric body frame velocity that IMU accelerometers directly measure. Orientation requires four dimensions encoded as a unit quaternion, a hypercomplex number $q = q_w + q_x i + q_y j + q_z k$ satisfying the constraint $q_w^2 + q_x^2 + q_y^2 + q_z^2 = 1$. This quaternion representation avoids the gimbal lock singularities that plague Euler angles when pitch approaches ±90 degrees, and it enables singularity-free interpolation for smooth AR object rendering. The final six dimensions encode IMU sensor biases: three for accelerometer offsets and three for gyroscope offsets, modeled as slowly varying parameters that drift on timescales of minutes to hours.

```text
State Vector (15D):
x = [p_x, p_y, p_z,           # Position in world frame (meters)
     v_x, v_y, v_z,           # Velocity in world frame (m/s)
     q_w, q_x, q_y, q_z,      # Orientation quaternion (unit norm)
     b_ax, b_ay, b_az,        # Accelerometer bias (m/s²)
     b_gx, b_gy, b_gz]        # Gyroscope bias (rad/s)
```

The prediction step integrates IMU measurements through continuous-time differential equations discretized using numerical integration methods. Given accelerometer measurement $a_m$ and gyroscope measurement $\omega_m$ sampled at time $t_k$, the true acceleration and angular velocity are $a = a_m - b_a$ and $\omega = \omega_m - b_g$ after bias correction. Position updates through $p_{k+1} = p_k + v_k \Delta t + \frac{1}{2} a \Delta t^2$, velocity through $v_{k+1} = v_k + a \Delta t$, and quaternion through the differential equation $\dot{q} = \frac{1}{2} q \otimes [\omega]$ where $[\omega]$ denotes the quaternion with zero real part and imaginary components equal to the angular velocity vector. This quaternion kinematics equation encodes rotation through the quaternion product, which for small timesteps can be approximated using a first-order Taylor expansion or implemented exactly through exponential map methods that convert angular velocity to a rotation quaternion via Rodrigues' formula.

```text
IMU Prediction (200-1000 Hz):

For each IMU sample at t_k:
  1. Bias-correct measurements:
       a_corrected = a_measured - b_accelerometer
       ω_corrected = ω_measured - b_gyroscope

  2. Rotate acceleration to world frame:
       a_world = q_current ⊗ [0, a_corrected] ⊗ q_current*
  
  3. Remove gravity (assuming g = [0, 0, -9.81] m/s²):
       a_world = a_world - g

  4. Integrate position and velocity:
       v_new = v_current + a_world * Δt
       p_new = p_current + v_current * Δt + 0.5 * a_world * Δt²

  5. Integrate orientation:
       Δq = exp(0.5 * ω_corrected * Δt)  # Rodrigues formula
       q_new = q_current ⊗ Δq
       q_new = q_new / ||q_new||  # Renormalize

  6. Update biases (random walk model):
       b_accel_new = b_accel_current + σ_accel * √Δt * N(0,1)
       b_gyro_new = b_gyro_current + σ_gyro * √Δt * N(0,1)
```

Covariance propagation accompanies state propagation, accounting for how uncertainty grows during dead-reckoning periods between absolute sensor updates. The covariance matrix $P$ evolves according to $P_{k+1} = F_k P_k F_k^T + Q_k$, where $F_k$ is the state transition Jacobian and $Q_k$ is the process noise covariance. The Jacobian encodes how small perturbations in the current state affect the predicted next state, derived by taking partial derivatives of the prediction equations with respect to each state variable. For example, the velocity Jacobian $\frac{\partial v_{k+1}}{\partial v_k} = I$ is simply the identity matrix since velocity prediction is linear, but the position Jacobian $\frac{\partial p_{k+1}}{\partial q_k}$ involves quaternion-rotation derivatives that couple position updates to orientation uncertainty. Process noise $Q_k$ models unmodeled dynamics and sensor white noise, specified from IMU datasheets: accelerometer noise density in $\mu g/\sqrt{Hz}$ and gyroscope noise density in $deg/s/\sqrt{Hz}$ scale with the square root of sample time to produce discrete-time covariance.

Visual measurement updates occur at camera frame rate, typically 30-60 Hz for stereo rigs or RGB-D cameras. The measurement model relates the robot's pose to observed feature locations in image space through camera projection geometry. Given a three-dimensional landmark position $p_w$ in world coordinates, the predicted pixel location follows the chain: (1) transform landmark to IMU frame via the inverse of the state's position and orientation, (2) transform from IMU frame to camera frame via the calibrated extrinsic transformation $T_{cam}^{IMU}$, (3) project to normalized image coordinates through perspective division, (4) distort through radial and tangential distortion polynomials, (5) scale by focal length and shift by principal point to pixel coordinates.

```text
Visual Measurement Model:

Given landmark p_world in world coordinates:

1. Transform to IMU frame:
     p_IMU = q_current* ⊗ [0, p_world - p_current] ⊗ q_current

2. Transform to camera frame (calibrated extrinsic):
     p_cam = R_cam_to_IMU^T * (p_IMU - t_cam_to_IMU)

3. Normalize by depth:
     x_normalized = p_cam.x / p_cam.z
     y_normalized = p_cam.y / p_cam.z

4. Apply radial distortion:
     r² = x_normalized² + y_normalized²
     x_distorted = x_normalized * (1 + k1*r² + k2*r⁴)
     y_distorted = y_normalized * (1 + k1*r² + k2*r⁴)

5. Project to pixels:
     u = f_x * x_distorted + c_x
     v = f_y * y_distorted + c_y

Measurement: z = [u, v] with covariance R_vision
```

The measurement Jacobian $H$ maps state perturbations to measurement residuals, computed through the chain rule applied to each stage of the projection pipeline. The position Jacobian $\frac{\partial z}{\partial p}$ captures how camera translation affects pixel locations—distant landmarks exhibit smaller pixel motion than nearby ones due to perspective foreshortening. The orientation Jacobian $\frac{\partial z}{\partial q}$ encodes how camera rotation moves features tangentially across the image plane, with magnitude proportional to angular distance from the principal point. This Jacobian evaluation requires careful attention to quaternion derivatives, which must account for the unit-norm constraint through a local tangent-space parameterization that uses only three degrees of freedom rather than the full four-dimensional quaternion vector.

Kalman gain computation balances the predicted state's covariance against measurement noise to determine optimal weighting. The gain matrix $K = P H^T (H P H^T + R)^{-1}$ scales linearly with state uncertainty $P$ and inversely with measurement uncertainty $R$, automatically increasing trust in measurements when prediction uncertainty has grown large during IMU dead-reckoning. The innovation vector $y = z_{measured} - H x_{predicted}$ quantifies the discrepancy between actual camera observations and what the current state estimate predicts should be visible. The state update $x_{updated} = x_{predicted} + K y$ adjusts the pose estimate proportionally to this innovation, weighted by the Kalman gain. Critically, the covariance update $P_{updated} = (I - K H) P_{predicted}$ reduces uncertainty when new measurements arrive, reflecting increased confidence from observing absolute landmark positions rather than continuing open-loop integration.

Multi-camera fusion extends this update step by concatenating measurements from all cameras into a single joint observation vector. If a stereo rig observes 50 features simultaneously, the measurement vector grows to 200 dimensions (four pixel coordinates—left and right u,v—per feature), and the measurement Jacobian becomes a 200×15 matrix encoding how each pixel observation depends on the robot's 15-dimensional state. This joint update provides stronger observability than sequential single-camera updates because cross-camera correlations constrain the relative geometry between sensors, reducing drift along weakly observable directions like the camera baseline axis. The computational cost grows quadratically with feature count due to the matrix inverse in the Kalman gain, motivating sparse update schemes that partition features into independent sets processed in parallel threads.

LiDAR integration follows an analogous measurement model but replaces pixel coordinates with spherical range-bearing observations. A LiDAR point returns azimuth $\alpha$, elevation $\beta$, and range $r$, which convert to Cartesian coordinates in the LiDAR's sensor frame through $p_{lidar} = [r \cos\beta \cos\alpha, r \cos\beta \sin\alpha, r \sin\beta]$. The measurement model predicts these observations by transforming expected landmark positions through the robot's pose and the calibrated LiDAR extrinsic transformation, then converting to spherical coordinates and comparing against actual returns. The key advantage over vision lies in range accuracy—LiDAR delivers millimeter precision at tens of meters, compared to stereo vision's rapidly degrading triangulation accuracy with distance. LiDAR's primary limitation is sparsity: even high-end automotive units return only 128×1024 points per scan compared to megapixel-resolution cameras, requiring careful feature association to match current scan points with map landmarks tracked across time.

Point cloud registration provides an alternative LiDAR fusion approach that bypasses explicit landmark tracking by directly aligning raw point clouds through geometric optimization. The Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm iteratively refines the robot's pose estimate by finding nearest-neighbor correspondences between the current scan and a reference map cloud, then computing the rigid transformation that minimizes point-to-point or point-to-plane distances. ICP converges through singular value decomposition of the cross-covariance matrix formed by corresponding point pairs, yielding the optimal rotation and translation in closed form each iteration. The covariance of this ICP-derived pose estimate can be approximated through the Hessian of the alignment objective, providing the measurement uncertainty $R$ needed to fuse ICP updates into the Kalman filter framework. Generalized ICP variants improve robustness by modeling each point as a Gaussian distribution rather than an infinitesimal sample, effectively down-weighting correspondences with large residuals that likely arise from occlusion or dynamic objects.

Temporal synchronization implements a message buffer architecture where each sensor publishes timestamped measurements into a priority queue ordered by timestamp. The fusion loop processes messages in chronological order, ensuring causal consistency—no measurement updates a state estimate retroactively after later measurements have already been incorporated. When a delayed measurement arrives with a timestamp earlier than the current filter time, the system must either discard it as stale or implement a buffered smoothing approach that maintains state history and retroactively reprocesses affected measurements. The fixed-lag smoother represents a practical middle ground, storing the last N state estimates and covariances, then rewinding the filter to the delayed measurement's timestamp, incorporating the update, and re-propagating forward through buffered IMU samples to return to the present. This smoothing incurs computational overhead proportional to buffer depth but recovers the full Bayesian optimality that would be lost by simply discarding late arrivals.

Hardware timestamping mitigates synchronization challenges by recording measurement times at the moment of sensor data acquisition rather than when packets reach the CPU. GPIO pins trigger interrupts when camera exposure completes or LiDAR mirror synchronization pulses arrive, capturing the timestamp before USB bulk transfer latency or network jitter can corrupt it. The Intel RealSense D435 stereo camera, for example, embeds FPGA-based hardware timestamps in each frame's metadata with microsecond precision. PTP network synchronization extends this accuracy across distributed sensors connected via Ethernet, using boundary clocks and transparent switches to maintain sub-microsecond alignment between spatially separated cameras or LiDAR units. The fusion algorithm must still account for sensor-specific latencies—camera exposure time, image signal processor pipeline delay, feature extraction compute time—by backdating each measurement's effective observation time to compensate for these known transport delays.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Real-time sensor fusion on embedded platforms demands algorithmic and implementation optimizations that preserve accuracy while meeting strict 100+ Hz update rates within power and thermal constraints. The naive Extended Kalman Filter implementation exhibits $O(n^3)$ complexity for the covariance update due to matrix inversion in the Kalman gain computation, prohibitive for the 15×15 state covariance matrices typical in robotic AR. Joseph form covariance updates $P = (I - KH)P(I - KH)^T + KRK^T$ improve numerical stability by maintaining positive definiteness even with finite-precision arithmetic, but they double the computation cost. Square root filtering factorizes $P = S S^T$ and propagates only the square root matrix $S$, reducing storage and exploiting the guaranteed positive definiteness of triangular matrices, at the cost of more complex Cholesky update rules.

Feature extraction latency dominates camera processing pipelines, with FAST corner detection requiring 3-5 milliseconds per VGA frame on ARM Cortex-A53 cores, and descriptor extraction for ORB features adding another 5-10 milliseconds per frame. GPU acceleration offloads this compute to dedicated SIMD hardware—the NVIDIA Jetson Xavier's CUDA cores process 640×480 images at 90 FPS through parallelized 16×16 tile-based corner detection, achieving 11-millisecond end-to-end latency from pixel readout to feature delivery. FPGA implementations push latency even lower by pipelining corner detection directly in the image sensor's data path, producing feature coordinates synchronously with pixel streaming without intermediate framebuffer storage. The Xilinx Zynq UltraScale+ achieves sub-millisecond feature extraction on 1280×720 streams by exploiting line-buffer access patterns that require only 8 rows of image memory rather than full frame storage.

IMU propagation at 200-1000 Hz demands fixed-point or SIMD-accelerated floating-point quaternion kinematics to avoid stalling the sensor fusion thread. The quaternion multiplication required for orientation propagation involves 16 multiply-accumulate operations per update, which at 1 kHz IMU rate consumes significant CPU cycles if implemented naively. ARM NEON intrinsics pack four quaternion components into 128-bit SIMD registers and execute the multiplications in parallel, reducing per-update cost from 16 to 4 vector multiply-add instructions. Lookup table precomputation for small-angle Rodrigues formula approximations trades memory for compute—a 256-entry table of $\exp(\omega \Delta t)$ for discretized angular velocities enables single-cycle quaternion updates with negligible accuracy loss for typical IMU rates where $|\omega| \Delta t < 0.1$ radians per sample.

Covariance matrix conditioning degrades during extended operation as numerical errors accumulate in the filter's prediction-update cycle, eventually violating the positive-definiteness constraint required for valid probability distributions. The symptom manifests as negative eigenvalues in $P$, causing the Cholesky decomposition used in Kalman gain computation to fail with non-recoverable errors. Periodic covariance resets every 10-30 seconds reinitialize $P$ to a diagonal matrix with empirically determined variances, sacrificing theoretical optimality to prevent divergence. Observability analysis identifies weakly constrained state dimensions where covariance grows unbounded—monocular visual odometry famously cannot determine absolute scale, causing velocity covariance to increase linearly with time until a scale reference measurement arrives. Adding semantic constraints like known landmark sizes or ground plane assumptions reintroduces observability by coupling scale to directly measured quantities.

Camera-IMU extrinsic calibration errors directly corrupt measurement Jacobians, introducing bias into state estimates that no amount of filtering can remove. A 1-degree rotation error in the camera-to-IMU transformation induces tangential registration errors proportional to landmark distance—at 5 meters range, this yields 8.7-centimeter positioning offsets that violate augmented reality requirements. Online calibration algorithms treat extrinsics as additional state dimensions estimated within the filter, but this approach suffers from weak observability unless the robot executes aggressive accelerations that excite inertial-visual correlations. Offline calibration using Kalibr or similar toolboxes achieves 0.1-degree rotation accuracy and 1-millimeter translation accuracy by observing a target across hundreds of poses, but requires careful excitation trajectories that independently perturb all six extrinsic degrees of freedom—pure translation fails to constrain rotation axes, and pure rotation leaves translation ambiguous up to a scale factor.

Rolling shutter artifacts corrupt visual measurements when camera exposure scans vertically across the sensor array over 10-30 milliseconds rather than capturing all pixels simultaneously. During this rolling interval, the camera's pose changes due to robot motion, causing straight edges to appear curved and introducing pixel displacements proportional to angular velocity and vertical pixel position. The measurement model must account for this effect by interpolating the robot's pose during the exposure window using buffered IMU samples, computing a different projection matrix for each image row. Ignoring rolling shutter induces systematic errors that scale with rotation rate—at 90 degrees per second yaw, a 20-millisecond exposure produces 0.5-degree angular smear, yielding multi-pixel feature displacement errors that corrupt pose estimates. Global shutter cameras eliminate this artifact at the cost of reduced light sensitivity and increased read noise, requiring higher-gain image signal processing that amplifies quantization errors.

LiDAR motion distortion mirrors rolling shutter challenges but manifests in point cloud space rather than image space. A rotating LiDAR sweeps a 360-degree azimuth scan over 50-100 milliseconds, during which the robot's pose evolves continuously. Points acquired early in the scan occupy different spatial coordinates than late-scan points unless compensated through pose interpolation. The de-skewing pipeline timestamps each laser return relative to scan start, then transforms it into a common reference frame by applying the interpolated IMU pose corresponding to that return's acquisition time. This requires synchronized buffering of 10-20 IMU samples per LiDAR scan and per-point transformation through the time-varying pose trajectory, increasing computational load by 100× compared to static scan processing. Some LiDAR units embed IMU sensors and perform internal de-skewing in firmware, delivering pre-compensated point clouds to the host CPU at the cost of reduced flexibility for fusion algorithm developers who cannot access raw timestamped returns.

Dynamic object filtering prevents corrupted landmark measurements from destabilizing the filter when tracked features belong to moving obstacles rather than the static environment. The challenge lies in distinguishing ego-motion-induced feature displacement from genuine object motion without ground truth labels. Statistical outlier rejection computes the Mahalanobis distance of each measurement innovation relative to its predicted covariance—innovations exceeding a chi-squared threshold with 95% confidence likely arise from incorrect associations or dynamic objects and are rejected before updating the state. RANSAC-based approaches fit the measurement model to random feature subsets, iteratively selecting the largest inlier set consistent with a single rigid motion hypothesis, thereby automatically discarding dynamic features as outliers. Deep learning segmentation networks provide semantic priors by labeling image regions as potentially dynamic (people, vehicles) versus static (buildings, roads), allowing the fusion algorithm to down-weight or discard features extracted from high-risk semantic classes.

Multi-hypothesis tracking addresses ambiguous data association when multiple landmarks project to similar image locations, creating correspondence uncertainty that cannot be resolved by nearest-neighbor matching. The Multiple Hypothesis Tracker (MHT) maintains parallel filter instances representing distinct association hypotheses—feature A matched to landmark 1 versus landmark 2—and propagates each hypothesis forward with probability weights reflecting likelihood ratios. Low-probability hypotheses are pruned when their cumulative likelihood falls below a threshold, preventing exponential branching. The surviving maximum-likelihood hypothesis determines state updates, effectively implementing a delayed decision that waits for additional measurements to disambiguate correspondence. This approach trades memory for robustness, requiring storage for 5-10 hypothesis branches and incurring 5-10× computational overhead, but it prevents catastrophic filter divergence from a single incorrect association that naive nearest-neighbor matching cannot recover from.

Sensor dropout handling extends the filter's robustness to temporary measurement loss during camera occlusion or LiDAR shadowing. When no visual or LiDAR updates arrive for extended periods, the filter continues propagating state through IMU integration while inflating covariance according to process noise models, accurately reflecting growing uncertainty during dead reckoning. The critical parameter is covariance inflation rate—too conservative (high process noise) causes filter overconfidence to vanish within seconds, preventing recovery when measurements resume; too aggressive causes unbounded uncertainty growth that eventually corrupts state estimates through numerical overflow. Adaptive process noise scaling monitors innovation statistics (the recent history of measurement-prediction mismatches) and adjusts $Q$ dynamically to maintain consistency—if innovations consistently exceed predicted covariance, the filter is overconfident and requires larger process noise to increase uncertainty.

Computational resource management partitions sensor processing across CPU cores and hardware accelerators to meet real-time deadlines. A typical pipeline assigns IMU propagation to a dedicated high-priority thread on Core 0 guaranteed to execute every 1-5 milliseconds, feature extraction to GPU compute shaders or FPGA fabric operating asynchronously, and Kalman update steps to medium-priority threads on Cores 1-2 that process camera or LiDAR measurements within 10-20 millisecond windows. Lock-free message queues decouple producers (sensor drivers) from consumers (fusion thread) without mutex contention, using atomic compare-and-swap operations to coordinate lock-free enqueue and dequeue. Priority inheritance protocols prevent priority inversion where the high-priority IMU thread blocks waiting for a lock held by the low-priority logging thread—the scheduler temporarily elevates the lock holder's priority to match the waiter, ensuring forward progress. Real-time Linux kernels with PREEMPT_RT patches bound worst-case scheduling latency below 100 microseconds, meeting the determinism requirements for 1 kHz IMU processing on general-purpose ARM or x86 hardware.
