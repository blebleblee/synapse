# Operational Verification Labs: Optoelectronics

## Objective & Operational Lab Setup

These laboratory exercises ground the practitioner in the empirical characterization, operational verification, and failure-mode analysis of optoelectronic devices—components that convert electrical signals into optical radiation or vice versa. The lab suite targets LEDs, laser diodes, photodiodes, phototransistors, optocouplers, and fiber-optic transceivers, emphasizing hands-on measurement techniques that reveal device physics through direct observation rather than abstract derivation. The goal is operational mastery: you will forward-bias light-emitting junctions, measure optical output power versus drive current, characterize spectral emission profiles, quantify photodetector responsivity across wavelengths, probe modulation bandwidth limits, and stress-test thermal runaway conditions until catastrophic failure. This is not semiconductor physics for its own sake; it is the discipline of translating datasheet parameters into real-world design margins, debugging intermittent optical link failures on the bench, and building intuition for how junction temperature, bias point drift, and optical coupling efficiency determine system reliability.

The analytical framework combines classical optics—radiometry, photometry, and waveguide theory—with solid-state device physics governing pn junctions, heterojunctions, and quantum wells. You will measure optical power in watts or dBm using calibrated photodetectors and integrating spheres, convert these measurements into luminous intensity (candela) or luminous flux (lumen) when appropriate, and cross-reference against manufacturer datasheet curves. Spectral analysis employs either a benchtop optical spectrum analyzer or a grating spectrometer to resolve emission line shapes, center wavelengths, and full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) bandwidth. For high-speed modulation characterization, you will drive optoelectronic devices with arbitrary waveform generators and capture optical transients using fast photodiodes coupled to oscilloscopes, extracting rise times, fall times, and small-signal bandwidth from the frequency-domain transfer function. Thermal imaging cameras or thermocouple probes monitor junction temperature during stress testing, correlating thermal resistance ($\Theta_{JA}$, junction-to-ambient in °C/W) with optical output degradation. Fiber-optic link budgets are validated by measuring insertion loss, return loss, and bit-error rate (BER) versus received optical power, employing calibrated attenuators and bit-error-rate testers (BERT).

The laboratory workbench requires a precision DC power supply capable of milliamp current regulation and millivolt resolution, essential for LED and laser diode biasing without exceeding absolute maximum ratings. A calibrated optical power meter with interchangeable detector heads (silicon photodiode for visible and near-infrared, germanium or InGaAs for telecom wavelengths around 1310 nm and 1550 nm) measures radiant flux and irradiance. An integrating sphere captures total luminous flux from Lambertian emitters, eliminating angular dependence. For spectral characterization, a fiber-coupled Ocean Optics or Thorlabs spectrometer with USB interface provides wavelength-resolved intensity data, exported as CSV for post-processing in Python or MATLAB. High-speed measurements demand a 1 GHz or faster oscilloscope with 50-ohm input impedance, a waveform generator with rise times below 1 ns, and a calibrated fast photodiode (e.g., Thorlabs DET10A, 1 GHz bandwidth). Fiber-optic tools include single-mode and multimode patch cables (SC/APC or LC connectors), FC/PC mating sleeves, variable optical attenuators, and an optical time-domain reflectometer (OTDR) for link diagnostics. Thermal characterization employs a FLIR infrared camera or K-type thermocouple epoxied to the device package with thermally conductive paste. Safety equipment includes laser safety goggles rated for the specific wavelength and power class, fume extraction for soldering and potting operations, and ESD-safe workstations with grounded wrist straps.

Software tools include Python with NumPy, SciPy, and Matplotlib for curve fitting and data visualization. Radiometric calculations and photometric conversions require photopic luminosity function data ($V(\lambda)$, CIE 1931 standard observer). Fiber-optic link budget spreadsheets implement path loss equations, accounting for connector insertion loss (typically 0.3 dB per mated pair for single-mode fiber), splice loss, fiber attenuation (0.2 dB/km at 1550 nm), and modal dispersion in multimode systems. SPICE-based circuit simulators (LTspice, Keysight ADS) model transimpedance amplifier (TIA) front-ends for photodetector signal conditioning, optimizing noise-equivalent power (NEP) and dynamic range. For laser diode threshold current extraction and slope efficiency calculation, linear regression of the L-I curve (optical power versus forward current) provides quantitative design parameters. All measurement data are logged with timestamps, environmental conditions (ambient temperature, humidity), and instrument calibration dates, ensuring traceability and repeatability.

Critical safety protocols govern laser diode operation. Class 3B and Class 4 laser sources require interlocked enclosures, beam dumps, and administrative controls to prevent accidental eye exposure. Even low-power LEDs in the ultraviolet range (UV-A, 315–400 nm) demand eye protection due to cumulative retinal hazard. Forward-biasing laser diodes without current-limiting resistors or active current regulation risks catastrophic optical damage (COD) from localized heating at the facet, permanently destroying the device in microseconds. Electrostatic discharge (ESD) is lethal to GaAs and InP laser diodes; always use grounded wrist straps and conductive foam for handling. Reverse-biasing optoelectronic devices beyond breakdown voltage induces irreversible junction damage; verify polarity with a multimeter diode test before applying power. Fiber-optic connectors must be cleaned with lint-free swabs and isopropyl alcohol before mating to prevent contamination-induced insertion loss and back-reflection. When cutting or cleaving optical fiber, wear safety glasses to protect against glass splinters, and dispose of fiber scraps in puncture-resistant containers.

## Exercise 1: Foundational Practical Implementation

The first exercise establishes baseline characterization of a visible-spectrum LED and a silicon photodiode, measuring the complete optoelectronic transfer function from electrical input to optical output and back to electrical detection. You will forward-bias a red AlGaInP LED (peak wavelength approximately 630 nm, typical forward voltage 2.0 V) through a series current-limiting resistor, sweep the drive current from 0 to 50 mA in 1 mA increments, and measure the corresponding optical output power using a calibrated silicon photodiode placed at a fixed distance and alignment. The resulting L-I curve (luminous intensity versus current) reveals the diode's turn-on threshold, linear operating region, and onset of efficiency droop at high injection currents due to Auger recombination and junction heating. Simultaneously, you will measure the forward voltage V_F across the LED at each current point, plotting the exponential I-V characteristic that follows the Shockley diode equation, extracting the ideality factor $n$ and series resistance $R_s$ through curve fitting. This dual measurement provides both optical and electrical design parameters needed for practical circuit implementation.

Begin by assembling the LED test circuit on a solderless breadboard. Select a standard 5 mm through-hole red LED with a specified luminous intensity of 200 mcd at 20 mA forward current. The series current-limiting resistor $R_s$ is calculated from Ohm's law: $R_s = (V_{supply} - V_F) / I_F$, where $V_{supply}$ is the DC power supply voltage (set to 5.0 V), $V_F$ is the LED forward voltage drop (approximately 2.0 V), and $I_F$ is the target forward current (20 mA). This yields $R_s = (5.0 - 2.0) / 0.020 = 150$ ohms; use a standard 150-ohm 1/4-watt resistor. Wire the anode of the LED to the positive terminal of the resistor, the resistor to the power supply positive output, and the LED cathode to the power supply ground. Insert a digital multimeter (DMM) in series with the circuit, configured to measure DC current with milliamp resolution, to monitor the actual forward current independent of supply voltage variations. Place a second DMM across the LED terminals in parallel, set to measure DC voltage, to capture the forward voltage drop $V_F$ directly at the junction.

For optical power measurement, mount the LED in a fixed holder or clamp, ensuring mechanical stability and consistent alignment. Position a calibrated silicon photodiode (e.g., Thorlabs FDS100, 1 cm² active area, spectral responsivity 0.4 A/W at 630 nm) at a distance of 10 cm from the LED lens, aligned along the optical axis to maximize coupling efficiency. Connect the photodiode in photovoltaic mode (zero-bias) to a picoammeter or a low-noise transimpedance amplifier with known gain (e.g., 1 MΩ feedback resistor, yielding 1 V output per microamp photocurrent). Record the photodiode output voltage or current reading, which is proportional to the incident optical power. To convert photocurrent $I_{ph}$ (in amperes) to optical power $P_{opt}$ (in watts), divide by the photodiode responsivity $\mathcal{R}(\lambda)$ at the LED emission wavelength: $P_{opt} = I_{ph} / \mathcal{R}(\lambda)$. For a silicon photodiode at 630 nm with $\mathcal{R} \approx 0.4$ A/W, a measured photocurrent of 4 μA corresponds to $P_{opt} = 4 \times 10^{-6} / 0.4 = 10$ μW incident on the detector.

Systematically sweep the LED forward current from 0 to 50 mA by adjusting the power supply voltage from 2.0 V to 10.0 V in 0.16 V increments, maintaining the 150-ohm series resistor. At each voltage step, record the forward current $I_F$ (from the series ammeter), the forward voltage $V_F$ (from the parallel voltmeter across the LED), and the photodiode output signal (converted to optical power $P_{opt}$). Pause 5 seconds between measurements to allow thermal equilibrium; junction self-heating shifts $V_F$ downward by approximately 2 mV/°C, and optical output power decreases with rising temperature due to reduced radiative recombination efficiency. Compile the data into three columns in a spreadsheet or Python NumPy array: `I_F_mA`, `V_F_volts`, and `P_opt_uW`. Plot the L-I curve as optical power versus forward current on a linear scale, identifying the threshold current (typically 1–2 mA for visible LEDs, where optical emission becomes detectable above noise floor) and the linear region where $P_{opt} \propto I_F$ holds. At high currents (above 30 mA for typical 5 mm LEDs), observe efficiency droop where the slope $\Delta P_{opt} / \Delta I_F$ decreases due to junction heating and carrier overflow from the active region.

Next, analyze the electrical I-V characteristic to extract the diode ideality factor and series resistance. The Shockley equation relates forward current to forward voltage: $I_F = I_s \left( e^{qV_F / nkT} - 1 \right)$, where $I_s$ is the saturation current, $q$ is the elementary charge ($1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C), $k$ is Boltzmann's constant ($1.381 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K), $T$ is absolute temperature (assume 300 K), and $n$ is the ideality factor (typically 1.5–2.5 for LEDs due to recombination in the depletion region and quasi-neutral regions). At room temperature, the thermal voltage $V_T = kT/q \approx 26$ mV. Taking the natural logarithm of both sides yields $\ln(I_F) = \ln(I_s) + (qV_F / nkT)$, suggesting a semilog plot of $\ln(I_F)$ versus $V_F$ should produce a straight line with slope $q / (nkT)$. In practice, series resistance $R_s$ causes deviation from ideal behavior at high currents, where the applied voltage $V_{applied} = V_F + I_F R_s$. To account for this, plot $\ln(I_F)$ versus $V_F$ and fit a linear regression to the low-current region (below 10 mA) where $I_F R_s \ll V_F$. The slope $m = q / (nkT)$ yields the ideality factor $n = q / (mkT) = 1.602 \times 10^{-19} / (m \times 1.381 \times 10^{-23} \times 300) \approx 38.6 / m$. For a typical red LED, expect $n \approx 2.0$, implying slope $m \approx 19.3$ V$^{-1}$.

To extract series resistance, examine the high-current region where the I-V curve deviates from exponential behavior. Rearrange the voltage equation as $V_{applied} = V_F + I_F R_s$, where $V_F$ follows the Shockley exponential. At currents above 20 mA, plot $V_{applied}$ versus $I_F$; the slope of the linear portion approximates $R_s$. For a 5 mm LED, typical series resistance ranges from 5 to 20 ohms, originating from bond wire resistance, bulk semiconductor resistivity, and contact resistance at the anode and cathode. A more rigorous extraction fits the full Shockley equation with series resistance included: $I_F = I_s \left( e^{q(V_{applied} - I_F R_s) / nkT} - 1 \right)$, using nonlinear least-squares regression (e.g., `scipy.optimize.curve_fit` in Python) to simultaneously determine $I_s$, $n$, and $R_s$.

For photometric characterization, convert the measured optical power (radiometric quantity in watts) into luminous intensity (photometric quantity in candela) using the photopic luminosity function. The LED's spectral power distribution is centered at 630 nm with a typical FWHM bandwidth of 20 nm; the photopic luminosity function $V(\lambda)$ at 630 nm is approximately 0.265 (relative to peak sensitivity at 555 nm where $V(555) = 1.0$). The luminous efficacy of radiation $K(\lambda) = 683 \, \text{lm/W} \times V(\lambda)$ quantifies the conversion from radiant flux to luminous flux. For the red LED, $K(630) = 683 \times 0.265 \approx 181$ lm/W. If the measured optical power is 10 μW at 20 mA forward current, the luminous flux is $\Phi_v = P_{opt} \times K(\lambda) = 10 \times 10^{-6} \times 181 \approx 1.81$ millilumens. Assuming a Lambertian emission pattern with half-angle $\theta_{1/2} = 15°$, the luminous intensity on-axis is $I_v = \Phi_v / \Omega$, where $\Omega = 2\pi(1 - \cos\theta_{1/2})$ is the solid angle subtended by the emission cone. For $\theta_{1/2} = 15°$, $\Omega \approx 0.214$ steradians, yielding $I_v \approx 1.81 / 0.214 \approx 8.5$ millicandela. Datasheet specifications typically quote luminous intensity in millicandela (mcd) at a standard test current of 20 mA; verify your measurement against the datasheet value (200 mcd for the example LED) to validate the optical setup and calibration.

Document the complete dataset in a structured table with columns: forward current (mA), forward voltage (V), optical power (μW), and derived quantities including luminous flux (mlm) and luminous intensity (mcd). Generate three publication-quality plots using Matplotlib: (1) L-I curve (optical power versus forward current) on linear axes, annotating the threshold current and linear slope (expressed as μW/mA, equivalent to wall-plug efficiency in percent when accounting for forward voltage); (2) I-V characteristic (forward current versus forward voltage) on a semilog scale, overlaying the fitted Shockley curve with extracted ideality factor and series resistance; (3) luminous intensity versus forward current, demonstrating the conversion from radiometric to photometric units. Include error bars derived from measurement uncertainty (typically ±1% for calibrated power meters and ±0.5% for DMMs). Store the data in CSV format with metadata: device part number, measurement date, ambient temperature, and instrument calibration certificates.

This foundational exercise establishes the optoelectronic measurement discipline: precise electrical biasing, calibrated optical power detection, and rigorous data analysis linking device physics (pn junction recombination, radiative efficiency) to practical circuit design (current limiting, thermal management). The L-I and I-V curves become the empirical fingerprint of the LED, enabling you to predict optical output, power dissipation, and thermal rise in any circuit configuration.

## Exercise 2: Parameter Variation & Empirical Analysis

The second exercise extends the baseline characterization by systematically varying environmental and operating parameters to quantify their impact on optoelectronic device performance. You will measure the temperature dependence of LED emission wavelength and optical output power, characterize the spectral responsivity of a photodiode across the visible and near-infrared spectrum, and evaluate the modulation bandwidth of an LED-photodiode link using small-signal AC analysis. These measurements reveal second-order effects critical for robust system design: how junction temperature shifts the emission spectrum due to bandgap shrinkage, how photodiode quantum efficiency varies with photon energy, and how carrier recombination lifetime and junction capacitance limit the maximum data rate in fiber-optic links. The empirical data provide design margins for worst-case environmental conditions, aging effects, and process variations across production lots.

Begin with thermal characterization of the LED's optical output power as a function of junction temperature. Mount the 5 mm red LED on a thermoelectric cooler (TEC) or in a temperature-controlled oven with programmable setpoints from -20°C to +80°C. Attach a K-type thermocouple to the LED package using thermally conductive epoxy (Omega Engineering OB-101), ensuring direct thermal contact with the plastic lens base where the die is mounted. Connect the LED in series with a precision current source (e.g., Keysight B2902A) set to 20 mA constant current, eliminating forward voltage variations that would confound thermal measurements. Position the calibrated silicon photodiode 10 cm from the LED along the optical axis, using the same fixture from Exercise 1 to maintain alignment. Sweep the ambient temperature from -20°C to +80°C in 10°C increments, allowing 10 minutes at each setpoint for thermal equilibrium. Record the thermocouple reading (junction temperature $T_J$, in °C) and the photodiode output (optical power $P_{opt}$, in μW). Observe that optical output power decreases linearly with junction temperature at approximately -0.5%/°C for AlGaInP red LEDs, a phenomenon caused by increased non-radiative recombination as thermal energy excites carriers into deep trap states and Auger processes become more probable. The temperature coefficient of optical power, $\alpha_P = (1/P_{opt,0}) \, dP_{opt}/dT_J$, is extracted from the slope of the $P_{opt}$ versus $T_J$ plot; typical values range from -0.3%/°C to -0.8%/°C depending on LED material system and wavelength.

To quantify spectral shift with temperature, replace the silicon photodiode with a fiber-coupled spectrometer (e.g., Ocean Optics USB2000+, wavelength range 200–1100 nm, resolution 1.5 nm). Cleave a 1-meter multimode fiber patch cable (62.5/125 μm core/cladding diameter) and position the cleaved end 5 mm from the LED lens, capturing a portion of the emitted light into the fiber's numerical aperture (NA ≈ 0.275 for standard multimode fiber). Connect the fiber to the spectrometer's SMA905 input and launch the Ocean Optics SpectraSuite software or export raw spectral data via USB for post-processing in Python. At each temperature setpoint, acquire a spectral scan with 100 ms integration time and 10-scan averaging to reduce shot noise. The resulting spectrum is a Gaussian or Lorentzian line shape centered at peak wavelength $\lambda_{peak}$ with FWHM bandwidth $\Delta\lambda$. Export the spectrum as a two-column ASCII file (wavelength in nm, intensity in arbitrary units) and load into Python for analysis. Fit a Gaussian function $I(\lambda) = I_0 \exp\left[ -((\lambda - \lambda_{peak})^2) / (2\sigma^2) \right]$, where $\sigma = \Delta\lambda / (2\sqrt{2\ln 2}) \approx \Delta\lambda / 2.355$, using `scipy.optimize.curve_fit` to extract $\lambda_{peak}$ and $\Delta\lambda$ at each temperature. Plot $\lambda_{peak}$ versus $T_J$ to observe the red-shift with increasing temperature, typically +0.1 nm/°C for red LEDs, arising from bandgap shrinkage described by the Varshni equation: $E_g(T) = E_{g,0} - \alpha T^2 / (T + \beta)$, where $E_{g,0}$ is the zero-temperature bandgap, and $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are material-dependent constants. For AlGaInP at 630 nm, the bandgap at 300 K is approximately 1.97 eV, and the temperature coefficient $dE_g/dT \approx -0.4$ meV/K, corresponding to $d\lambda/dT \approx +\lambda^2 / (hc) \times |dE_g/dT| \approx (630 \times 10^{-9})^2 / (6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times 3 \times 10^8) \times 0.4 \times 10^{-3} \approx 0.1$ nm/°C, consistent with empirical observation.

Next, characterize the photodiode's spectral responsivity across the visible and near-infrared spectrum to understand how quantum efficiency $\eta(\lambda)$ varies with photon energy. The responsivity $\mathcal{R}(\lambda)$ (in A/W) relates photocurrent to incident optical power: $I_{ph} = \mathcal{R}(\lambda) \times P_{opt}$, and is proportional to quantum efficiency: $\mathcal{R}(\lambda) = \eta(\lambda) \times (q\lambda) / (hc)$, where $q$ is elementary charge, $h$ is Planck's constant, and $c$ is the speed of light. For an ideal photodiode with $\eta = 1.0$ (one electron-hole pair generated per incident photon), the responsivity increases linearly with wavelength: $\mathcal{R}_{ideal}(\lambda) = \lambda / 1240$ A/W, where $\lambda$ is in nanometers (derived from $hc/q \approx 1240$ eV·nm). Silicon photodiodes exhibit peak quantum efficiency near 800–900 nm ($\eta \approx 0.8$), decreasing toward shorter wavelengths due to surface recombination and toward longer wavelengths beyond 1100 nm where photon energy falls below the silicon bandgap (1.12 eV).

Set up a monochromator or tunable light source covering 400–1100 nm in 25 nm steps. If a monochromator is unavailable, use a set of narrowband LEDs spanning the spectrum: violet (405 nm), blue (470 nm), green (525 nm), yellow (590 nm), red (630 nm), deep red (660 nm), and near-infrared (850 nm, 940 nm). For each wavelength, illuminate the silicon photodiode (e.g., Hamamatsu S1336-BQ, 5.8 mm × 5.8 mm active area) with a known optical power $P_{opt}$ measured using a calibrated reference power meter (e.g., Thorlabs PM100D with S120C sensor). Connect the photodiode in photovoltaic mode (cathode to virtual ground of a transimpedance amplifier, anode grounded) to minimize dark current and maximize linearity. Use a low-noise operational amplifier (e.g., OPA129, input bias current < 100 fA) with a 1 MΩ feedback resistor, yielding transimpedance gain $G = 10^6$ V/A (1 V output per microamp photocurrent). Record the photodiode output voltage $V_{out}$ and calculate photocurrent $I_{ph} = V_{out} / G$. Divide photocurrent by incident optical power to obtain measured responsivity: $\mathcal{R}_{meas}(\lambda) = I_{ph} / P_{opt}$. Repeat at each wavelength, compiling a table of $\lambda$ (nm) versus $\mathcal{R}_{meas}$ (A/W). Compute quantum efficiency from responsivity: $\eta(\lambda) = \mathcal{R}_{meas}(\lambda) \times (hc) / (q\lambda) = \mathcal{R}_{meas}(\lambda) \times 1240 / \lambda$. Plot $\mathcal{R}_{meas}(\lambda)$ and $\eta(\lambda)$ versus wavelength, overlaying the manufacturer's datasheet curve for validation. Silicon photodiodes typically exhibit $\eta \approx 0.3$ at 400 nm (blue), $\eta \approx 0.7$ at 630 nm (red), peak $\eta \approx 0.9$ at 850 nm (near-infrared), and $\eta < 0.1$ beyond 1050 nm where absorption depth exceeds the depletion width.

For high-speed modulation bandwidth characterization, construct an LED-photodiode optical link and measure the small-signal frequency response using a network analyzer or an oscilloscope with FFT capability. Replace the 5 mm through-hole LED with a high-speed surface-mount LED (e.g., Kingbright APT2012F3C, 1 MHz typical bandwidth) or a red laser diode (e.g., 650 nm DVD laser diode, 200 MHz bandwidth) driven by a bias tee that superimposes DC bias current with AC modulation. The bias tee consists of a DC blocking capacitor (10 μF) in series with the AC signal path and an RF choke inductor (10 μH) in series with the DC bias current, allowing independent control of DC operating point and AC modulation amplitude. Set the DC bias current to 20 mA using a lab power supply and current-limiting resistor connected through the bias tee's DC port. Connect a waveform generator (Tektronix AFG3102, 100 MHz bandwidth) to the bias tee's RF port, applying a small-signal sinusoidal modulation with amplitude 2 mA peak-to-peak (10% modulation depth relative to 20 mA DC bias) and frequency swept from 100 Hz to 100 MHz in logarithmic steps (10 points per decade). Position a fast silicon photodiode (Thorlabs DET10A, 1 GHz bandwidth, 0.8 A/W responsivity at 650 nm) 2 cm from the LED/laser diode, capturing the modulated optical signal. Connect the photodiode output to a 50-ohm transimpedance amplifier or directly to a 1 GHz oscilloscope (Tektronix MDO3104, 50-ohm input impedance) operating in AC coupling mode.

At each modulation frequency, measure the peak-to-peak amplitude of the photodiode output voltage $V_{pp}(\omega)$ using the oscilloscope's AC RMS measurement function or by capturing the waveform and computing $V_{pp} = \max(V_{out}) - \min(V_{out})$ in Python. Normalize the amplitude to the low-frequency reference value $V_{pp,0}$ measured at 100 Hz, where the LED response is flat and limited only by DC current-to-light conversion efficiency. The normalized frequency response is $H(f) = V_{pp}(f) / V_{pp,0}$, expressed in decibels: $H_{dB}(f) = 20 \log_{10}(H(f))$. Plot $H_{dB}(f)$ versus frequency on a semilog scale (frequency on log axis, magnitude in dB on linear axis). The -3 dB bandwidth $f_{-3dB}$ is the frequency at which $H_{dB}(f_{-3dB}) = -3$ dB, corresponding to $H(f_{-3dB}) = 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707$. For a single-pole low-pass system (first-order RC response), the transfer function is $H(f) = 1 / \sqrt{1 + (f/f_{-3dB})^2}$, and the slope beyond the corner frequency is -20 dB/decade. Fit this model to the measured data using nonlinear regression to extract $f_{-3dB}$. Standard 5 mm LEDs exhibit $f_{-3dB} \approx 1$ MHz, limited by minority carrier recombination lifetime $\tau_r \approx 1/(2\pi f_{-3dB}) \approx 160$ ns. High-speed LEDs achieve 10–50 MHz bandwidth by reducing active region volume and increasing doping concentration, while laser diodes exceed 1 GHz through stimulated emission dynamics and low junction capacitance.

To isolate the contributions of LED rise time and photodiode bandwidth, measure the optical pulse response in the time domain. Replace the sinusoidal modulation with a square wave at 1 MHz (50% duty cycle) and capture the photodiode output on the oscilloscope, triggering on the rising edge. Measure the 10%-to-90% rise time $t_r$ and fall time $t_f$ of the optical waveform. The rise time relates to bandwidth as $t_r \approx 0.35 / f_{-3dB}$ for a first-order system, providing a quick estimate of modulation bandwidth without sweeping frequency. For the 1 MHz LED, expect $t_r \approx 350$ ns. If the measured rise time is faster, the photodiode or oscilloscope bandwidth is limiting; if slower, the LED is the bottleneck. Repeat the measurement with the fast photodiode replaced by the slow silicon photodiode from Exercise 1 (typical bandwidth 10 kHz) to observe bandwidth-limited pulse distortion.

Compile the empirical data into a comprehensive characterization report documenting temperature coefficients, spectral responsivity curves, and modulation bandwidth for the optoelectronic devices under test. Generate parametric plots showing optical power versus junction temperature with linear fit and extracted temperature coefficient $\alpha_P$; peak wavelength versus temperature with linear fit and extracted spectral shift $d\lambda/dT$; photodiode responsivity and quantum efficiency versus wavelength with comparison to datasheet; and frequency response magnitude plot with annotated -3 dB bandwidth. Include tabulated numerical data with uncertainties: for optical power measurements, ±2% from photodiode calibration error; for spectral measurements, ±0.5 nm from spectrometer resolution; for bandwidth measurements, ±5% from oscilloscope timebase accuracy and signal-to-noise ratio. This dataset enables worst-case design analysis: if the LED will operate over an industrial temperature range of -40°C to +85°C, and optical output decreases by 0.5%/°C, budget a 62.5% reduction in optical power at the hot extreme relative to room temperature, requiring a corresponding increase in drive current or reduction in link loss to maintain adequate signal-to-noise ratio at the receiver.

## Exercise 3: Stress Testing, Boundary Failure & Recovery Protocol

The final exercise subjects optoelectronic devices to extreme operating conditions to identify failure mechanisms, quantify reliability margins, and establish safe operating area (SOA) boundaries. You will drive an LED beyond its maximum rated current until catastrophic thermal runaway, forward-bias a laser diode without current limiting to induce catastrophic optical damage (COD), reverse-bias a photodiode to avalanche breakdown voltage to measure dark current multiplication, and thermally cycle an optocoupler to accelerate solder joint fatigue and package delamination. These destructive tests are not academic exercises; they replicate field failure modes caused by transient overvoltage spikes, inadequate thermal management, and manufacturing defects, providing quantitative data for derating guidelines, fault protection circuits, and accelerated life testing models. The goal is to witness failure firsthand—to hear the audible pop of junction burnout, observe the non-recoverable degradation of optical output, and correlate failure with measurable precursors like forward voltage shift and thermal resistance increase—transforming abstract reliability statistics into tactile, memorable lessons that inform conservative design practices.

Begin with LED overcurrent stress testing to characterize thermal runaway and junction burnout. Select an inexpensive 5 mm red LED (not the calibrated device from Exercises 1–2; use a sacrificial unit) with datasheet maximum continuous forward current $I_{F,max} = 30$ mA and peak pulsed current $I_{F,peak} = 100$ mA (10% duty cycle, 10 μs pulse width). Mount the LED on a breadboard with a series ammeter (DMM in current mode, 200 mA range) and a parallel voltmeter (second DMM across LED terminals, 20 V range). Use a lab power supply configured for voltage control mode (not current limit mode) to enable overcurrent conditions; set the output voltage to 3.0 V and monitor the forward current. Gradually increase the supply voltage in 0.5 V increments every 30 seconds, recording forward current $I_F$ and forward voltage $V_F$ at each step. As current exceeds the rated maximum, junction temperature rises due to power dissipation $P_{diss} = V_F \times I_F$. The forward voltage $V_F$ initially decreases with temperature (negative temperature coefficient, approximately -2 mV/°C for silicon and GaAs diodes), but as thermal resistance $\Theta_{JA}$ drives junction temperature toward the maximum rated value (typically 100–150°C for epoxy-packaged LEDs), the decreasing $V_F$ allows even higher current to flow, creating positive feedback. This thermal runaway accelerates until the junction reaches the intrinsic temperature limit (approximately 200°C for AlGaInP), at which point non-radiative recombination dominates, optical output collapses, and the junction metallization or bond wire fuses open, resulting in permanent open-circuit failure.

Monitor the LED optical output throughout the stress test using the calibrated silicon photodiode positioned 10 cm away. Plot optical power $P_{opt}$ versus forward current $I_F$ in real time, observing that optical efficiency $\eta_{ext} = P_{opt} / (V_F \times I_F)$ peaks at moderate current (around 20 mA) and decreases at high current due to efficiency droop and junction heating. Continue increasing supply voltage until the LED fails catastrophically—either an open circuit (infinite resistance, zero current) or a short circuit (forward voltage drops to near zero, current limited only by series resistance). The failure mode depends on the specific mechanism: bond wire fusing produces an open circuit, while junction metallization melting or electromigration-induced shorting produces a low-resistance path. Record the failure current $I_{F,fail}$ and failure voltage $V_{supply,fail}$; typical 5 mm LEDs fail between 100–200 mA continuous forward current when driven without active current regulation. After failure, inspect the LED under a microscope to identify physical damage: charred epoxy lens, discolored die surface, or broken bond wire. This empirical data quantifies the margin between rated maximum current (30 mA) and actual failure threshold (150 mA), supporting a derating guideline of 50–60% (operate at ≤18 mA for long-term reliability).

Next, characterize laser diode catastrophic optical damage (COD) by forward-biasing a low-cost red DVD laser diode (650 nm, 5 mW rated output power) without current limiting. Laser diodes are orders of magnitude more sensitive to overcurrent than LEDs due to optical power density concentration at the cleaved facet: a 1 μm × 3 μm emitting aperture confines megawatts per square centimeter of optical intensity, and any surface contamination, crystal defect, or localized heating initiates a runaway absorption process where the semiconductor material melts, forming a non-radiative recombination center that propagates inward along the cavity axis, permanently destroying the laser within microseconds. This failure mode is called catastrophic optical damage (COD), and it is the primary reliability concern for laser diodes in commercial applications.

**WARNING: Laser diodes are ESD-sensitive and require current-limited power supplies. This experiment intentionally induces failure for educational purposes; use a sacrificial laser diode and proper eye protection (laser safety goggles rated OD 5+ at 650 nm). Never look directly into the laser output, even at sub-milliwatt power levels.**

Mount the laser diode in a TO-can package with anode, cathode, and case pins identified via datasheet or multimeter diode test (forward voltage drop approximately 1.5–2.0 V between anode and cathode, infinite resistance between all other pin pairs). Connect the cathode to ground and the anode to a lab power supply through a series ammeter (DMM, 200 mA range). Position a calibrated photodiode 10 cm from the laser output aperture, aligned along the beam axis. Set the power supply to 0 V and enable the output. Gradually increase the voltage in 0.1 V increments every 5 seconds, monitoring forward current $I_F$ and optical power $P_{opt}$. The laser diode exhibits an exponential I-V curve similar to an LED at low currents (below threshold), but once the forward current reaches the threshold current $I_{th}$ (typically 20–50 mA for red DVD laser diodes), stimulated emission begins, and optical power increases sharply with current. The slope efficiency $\eta_{slope}$ (in mW/mA) is the derivative $dP_{opt}/dI_F$ in the lasing region, typically 0.2–0.5 mW/mA for red laser diodes.

Continue increasing current beyond the rated maximum (typically 60 mA continuous for a 5 mW laser diode) until COD occurs. The failure is abrupt: optical power collapses to near zero (residual LED-like emission from spontaneous recombination remains), and forward voltage shifts to a higher value due to increased series resistance from the damaged facet region. Record the failure current $I_{F,COD}$, typically 100–200 mA for unprotected DVD laser diodes. After failure, measure the laser diode's forward voltage at 10 mA; it will have increased by 0.5–1.0 V relative to the pre-failure value, indicating irreversible junction damage. Attempt to operate the laser diode at the original rated current (40 mA); optical output will be < 10% of the original power, confirming permanent degradation. Disassemble the TO-can package (carefully grind away the metal cap or use a laser diode opener tool) and inspect the facet under a microscope. The COD damage appears as a dark spot or line at the facet, representing melted or amorphized semiconductor material.

This destructive test demonstrates why laser diodes must never be driven without active current regulation and protection circuits. Commercial laser diode drivers incorporate fast-acting current limiters (sub-microsecond response time), slow-start circuits to prevent inrush current spikes during power-on, and photodiode feedback loops that adjust drive current to maintain constant optical output despite temperature drift and aging. The lesson is visceral: one momentary overcurrent event—lasting microseconds—irreversibly destroys the device, and no amount of subsequent careful operation can recover the lost performance.

For photodiode avalanche breakdown and dark current characterization, reverse-bias a silicon photodiode (same device used in Exercises 1–2) to its breakdown voltage and measure the dark current multiplication factor. Photodiodes operated in photoconductive mode (reverse-biased) exhibit extremely low dark current (picoamperes to nanoamperes) due to thermal generation of electron-hole pairs in the depletion region. As reverse voltage approaches the breakdown voltage $V_{BR}$ (typically 20–100 V for silicon photodiodes, depending on doping concentration and junction depth), impact ionization multiplies the dark current exponentially: accelerated carriers collide with lattice atoms, liberating additional electron-hole pairs in an avalanche process, increasing dark current by orders of magnitude. While avalanche photodiodes (APDs) exploit this multiplication for high-sensitivity detection, standard photodiodes operated near breakdown exhibit noise and nonlinearity that degrade signal-to-noise ratio.

Connect the photodiode in reverse bias with cathode to a variable high-voltage power supply (0–100 V, < 1 mA current limit) and anode grounded. Ensure the test is conducted in complete darkness (shield the photodiode with opaque enclosure or conduct in a dark room) to eliminate photocurrent. Connect a picoammeter or electrometer (Keithley 6485, 1 fA resolution) in series to measure dark current $I_{dark}$. Sweep reverse voltage from 0 V to 90% of the datasheet breakdown voltage in 2 V increments, recording $I_{dark}$ versus $V_R$. At low voltages (below 10 V), dark current remains nearly constant, dominated by surface leakage and bulk thermal generation: $I_{dark,0} \approx q \times n_i^2 \times V_{dep} \times A / N_D$, where $n_i$ is intrinsic carrier concentration, $V_{dep}$ is depletion volume, $A$ is active area, and $N_D$ is donor doping concentration. As reverse voltage increases, depletion width expands, increasing thermal generation volume and causing dark current to rise gradually (square-root dependence on voltage). Near breakdown, impact ionization causes exponential increase: $I_{dark}(V_R) = I_{dark,0} \times M(V_R)$, where $M(V_R) = 1 / (1 - (V_R / V_{BR})^n)$ is the avalanche multiplication factor, with exponent $n \approx 3$–6 for silicon. Plot $I_{dark}$ versus $V_R$ on a log-linear scale (log $I_{dark}$, linear $V_R$) to identify the breakdown onset where the slope increases sharply. Measure the voltage at which $I_{dark}$ exceeds 100 nA, defining the practical operating limit for low-noise photodetection. For the Hamamatsu S1336-BQ photodiode, expect $V_{BR} \approx 60$ V and $I_{dark} < 1$ nA at $V_R = 10$ V, increasing to 10 nA at $V_R = 50$ V.

Finally, perform thermal cycling stress testing on a PC817 optocoupler (industry-standard 4-pin DIP package containing an infrared LED coupled to a phototransistor) to accelerate solder joint fatigue and package delamination, common failure modes in automotive and industrial applications subject to extreme temperature swings. The thermal cycle test subjects the device to repeated transitions between cold (-40°C) and hot (+125°C) extremes, inducing mechanical stress from coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) mismatch between the silicon die (CTE ≈ 2.6 ppm/°C), copper leadframe (CTE ≈ 17 ppm/°C), and epoxy molding compound (CTE ≈ 15–25 ppm/°C). Each thermal cycle induces strain accumulation at the die attach and bond wire interfaces, eventually causing cracks, delamination, or wire lift-off after hundreds to thousands of cycles, depending on the temperature range and ramp rate.

Construct a simple optocoupler test circuit on a PCB or breadboard. Connect the PC817 input LED in series with a 330-ohm resistor and a 5 V power supply, setting the forward current to approximately 10 mA. Connect the output phototransistor in common-emitter configuration: collector to +5 V through a 1 kΩ pull-up resistor, emitter to ground, and monitor the collector voltage (output state: low when LED is on, high when LED is off). Mount the PCB in a programmable temperature chamber (Cincinnati Sub-Zero Z-Plus series or equivalent) with integrated data logging. Program a thermal cycle profile: -40°C hold for 15 minutes, ramp to +125°C at 10°C/minute, hold for 15 minutes, ramp to -40°C at 10°C/minute, repeat for 500 cycles. Continuously monitor the optocoupler transfer characteristics throughout the test: at the end of each cycle (when temperature returns to +25°C), measure the current transfer ratio (CTR) defined as $\text{CTR} = (I_C / I_F) \times 100\%$, where $I_F$ is LED forward current (10 mA) and $I_C$ is phototransistor collector current. To measure $I_C$, replace the 1 kΩ pull-up with a 100-ohm sense resistor and measure voltage drop: $I_C = V_{sense} / 100$. Initial CTR for a new PC817 is typically 80–160% (0.8–1.6 mA collector current per 1 mA LED current); datasheet minimum is 50%.

Plot CTR versus cycle number, observing gradual degradation: after 100 cycles, CTR may decrease by 5–10%; after 500 cycles, 20–30% degradation is common. The degradation mechanism includes bond wire fatigue cracking (reducing electrical continuity), die attach delamination (increasing thermal resistance and junction temperature, reducing LED efficiency), and epoxy lens yellowing from UV exposure and oxidation (reducing optical coupling efficiency). If CTR drops below 50% of initial value, the optocoupler is considered failed. After thermal cycling, perform destructive physical analysis (DPA): carefully decapsulate the optocoupler by immersing in fuming nitric acid for 4 hours (fume hood required, extreme caution), rinsing, and inspecting under scanning electron microscope (SEM) to identify crack propagation at bond wire heel, die attach voiding, or leadframe corrosion.

For each stress test, compile a failure analysis report documenting the failure mode (open circuit, short circuit, parametric drift), failure threshold (current, voltage, or cycle count), and visual evidence (photographs, SEM micrographs). Compare empirical failure data against industry reliability models: for thermal cycling, the Coffin-Manson equation predicts cycles to failure $N_f = C \times (\Delta T)^{-m}$, where $\Delta T$ is temperature swing, $m \approx 2.5$ for solder joints, and $C$ is a material constant; for laser diode COD, failure rate follows a log-normal distribution with median time-to-failure inversely proportional to optical power density squared. These empirical data validate derating guidelines: operate LEDs at ≤50% of rated current, laser diodes at ≤70% of maximum power with active current limiting, photodiodes at ≤50% of breakdown voltage, and design thermal management to keep junction temperature below 100°C even under worst-case ambient conditions.

**Derating Guidelines for Long-Term Reliability (>10 Years MTBF):**

- **LED forward current:** Operate at ≤50% of rated $I_{F,max}$. Example: 30 mA rated → 15 mA operating current.
- **Laser diode output power:** Operate at ≤70% of rated maximum. Example: 5 mW rated → 3.5 mW operating power.
- **Photodiode reverse bias:** Operate at ≤50% of breakdown voltage $V_{BR}$. Example: 60 V breakdown → 30 V maximum bias.
- **Junction temperature:** Maintain $T_J < 100$°C under worst-case ambient conditions. Use heatsinks, forced air cooling, or reduce drive current.
- **Optocoupler CTR margin:** Design circuits with ≥2× margin on minimum CTR. Example: 50% minimum CTR → design assumes 100% initial, tolerates 50% degradation.

**Diagnostic Flowchart for Intermittent Optical Link Failures:**

1. **Symptom: Intermittent signal loss or high bit-error rate.**
   - Measure received optical power with power meter. If power fluctuates >1 dB over minutes, suspect mechanical vibration causing fiber misalignment or connector contamination.
   - Clean all fiber connectors with lint-free swabs and isopropyl alcohol. Re-measure insertion loss; should be < 0.5 dB per connection.
   - If power stable but BER high, suspect insufficient signal-to-noise ratio. Measure receiver sensitivity (minimum optical power for BER < 10^-9). If received power is within 3 dB of sensitivity, increase transmitter power or reduce link loss.

2. **Symptom: Gradual optical power degradation over weeks/months.**
   - Measure LED or laser diode forward voltage at constant current. If $V_F$ increases by >10%, suspect bond wire degradation or die attach failure.
   - Perform thermal imaging of transmitter module. If junction temperature exceeds 100°C, improve thermal management: add heatsink, increase airflow, or reduce drive current.
   - Measure optocoupler CTR if used in feedback loop. If CTR decreased by >30%, replace optocoupler.

3. **Symptom: Catastrophic failure (no optical output).**
   - Measure LED/laser diode forward voltage at 1 mA test current. If $V_F$ near zero, junction shorted (COD or ESD damage). If $V_F > 10$ V, junction open (bond wire fused).
   - Inspect transmitter and receiver for physical damage: burnt components, discolored epoxy, cracked fiber.
   - Check for reverse polarity connection or transient overvoltage event (lightning strike, inductive kick-back from relay coil). Add TVS diodes for transient protection.

4. **Symptom: Temperature-dependent performance (works cold, fails hot or vice versa).**
   - Measure L-I curve at multiple temperatures (-20°C, +25°C, +85°C). If optical power drops >50% at temperature extremes, increase drive current or use temperature compensation circuit (thermistor in bias feedback loop).
   - Check for thermal runaway: if forward current increases uncontrollably with temperature (due to negative $V_F$ tempco), add active current regulation circuit (LM317 or dedicated LED driver IC).

**Safety and Handling Checklist for Laser Diodes:**

- [ ] Laser safety goggles worn (OD rating matches wavelength and power class)
- [ ] ESD wrist strap connected to ground
- [ ] Current-limited power supply configured (maximum current set to 1.5× $I_{th}$, never direct voltage source)
- [ ] Beam dump or optical power meter positioned to capture output beam (never allow uncontrolled reflections)
- [ ] Interlock system armed if Class 3B or Class 4 laser (door switch cuts power when enclosure opened)
- [ ] Slow-start circuit implemented (ramp drive current over 1 ms minimum to prevent inrush spike)
- [ ] Photodiode feedback loop active (monitor output power and reduce drive current if power exceeds setpoint)
- [ ] Junction temperature monitored (thermistor or thermal camera confirms $T_J < 60$°C for CW operation)

**Empirical Rules of Thumb for Optoelectronic Circuit Design:**

- **LED current-limiting resistor:** $R = (V_{supply} - V_F) / I_F$, where $V_F$ is forward voltage at target $I_F$. Always verify power dissipation: $P_R = I_F^2 \times R$ must be less than resistor wattage rating.
- **Photodiode transimpedance gain:** For low-noise operation, choose feedback resistor $R_f = 10^6$ to $10^7$ ohms and minimize parasitic capacitance (short PCB traces, guard rings). Bandwidth $f_{-3dB} = 1 / (2\pi R_f C_{total})$, where $C_{total} = C_{photodiode} + C_{opamp} + C_{PCB}$.
- **Fiber-optic link margin:** Design for minimum 6 dB system margin: $P_{TX} - \text{Loss}_{total} - P_{RX,sensitivity} \geq 6$ dB, where $\text{Loss}_{total}$ includes fiber attenuation, connector insertion loss, splice loss, and aging allowance (1 dB).
- **Laser diode protection:** Always include series resistor (10–50 ohms) and parallel Schottky diode (reverse-biased, anode to ground, cathode to laser anode) to clamp reverse voltage transients below 0.3 V. Add series inductor (10 μH) to limit $di/dt$ during power-on.

This operational reference provides the empirical foundation for translating laboratory measurements into robust field-deployable optoelectronic systems, with quantitative margins that account for component variation, environmental stress, and aging degradation over the product lifetime.
