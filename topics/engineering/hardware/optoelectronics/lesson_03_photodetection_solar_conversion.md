# Lesson 3: Photodetection and Solar Conversion

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The conversion of optical radiation into electrical signals represents one of the most fundamental operations in optoelectronics, bridging the electromagnetic spectrum to the domain of electronic circuits. Unlike passive optical components that merely guide or manipulate photons, photodetectors and solar cells perform the active quantum mechanical transaction of photon annihilation coupled with electron-hole pair generation. This transduction process operates at the intersection of solid-state physics, semiconductor band theory, and electromagnetism, demanding precise understanding of carrier dynamics within crystalline lattices under optical illumination.

At the heart of all photodetection lies the photoelectric effect, first explained comprehensively by Albert Einstein in 1905. When a photon with energy $E = h\nu$ strikes a semiconductor material, it may be absorbed if its energy exceeds the bandgap energy $E_g$. This absorption event promotes an electron from the valence band to the conduction band, simultaneously creating a hole in the valence band. The resulting electron-hole pair constitutes the primary photocarrier generation event. The quantum efficiency of this process—the probability that an incident photon generates a collectable carrier pair—fundamentally limits all downstream performance metrics.

The photodiode, in its simplest manifestation, consists of a p-n junction operated under reverse bias. The depletion region formed at this junction creates a built-in electric field that sweeps photogenerated carriers toward their respective collection terminals before recombination can occur. Electrons drift toward the n-side anode while holes migrate toward the p-side cathode, generating a photocurrent proportional to incident optical power. This proportionality constant, termed responsivity and measured in amperes per watt, encapsulates the device's conversion efficiency across the wavelength spectrum of interest.

Several critical invariants govern photodetector performance regardless of specific device architecture. First, the absorption depth must be matched to the depletion width to ensure photogenerated carriers experience sufficient electric field for collection before recombination. Silicon photodiodes, for instance, exhibit optimal responsivity near 800–900 nm where absorption depth and junction engineering align favorably. Second, the transit time of carriers across the depletion region establishes an upper frequency limit for optical modulation detection. High-speed photodiodes minimize this transit time through thin depletion regions, trading off quantum efficiency for bandwidth. Third, thermal noise, shot noise, and dark current collectively determine the noise equivalent power (NEP), the minimum detectable optical signal. Cooling reduces dark current exponentially but cannot eliminate the fundamental shot noise arising from the discrete nature of charge carriers.

Solar cells operate on identical photocarrier generation principles but optimize for power extraction rather than signal fidelity. The critical distinction lies in operating point: photodiodes typically operate under reverse bias in the photoconductive regime, while solar cells operate near forward bias at the maximum power point where the product of voltage and current reaches its peak. The Shockley-Queisser limit, derived from detailed balance considerations of photon absorption and radiative recombination, establishes a theoretical efficiency ceiling of approximately 33.7% for single-junction cells under unconcentrated terrestrial sunlight. This limit arises from fundamental thermodynamic constraints: photons with energy below the bandgap pass through unabsorbed, while excess energy from above-bandgap photons thermalizes to the band edge, dissipating as heat.

The quantum efficiency metric bifurcates into external quantum efficiency (EQE) and internal quantum efficiency (IQE). External quantum efficiency accounts for all incident photons, including those reflected from the device surface or absorbed in inactive regions. Internal quantum efficiency considers only photons absorbed in the active junction region. The ratio EQE/IQE quantifies optical losses from reflection and parasitic absorption. Anti-reflection coatings, typically consisting of quarter-wave dielectric stacks, minimize front-surface reflection across the spectral band of interest. Silicon solar cells commonly employ silicon nitride (SiₓNᵧ) with refractive index near 2.0, providing optimal impedance matching between air (n ≈ 1.0) and silicon (n ≈ 3.5).

Responsivity, expressed mathematically as $R(\lambda) = \frac{I_{ph}}{P_{opt}} = \frac{\eta(\lambda) q \lambda}{hc}$, reveals the wavelength-dependent conversion relationship. Here $\eta(\lambda)$ represents the wavelength-specific quantum efficiency, $q$ denotes the elementary charge (1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C), $\lambda$ is the optical wavelength, $h$ is Planck's constant (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s), and $c$ is the speed of light (2.998 × 10⁸ m/s). For an ideal photodetector with unity quantum efficiency, responsivity increases linearly with wavelength up to the bandgap cutoff wavelength $\lambda_g = \frac{hc}{E_g}$. Silicon photodiodes (Eg ≈ 1.12 eV) therefore exhibit maximum responsivity near 1100 nm, while InGaAs devices (Eg ≈ 0.75 eV) extend response to approximately 1650 nm, covering the telecommunications C-band.

The junction capacitance $C_j$ introduces a critical frequency-dependent impedance that limits high-speed operation. This capacitance arises from the charge storage in the depletion region and scales inversely with depletion width according to $C_j = \epsilon A / W$, where $\epsilon$ represents the semiconductor permittivity, $A$ the junction area, and $W$ the depletion width. The resulting RC time constant, formed with the load resistance $R_L$, establishes a bandwidth limitation $f_{3dB} = \frac{1}{2\pi R_L C_j}$. High-speed photodetectors employ small active areas to minimize capacitance, often utilizing traveling-wave geometries or resonant circuit matching to extend bandwidth beyond the simple RC limit.

Noise mechanisms in photodetectors partition into fundamental quantum noise and extrinsic noise sources. Shot noise, arising from the discrete nature of charge carrier flow, manifests as a current fluctuation with spectral density $i_{shot}^2 = 2q(I_{ph} + I_{dark})B$, where $B$ represents the measurement bandwidth and $I_{dark}$ the dark current flowing in the absence of illumination. Dark current originates from thermal generation of carriers within the depletion region and surface leakage paths, decreasing exponentially with temperature according to $I_{dark} \propto \exp(-E_g/2kT)$. Thermal noise from the load resistance contributes additional current fluctuation with spectral density $i_{thermal}^2 = 4kTB/R_L$. Low-noise photodetection demands careful optimization of load resistance, operating temperature, and transimpedance amplifier design to position the combined noise floor below the minimum signal level of interest.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The semiconductor p-n junction photodiode architecture consists of distinct regions, each contributing specific functionality to the photoconversion process. Beginning at the optical entrance surface, a thin heavily-doped p⁺ or n⁺ layer (typically 0.1–0.5 μm thick) forms the ohmic contact while minimizing absorption in this electrically inactive region. Below this contact layer lies the lightly-doped absorption region where the majority of photocarrier generation occurs. This intrinsic or lightly-doped layer, ranging from 2 to 50 μm depending on wavelength optimization, must be thick enough to absorb the incident radiation yet thin enough to maintain reasonable transit times. The junction terminates in a heavily-doped substrate providing mechanical support and the back contact.

Under reverse bias operation, the depletion region expands into the lightly-doped absorption layer, creating an electric field typically on the order of 10⁴ to 10⁵ V/cm. This field magnitude ensures that photogenerated carriers experience drift transport rather than slow diffusion. Carrier drift velocity in silicon saturates near 10⁷ cm/s at fields above approximately 10⁴ V/cm, establishing transit times in the picosecond range for modern thin-depletion structures. The applied reverse bias voltage must exceed the built-in potential (typically 0.7–0.9 V for silicon) to fully deplete the absorption region, but excessive bias risks avalanche breakdown and increased dark current from field-enhanced generation mechanisms.

The PIN photodiode architecture inserts an intrinsic (or lightly-doped) region between heavily-doped p⁺ and n⁺ layers, providing independent control over absorption depth and junction capacitance. This structure dominates high-speed telecommunications applications where the thick intrinsic layer (10–50 μm) ensures high quantum efficiency while the wide depletion region minimizes capacitance. The InGaAs/InP PIN photodiode, lattice-matched at the composition In₀.₅₃Ga₀.₄₇As, provides optimal responsivity across the 1300–1550 nm telecommunications bands. The InGaAs absorption layer (2–4 μm typical) sits atop a transparent InP substrate, with epitaxial growth controlling the precise doping profiles and interface abruptness.

Avalanche photodiodes (APDs) introduce internal current gain through impact ionization within a high-field multiplication region. When accelerated carriers acquire sufficient kinetic energy—typically 1.5–2 times the bandgap energy—they can ionize additional valence electrons through inelastic collisions, creating secondary electron-hole pairs. These secondary carriers undergo further acceleration and ionization, resulting in an avalanche multiplication process. The multiplication factor $M$ follows the relation $M = \frac{1}{1 - (V/V_{BR})^n}$, where $V$ represents the applied reverse bias, $V_{BR}$ the breakdown voltage, and $n$ an empirically determined exponent (typically 3–6 for silicon APDs). Commercial silicon APDs achieve gain factors of 50–200 before excess noise renders further multiplication counterproductive.

The APD structure partitions into separate absorption and multiplication regions in the reach-through APD geometry. Photons absorb in a moderately-doped p-type layer where the electric field remains below the impact ionization threshold. Photogenerated electrons drift into a thin (0.5–2 μm) heavily-doped n-region where field strength exceeds 3 × 10⁵ V/cm, initiating avalanche multiplication. This separation allows independent optimization of absorption efficiency and multiplication gain. The reach-through design requires precise control of doping profiles to ensure the depletion region "reaches through" from the multiplication region into the absorption layer at the specified operating voltage.

Avalanche multiplication introduces excess noise beyond the shot noise of the primary photocurrent. This excess noise arises from the stochastic nature of the ionization process, where statistical fluctuations in multiplication factor contribute additional current variance. The noise power increases faster than signal power, degrading signal-to-noise ratio at high gain. The excess noise factor $F$ relates to the multiplication factor through $F = M^x$, where $x$ ranges from 0.3 to 0.7 depending on the ratio of electron and hole ionization coefficients (k = αₑ/αₕ). Silicon, with k ≈ 0.02, exhibits relatively low excess noise (x ≈ 0.3), while germanium and III-V compounds show higher excess noise due to more comparable ionization coefficients.

Solar cell architecture optimizes for maximum photocurrent generation across the broad solar spectrum (280–2500 nm) while minimizing resistive losses and recombination. The front surface features a heavily-doped n⁺ emitter (0.3–0.5 μm, Nₐ ≈ 10¹⁹ cm⁻³) forming a shallow junction with a lightly-doped p-type base (200–300 μm, Nₐ ≈ 10¹⁶ cm⁻³). This asymmetric doping profile positions the junction near the illuminated surface where the majority of short-wavelength photons absorb, while the thick base layer captures long-wavelength radiation with absorption lengths exceeding 100 μm. Surface texturing through anisotropic etching creates pyramidal structures (5–10 μm height) that reduce reflection through multiple bounce paths and increase effective optical path length through ray obliquity.

The metallization grid on the front surface must balance two competing requirements: sufficient metal coverage to minimize series resistance, yet minimal area blockage to maximize photon collection. The optimized finger pitch (spacing between parallel metal lines) emerges from detailed loss analysis balancing resistive losses in the silicon emitter layer against shading losses from metal coverage. Typical designs employ finger pitches of 1.5–2.5 mm with individual finger widths of 80–120 μm, achieving metal coverage fractions below 8%. The rear surface features full-area metallization, often incorporating a back-surface field (BSF) layer—a heavily-doped p⁺ region creating a potential barrier that reflects minority carriers away from the rear surface, reducing recombination losses.

The current-voltage relationship of an illuminated solar cell follows the diode equation modified by the photogenerated current: $I = I_0[\exp(qV/nkT) - 1] - I_{ph}$. Here $I_0$ represents the reverse saturation current (typically 10⁻¹² to 10⁻¹⁰ A/cm²), $n$ the ideality factor (1.0–1.5), $k$ Boltzmann's constant (1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K), and $T$ the absolute temperature. At short-circuit conditions (V = 0), the current equals the photocurrent $I_{sc} \approx I_{ph}$. At open-circuit conditions (I = 0), the voltage reaches $V_{oc} = \frac{nkT}{q}\ln\left(\frac{I_{ph}}{I_0} + 1\right)$. The maximum power point occurs at intermediate voltage and current values where the product IV maximizes, typically at Vₘₚ ≈ 0.8Vₒc and Iₘₚ ≈ 0.9Iₛc for high-quality silicon cells.

The fill factor FF quantifies the squareness of the I-V curve, defined as the ratio of maximum power to the product of short-circuit current and open-circuit voltage: $FF = \frac{I_{mp}V_{mp}}{I_{sc}V_{oc}}$. Ideal cells with negligible series resistance and infinite shunt resistance achieve fill factors approaching 0.85–0.89. Practical cells exhibit reduced fill factors due to resistive losses in the metal grid and emitter layer (series resistance Rₛ), and leakage currents through defects and cell edges (shunt resistance Rₛₕ). The overall conversion efficiency combines these factors: $\eta = \frac{FF \cdot I_{sc} \cdot V_{oc}}{P_{in}}$, where Pᵢₙ represents the incident solar power (typically 1000 W/m² for AM1.5G standard test conditions).

Carrier collection efficiency depends critically on minority carrier diffusion length $L = \sqrt{D\tau}$, where $D$ represents the diffusion coefficient and $\tau$ the minority carrier lifetime. In the p-type base of a silicon solar cell, electrons constitute the minority carriers with diffusion lengths typically ranging from 100 μm (Czochralski wafers) to over 1000 μm (float-zone material). Recombination mechanisms partition into radiative, Auger, and Shockley-Read-Hall (SRH) processes. Radiative recombination, the inverse of photon absorption, becomes significant only at high injection levels and represents a fundamental loss mechanism. Auger recombination, involving three-particle interactions where the recombination energy transfers to a third carrier, dominates in heavily-doped regions and at very high injection levels. SRH recombination through mid-gap defect states typically limits lifetime in practical devices, with transition metals (Fe, Cr, Cu) acting as particularly efficient recombination centers.

Surface recombination velocity $S$, measured in cm/s, quantifies carrier loss rate at semiconductor surfaces and interfaces. Bare silicon surfaces exhibit recombination velocities exceeding 10⁶ cm/s due to the high density of surface states (dangling bonds) within the bandgap. Thermal oxidation to form SiO₂ reduces surface recombination velocity to 10³–10⁴ cm/s through chemical passivation of dangling bonds. Modern high-efficiency cells employ additional dielectric passivation layers such as silicon nitride (SiNₓ) or aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) combined with hydrogen-rich cap layers, achieving surface recombination velocities below 10 cm/s on planar surfaces. The effective lifetime $\tau_{eff}$ combines bulk and surface recombination according to $\frac{1}{\tau_{eff}} = \frac{1}{\tau_{bulk}} + \frac{2S}{W}$, where $W$ represents the wafer thickness.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Achieving shot-noise-limited photodetection requires meticulous attention to amplifier design, operating point selection, and thermal management. The transimpedance amplifier (TIA), converting photodiode current to voltage, must present low input impedance to minimize bandwidth limitations from junction capacitance while providing sufficient gain to overwhelm subsequent amplifier noise. The classic inverting operational amplifier TIA configuration employs a feedback resistor $R_f$ setting the transimpedance gain (V/A). The input-referred current noise spectral density combines contributions from the feedback resistor thermal noise, photodiode shot and dark noise, and amplifier voltage and current noise: $i_{n,total}^2 = \frac{4kT}{R_f} + 2q(I_{ph} + I_{dark}) + \frac{e_{n,amp}^2}{R_f^2} + i_{n,amp}^2$. Minimizing total noise demands careful selection of $R_f$ balancing thermal noise (decreasing with increasing $R_f$) against bandwidth (decreasing with increasing $R_f$ due to feedback pole formation).

High-speed photoreceivers operating beyond 10 Gb/s must address parasitics and packaging constraints that dominate performance at these data rates. Wirebond inductance (typically 0.5–1 nH per mm) forms a parasitic series impedance that resonates with junction capacitance, introducing peaking in the frequency response. Modern high-speed photodetectors employ flip-chip bonding or direct fiber attachment to minimize parasitic inductance. The photodiode equivalent circuit includes not only junction capacitance but also series resistance from the contact layers and bulk semiconductor, typically 5–50 Ω depending on device geometry. This series resistance combines with the TIA input impedance to form a voltage divider that reduces responsivity at high frequencies.

Optical power handling limits emerge from several physical mechanisms. At moderate power levels (1–10 mW), thermal heating from photocurrent dissipation raises junction temperature, increasing dark current and shifting device characteristics. The thermal resistance from junction to heatsink, typically 50–200 K/W for packaged devices, determines the temperature rise $\Delta T = P_{dissipated} \cdot R_{thermal}$. High-power photodiodes incorporate backside metallization on thermally conductive substrates (AlN, BeO, diamond) to minimize thermal resistance. At higher power levels (10–100 mW), space-charge effects become significant. The photocurrent density creates a voltage drop across the depleted region that reduces the electric field, decreasing carrier drift velocity and increasing transit time. This space-charge screening effect eventually leads to gain compression where responsivity decreases with increasing optical power.

Catastrophic optical damage (COD) represents the ultimate power-handling limit, occurring when localized heating at defects or facets initiates a thermal runaway process. At optical intensities exceeding approximately 10⁶–10⁷ W/cm², defect sites experience rapid temperature increase due to enhanced absorption. The rising temperature increases the absorption coefficient through bandgap shrinkage, further accelerating heating in a positive feedback loop. Within nanoseconds, local melting occurs, permanently damaging the semiconductor. COD-limited power handling typically ranges from 10 mW for small-area high-speed photodiodes to over 1 W for large-area devices with careful thermal design. Preventing COD requires distributing optical power over sufficient area and ensuring excellent material quality to minimize defect-initiated damage.

Avalanche photodiode operation requires precision bias control to maintain stable gain. The multiplication factor's strong dependence on applied voltage—with typical slopes of 5–10%/volt near the operating point—demands bias regulation better than 10 mV to maintain gain stability within 1%. Temperature variation further complicates bias control: the breakdown voltage shifts by approximately 0.01–0.1%/K depending on semiconductor material. Silicon APDs require positive temperature compensation (increasing bias with temperature), while InGaAs APDs require negative compensation. Active bias control circuits monitor either the APD current or optical output from a reference photodiode to servo the bias voltage, compensating for both temperature drift and aging effects.

Excess noise factor optimization involves careful design of the multiplication region doping profile. Uniform multiplication regions exhibit higher excess noise than graded structures where field strength varies with position. The dead-space effect—the finite distance required for carriers to acquire sufficient energy for ionization—reduces excess noise in thin multiplication regions below 1 μm. This effect arises because carriers born near the entrance of the multiplication region experience less multiplication than those born deep within, reducing gain variance. Single-photon avalanche diodes (SPADs) exploit this effect in combination with above-breakdown bias operation to achieve gain factors exceeding 10⁶, enabling detection of individual photon arrivals. SPAD operation requires gated or actively-quenched bias control to prevent continuous avalanche after photon detection.

Solar cell optimization for maximum efficiency requires addressing multiple loss mechanisms simultaneously. Optical losses partition into reflection (2–4% for single-layer anti-reflection coating, <1% for dual-layer), shading from front metallization (5–8%), and incomplete absorption of long-wavelength photons (5–10% for 200 μm silicon wafers). Recombination losses include surface recombination at front and rear surfaces (combined 1–3% relative efficiency loss for well-passivated surfaces), Auger recombination in the heavily-doped emitter and base (2–3%), and SRH recombination in the bulk and space-charge region (1–5% depending on material quality). Resistive losses include series resistance from the emitter sheet resistance, contact resistance, and metal grid resistance (combined 2–4% relative efficiency loss), and shunt resistance losses from edge and junction defects (typically <0.5% for high-quality cells).

The temperature coefficient of efficiency for silicon solar cells amounts to approximately -0.4 to -0.5%/K, arising primarily from the temperature dependence of open-circuit voltage. The bandgap energy decreases with temperature at a rate of approximately -2.3 × 10⁻⁴ eV/K for silicon, while the reverse saturation current $I_0$ increases exponentially according to $I_0 \propto T^3 \exp(-E_g/kT)$. This exponential dependence causes Vₒc to decrease approximately 2.2 mV/K for silicon cells. Short-circuit current increases slightly with temperature (0.06%/K) due to bandgap shrinkage extending the spectral response, but this small positive coefficient cannot compensate for the voltage loss. High-temperature operation (85°C) of solar modules reduces efficiency by 15–20% relative to standard test conditions (25°C), emphasizing the importance of thermal management through backsheet design and air circulation.

Potential-induced degradation (PID) has emerged as a critical long-term reliability concern for fielded photovoltaic systems. When solar modules operate at high negative potential relative to ground—typical in high-voltage strings with grounded negative terminals—sodium ions from the glass migrate through the encapsulant into the anti-reflection coating and silicon, creating recombination centers near the emitter. This ionic contamination increases surface recombination velocity and introduces shunt paths, degrading performance by 50% or more within the first few years of operation. PID affects primarily p-type silicon cells with aluminum oxide passivation; n-type cells and cells with silicon nitride passivation show superior resistance. Mitigation strategies include potential-free grounding schemes, voltage reversal during evening hours, and encapsulant formulations with sodium-blocking properties.

Spectral mismatch between the solar simulator used for cell testing and the actual deployment spectrum introduces systematic errors in efficiency measurements. The standard AM1.5G spectrum represents global hemispherical irradiance with the sun at a zenith angle of 48.2°, including both direct and diffuse components. Real-world spectra vary with time of day, season, atmospheric conditions, and geographic location. Cells optimized for standard test conditions may show 5–10% different performance under cloudy-sky or desert spectra. Precise efficiency determination requires measurement of spectral responsivity across the full operational wavelength range, then convolution with the actual incident spectrum. Reference cells calibrated at national metrology laboratories ensure traceability to absolute radiometric standards, with uncertainties below 1% for carefully characterized devices.

Bifacial solar cells, collecting light from both front and rear surfaces, achieve 5–30% additional energy yield depending on ground albedo and installation geometry. The rear side typically employs full-area passivation with localized metal contacts (passivated emitter rear contact, PERC), achieving rear-side efficiency 70–95% of front-side performance. Albedo from grass (20%), concrete (30–40%), or white membrane (70%) reflects diffuse radiation onto the rear surface. Elevated mounting increases rear-side irradiance by allowing ground-reflected light to reach the entire rear surface. Bifacial gain varies strongly with installation parameters: ground-mounted systems achieve 10–15% gain, while rooftop systems typically see 5–8% due to lower effective albedo from the dark roof membrane.

Tandem solar cells combining multiple junctions with different bandgaps overcome the Shockley-Queisser limit for single-junction cells. A high-bandgap top cell absorbs short-wavelength photons while transmitting longer wavelengths to a low-bandgap bottom cell. The optimal bandgap combination depends on whether current matching is required (two-terminal tandem) or cells operate independently (four-terminal or mechanically-stacked tandem). For a two-terminal silicon-based tandem, the top cell requires a bandgap near 1.7 eV, achievable with perovskites, III-V materials, or chalcogenides. The theoretical efficiency limit for an ideal two-junction tandem reaches 45% under one-sun illumination, with three-junction devices approaching 50% and infinite junctions converging toward the thermodynamic limit near 86% under maximum concentration.

Perovskite-silicon tandem cells have progressed rapidly, achieving laboratory efficiencies exceeding 33% through careful optimization of the perovskite composition (mixed cation, mixed halide formulations like FA₀.₈₃Cs₀.₁₇Pb(I₀.₈Br₀.₂)₃), interface recombination minimization using self-assembled monolayers, and TCO (transparent conducting oxide) selection balancing conductivity with optical transmission. The primary challenge remains long-term stability: perovskites degrade under combined stress from moisture, oxygen, heat, light, and bias. Encapsulation strategies must prevent ion migration while maintaining flexibility to accommodate thermal expansion mismatch between layers. Recent stability tests demonstrate <5% degradation after 1000 hours at 85°C and maximum power point tracking, approaching but not yet meeting the decades-long operational lifetime required for commercial viability.

Concentrator photovoltaics (CPV) employ optical elements (Fresnel lenses or parabolic mirrors) to focus sunlight 100–1000× onto small high-efficiency cells, enabling use of expensive III-V multijunction devices with efficiency exceeding 40%. The photocurrent scales linearly with concentration, while open-circuit voltage increases logarithmically according to $V_{oc} = V_{oc,1sun} + \frac{nkT}{q}\ln(C)$, where $C$ represents the concentration factor. Series resistance losses increase quadratically with concentration, demanding ultra-low resistance contacts and thick metal grids. Thermal management becomes critical at high concentration: a 1 cm² cell under 500× concentration dissipates 20–30 W thermal power, requiring active cooling to maintain junction temperature below 80°C. CPV systems achieve system efficiency of 30–35% in direct normal irradiance (DNI) but require dual-axis tracking and perform poorly under cloudy conditions, limiting deployment to high-DNI locations like deserts.

Quantum dot solar cells and hot-carrier cells represent advanced concepts pursuing efficiency beyond conventional limits through modified carrier dynamics. Quantum dots with size-tunable bandgaps enable multiple exciton generation (MEG), where a single high-energy photon generates multiple electron-hole pairs if its energy exceeds twice the quantum dot bandgap. Demonstration of MEG with quantum efficiency exceeding 100% has been achieved in colloidal PbSe quantum dots, but incorporating quantum dots into functional photovoltaic devices with adequate charge extraction remains challenging. Hot-carrier cells aim to extract photoexcited carriers before they thermalize to the band edge, requiring energy-selective contacts and slowed cooling rates. Both approaches remain in fundamental research stages, with demonstrated efficiencies below 5%, far from theoretical limits that exceed 60% under one-sun illumination.
