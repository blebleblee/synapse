# Lesson 6: Fabrication Processes, CMOS Integration, Emerging Device Architectures

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The journey from a silicon wafer to a functional integrated circuit represents one of humanity's most intricate manufacturing endeavors. Unlike discrete component assembly, where resistors and capacitors are soldered onto a PCB, semiconductor fabrication is a subtractive and additive dance performed at scales invisible to the naked eye. A modern 3 nanometer process node contains transistors whose gate lengths are physically shorter than a single wavelength of visible light. This impossibility—printing features smaller than the wavelength of light used to print them—reveals the central tension that defines modern semiconductor manufacturing: we have pushed photolithography into a regime where classical diffraction limits are bypassed through increasingly sophisticated optical tricks, chemical processes, and computational compensation.

The fundamental challenge that drives all semiconductor fabrication is achieving *reproducible, high-yield manufacture of billions of transistors per chip at economically viable costs*. This constraint sits at the intersection of physics, chemistry, and economics. Every process step—photolithography, etching, deposition, planarization, and thermal annealing—introduces variability. Process variation manifests as threshold voltage drift, metal-oxide-semiconductor (MOS) capacitance mismatch, interconnect resistance fluctuations, and leakage current spread across millions of transistors on a single die. The fabrication engineer's job is to understand these sources of variation, predict their statistical distribution, and design the process flow so that the resulting chips meet electrical specifications despite this inherent randomness.

The physics foundation rests on several bedrock principles. First, semiconductor doping creates p-type and n-type regions through diffusion of dopant atoms (boron, phosphorus, arsenic) into silicon. The dopant concentration profile determines the electric field distribution and hence the transistor's threshold voltage and drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL) characteristics. Second, metal-oxide-semiconductor interfaces exhibit quantum mechanical behavior: carriers tunnel through the oxide under high electric fields, leading to gate leakage current. Third, copper and aluminum interconnects exhibit resistivity that scales inversely with cross-sectional area; as metal pitches shrink, resistance rises dramatically, increasing RC delay and power dissipation. Fourth, thermal processing—from dopant drive-in diffusion to silicide formation to interconnect annealing—follows diffusion equations and thermal budget constraints; exceed the thermal budget and you degrade device characteristics or create reliability hazards.

The invariant that ties all these considerations together is the *technology roadmap*—typically represented as the International Technology Roadmap for Semiconductors (ITRS) or its successor, the More Moore / More Than Moore (MMTM) roadmap. This roadmap prescribes the nominal gate length (or "node" designation, such as 3 nm, 5 nm, 7 nm) along with corresponding metal pitch, contacted poly pitch, fin width (for FinFETs), oxide thickness, and power supply voltage for each process generation. The roadmap is not a decree from physics; it is a collective industry commitment to specific design rules and performance targets. Meeting these targets requires revolutionary process innovations roughly every two to three years.

The economic driver cannot be overlooked. Semiconductor fabs cost $5–20 billion to construct and operate. A single defect—a particle of dust, a photomask aberration, a thermally induced stress point—can render a die inoperable. Yield (the percentage of dies that pass electrical test) directly translates to manufacturing cost per good chip. A process with 70% yield costs twice as much per functional chip as one with 85% yield, assuming similar defect density and fab utilization. Thus, the fabrication engineer constantly trades off cost (fewer process steps, shorter cycle time, higher wafer throughput) against yield and performance.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

### The Photolithography Foundation: Diffraction Limits and Computational Lithography

Photolithography remains the cornerstone of semiconductor manufacturing, despite its conceptual simplicity: shine ultraviolet (UV) light through a photomask, focus the light onto a photoresist-coated wafer, develop the resist to create a pattern, and use that pattern as an etch mask. The challenge is that light diffracts. A feature printed at wavelength λ with lens numerical aperture (NA) has a minimum resolvable pitch governed by the Rayleigh criterion: pitch ≈ λ / (2·NA). For 193 nm immersion lithography (the current industry workhorse), with NA ≈ 1.35, the diffraction-limited pitch is roughly 71 nm. Yet we regularly print features at 20–30 nm pitch, which is three times smaller than the diffraction limit. How?

The answer lies in a suite of computational and optical techniques collectively called *resolution enhancement techniques* (RET). The most impactful is **multiple patterning**, in which a single metal layer is divided into multiple lithography exposures. In self-aligned quadruple patterning (SAQP), a mandrel of width w is coated with a spacer material to double the effective pitch. The mandrel is then removed, leaving two spacers separated by 2w. These two spacers define two pattern copies. Repeat the process with a second mandrel orientation, and you have quadruple the pattern density. The trade-off is that the process complexity balloons—SAQP requires six to eight distinct lithography and etch steps for a single metal layer—and defectivity increases because each additional step introduces new failure modes.

Another critical RET is **model-based optical proximity correction** (OPC). The photomask is not a direct miniature of the intended layout; instead, the mask pattern is systematically perturbed using computational models of aerial image formation, photoresist chemistry, and etch dynamics. These perturbations counteract diffraction and resist nonlinearity, so that the final etched pattern matches the design intent. OPC is so computationally intensive that modern mask shops use GPU-accelerated solvers that can take hours to optimize a single reticle (which is a quarter or fifth of a chip, depending on the stepper reduction ratio). The mask cost itself is now a significant fraction of total process development cost.

Extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography, operating at 13.5 nm wavelength, promises to simplify patterning by reducing the need for multiple exposures. However, EUV source power is limited (currently ~200 W for production tools), photomask defects are harder to manage, and photoresist sensitivity and line-width roughness (LWR) remain challenging. As of 2024, EUV is transitioning from research into high-volume manufacturing, but it coexists with 193 nm immersion plus multiple patterning for the foreseeable future.

### Etching: Physical and Chemical Pathways to Sub-100 nm Trenches

Once a pattern is formed in photoresist, that pattern must be transferred into the underlying film—whether silicon, oxide, nitride, or metal. This transfer is accomplished through **etching**, which broadly divides into wet chemical etching and dry plasma etching.

Wet etching, where a wafer is immersed in a chemical solution, is inherently isotropic: the etchant attacks the substrate equally in all directions. A trench etched 50 nm deep will also undercut the resist mask by roughly 50 nm, creating sloped walls. Isotropic etching is unsuitable for modern technology nodes where aspect ratios (depth-to-width ratios) can exceed 10:1. Consequently, almost all production etching today uses **plasma-based reactive ion etching** (RIE) or more advanced variants like deep RIE (DRIE).

In RIE, a low-pressure plasma (typically 0.1–1 Pa) is struck between a cathode (the wafer) and an anode inside a processing chamber. A radiofrequency (RF) voltage (typically 13.56 MHz) is applied, ionizing a precursor gas—perhaps CF₄ (carbon tetrafluoride) for oxide etching or Cl₂ (chlorine) for polysilicon etching. The ions are accelerated toward the cathode by the electric field, bombarding the wafer surface with energies of tens to hundreds of electron volts. This ion bombardment is directional, creating anisotropic etching. Simultaneously, the precursor gas dissociates into radicals that chemically react with the substrate. The combination of physical sputtering (ion bombardment) and chemical reaction creates the etch selectivity and directionality needed for sub-micron features.

The etch selectivity—the ratio of etch rates between the target material and a mask material—is critical. If you are etching a trench in silicon dioxide through a silicon nitride mask, you want the oxide etch rate to be high and the nitride etch rate to be low. A selectivity of 10:1 means the nitride erosion is 10% of the oxide etch depth; for a 100 nm trench, the nitride mask erodes only 10 nm, which is usually acceptable. However, achieving high selectivity often requires careful gas mixture tuning and plasma parameter optimization. For example, etching oxide with CF₄/H₂ chemistry works well when the CF₄/H₂ ratio is precisely controlled; deviate slightly, and selectivity can drop from 20:1 to 5:1.

The **Bosch process**, developed for DRIE, cycles between two steps: an etching step using SF₆ (sulfur hexafluoride) and a sidewall passivation step using C₄F₈ (octafluorocyclobutane). During the SF₆ step, fluorine radicals etch silicon isotropically, but the RF power is high, creating a directional ion bombardment component. During the C₄F₈ step, a polymer film is deposited on the trench sidewalls, protecting them from etch. The bottom of the trench, being exposed to ion bombardment, sputter-cleans this polymer, allowing further etching. By cycling these two steps, aspect ratios exceeding 30:1 are achievable. Scalloping—ripples on the sidewalls caused by the cyclic deposition and etching—is a side effect; mitigating scalloping requires careful tuning of cycle timing and plasma parameters.

### Thin-Film Deposition: Chemical Vapor Deposition, Physical Vapor Deposition, and Atomic Layer Deposition

After etching trenches, holes, or vias, those features must be filled with conductive or insulating material. Deposition processes are categorized by their mechanism: chemical vapor deposition (CVD), physical vapor deposition (PVD), and atomic layer deposition (ALD).

**Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD)** involves flowing a precursor gas into a heated chamber where the precursor thermally decomposes or reacts on the wafer surface, depositing a solid film. For oxide deposition, the precursor might be tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) or hexamethyldisiloxane (HMDSO). The reaction occurs at the surface at elevated temperature (typically 200–800 °C depending on the process). CVD is relatively fast—film growth rates range from 10 nm/min to several hundred nm/min—and can achieve good conformality (uniform thickness) in high-aspect-ratio features if the process is optimized. However, CVD's chemical reaction pathways are temperature-dependent, and achieving uniform deposition across a large wafer requires careful control of gas flow patterns, pressure, and temperature uniformity. Local hot spots can lead to preferential growth, causing film thickness nonuniformity (film within-wafer uniformity, or "WIU," is a critical metric; modern processes target ±5% WIU or better).

**Physical Vapor Deposition (PVD)** relies on sputtering: high-energy ion bombardment of a target material causes atoms to be ejected from the target surface, which then travel through vacuum and deposit on the wafer. Sputtering is inherently directional—atoms ejected from the target travel roughly in straight lines (due to the low pressure, typically 0.1–1 Pa)—so PVD suffers from poor conformality in deep, narrow trenches. The top of a trench fills preferentially, and the bottom may receive little or no material. However, PVD is chemically inert and works for nearly any material—metals (copper, tungsten, aluminum, tantalum), alloys, nitrides, and carbides. The etch chamber walls do not poison the process because the deposition mechanism is physical bombardment, not chemical reaction. For contacts and barriers, PVD is invaluable.

**Atomic Layer Deposition (ALD)** is a self-limiting, two-step cyclic process that deposits material one atomic layer at a time. A precursor gas (e.g., trimethylaluminum, TMA) is pulsed into the chamber, where it chemisorbs on the surface. After a purge step, a co-reactant (e.g., water) is introduced, which reacts with the chemisorbed precursor, forming a compound film and releasing a byproduct (e.g., methane). After another purge, the cycle repeats. Because each cycle is self-limiting—the precursor saturates the surface and no further reaction occurs—film thickness is digitally controlled: N cycles deposit a film of thickness N × δt, where δt is the per-cycle thickness (typically 0.1–0.2 nm). ALD's conformality is exceptional, even in 50:1 aspect ratio features. The downside is that ALD is extremely slow: typical growth rates are 0.1–1 nm/min, making it economical only for thin films (1–100 nm). High-κ dielectrics (HfO₂, Al₂O₃) for gate oxides and memory capacitors are now routinely deposited via ALD.

### Planarization and Chemical-Mechanical Polishing

As films are deposited and etched, surface topography becomes increasingly chaotic. A trench filled with metal, surrounded by oxide, creates a relief pattern—high peaks of metal and low valleys of oxide—that is problematic for subsequent lithography. The optical system assumes a relatively flat surface; if topography varies by more than roughly one-tenth of the wavelength being used, the lithographic focus becomes ill-defined, and pattern fidelity degrades.

**Chemical-mechanical polishing (CMP)** removes material by grinding the wafer surface against a rotating polishing pad while dispensing a chemical slurry. The slurry contains abrasive particles (typically silica or CeO₂) suspended in a liquid matrix. The mechanical action of the abrasive particles removes material, while the chemical constituents dissolve or soften the material, enhancing removal. The polish rate depends on the pad pressure, platen rotation speed, wafer rotation speed, slurry chemistry, and the material being polished.

CMP is highly selective: hard materials (e.g., titanium nitride, TiN) polish slowly, while soft materials (e.g., copper, SiO₂ in the presence of the right slurry) polish rapidly. This selectivity is exploited during metal layer planarization: after copper deposition fills vias and trenches, CMP removes the excess copper from the field regions while stopping at the TiN barrier layer. The TiN acts as a dishing stopper—a harder material that stops the polish and prevents over-removal. After TiN removal by a separate selective etch, the surface is nearly flat, ready for the next lithography layer.

The challenge with CMP is *dishing* and *erosion*. Dishing occurs when a wide metal feature is preferentially removed, leaving a depression. Erosion occurs in narrow field regions where material is removed more aggressively than in dense features. Both phenomena arise from nonuniform pressure and chemical-mechanical interaction on different feature scales. Mitigating these requires careful slurry formulation, pad design, and process parameter tuning. Modern CMP processes achieve within-wafer planar height variation of only 50–200 nm on a 300 mm wafer, which is adequate for 5 nm technology nodes but becomes increasingly challenging at 3 nm and beyond.

### Thermal Budgets and Dopant Activation

Thermal processing—diffusion, annealing, silicide formation—redistributes dopants and defines many of the device's electrical properties. The cumulative thermal energy supplied to a wafer during fabrication is its **thermal budget**. A typical thermal budget is expressed in time-temperature pairs: for example, a 1000 °C anneal for 10 seconds corresponds to a higher thermal budget than a 900 °C anneal for 5 seconds, even though the product might be nominally lower.

Dopant activation requires thermal energy. When boron or phosphorus is implanted into silicon, it occupies interstitial (non-substitutional) positions. To activate the dopant—to move it into substitutional sites where it can donate or accept electrons—the wafer must be annealed at elevated temperature. A typical rapid thermal anneal (RTA) heats the wafer to 1000–1050 °C for 5–30 seconds. Longer durations allow deeper dopant diffusion, which can broaden the junction and lower the peak dopant concentration. Shallower junctions require shorter anneal times or lower temperatures.

For advanced nodes, thermal budget is a severe constraint. A 3 nm technology node operates at reduced power supply voltages (0.8–1.0 V, down from 1.2–1.8 V in earlier nodes) and achieves this partly through reduced oxide thickness and lower junction depths. Yet lower junction depth demands tighter control of dopant diffusion, which requires colder processing temperatures or ultrashort thermal cycles. Spike anneals—single millisecond pulses at very high temperature (1100–1200 °C)—partially solve this by activating dopants rapidly before they have time to diffuse. However, spike anneals create other challenges: the steep temperature gradient during the spike induces stress, which can cause slip dislocations; wafer bow and warping increase; and contamination from furnace walls or wafer boat materials becomes more problematic.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

### FinFET Fabrication: Fin Patterning and Challenges

The planar metal-oxide-semiconductor transistor, where a gate electrode sits atop a thin oxide layer on a flat silicon surface, hits fundamental limits as the gate length shrinks. The problem is **gate-induced drain leakage** (GIDL): the gate electric field penetrates laterally into the channel and junction regions, lowering the barrier for band-to-band tunneling current. As gate length drops below 20 nm, GIDL becomes significant, increasing off-state leakage power dissipation. Worse, the gate loses electrostatic control over the channel—a phenomenon called drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL)—causing the subthreshold swing (the slope of the drain current vs. gate voltage in the subthreshold region) to exceed the ideal 60 mV/decade limit at room temperature.

The **FinFET** (finned field-effect transistor) solves this by wrapping the gate around three sides of a thin silicon fin. The fin is typically 5–20 nm wide for a 7 nm technology node. The gate oxide surrounds the fin, and the gate electrode wraps around it. This three-dimensional structure provides superior electrostatic control: the electric field from the gate penetrates into the fin from three sides, dramatically reducing GIDL and improving subthreshold swing. Drain-induced barrier lowering is reduced because the gate-controlled depletion region is more extensive.

Fabricating FinFETs requires several precision steps. First, the silicon substrate is partially etched to define a hard mask layer (typically SiO₂ or Si₃N₄). The hard mask is patterned via lithography and RIE to define the fin locations. Then, a deep etch (using the Bosch process or similar) removes silicon between fins, leaving fin structures standing vertically. The etch depth must be controlled precisely: too shallow, and parasitic capacitance between fins increases; too deep, and the fin width becomes nonuniform due to lateral etch effects. A typical fin etch might be 40–60 nm deep for a 7 nm node.

After the fin etch, the fin is oxidized to grow a thin oxide layer (typically 1–2 nm for gate oxide) on the fin sidewalls. This oxidation is controlled via furnace temperature and time; the challenge is that the fin corners oxidize faster than flat surfaces due to stress at the corner, leading to rounded corners. Sharp corners increase parasitic tunneling current; rounded corners are acceptable but complicate device modeling. After oxide growth, a gate electrode material (typically a metal or metal-nitride stack such as TiN or a work-function-tuned metal like TaNx) is deposited and patterned to wrap around the fin.

The biggest fabrication challenge with FinFETs is **fin linewidth control**. The fin width directly determines the transistor's driving capability (transconductance) and threshold voltage. A variation of ±10% in fin width translates to roughly ±5–10% variation in threshold voltage across the die. Modern 7 nm fabs achieve fin width uniformity of ±2 nm (on a nominal width of 8 nm), which is a remarkable feat. This requires ultra-precise lithography, etching, and metrology. In-line scanning electron microscopy (SEM) measurements are taken after the fin etch to verify fin widths and trigger feed-forward adjustments to subsequent lithography steps if needed.

Another challenge is **fin-to-fin leakage**. Fins are not completely isolated; if two fins are separated by only 20–30 nm (which is typical), minority carriers can drift laterally between them, creating parasitic leakage current. This is mitigated by shallow trench isolation (STI) filled with oxide between fins. The STI oxide is grown and then planarized via CMP. If the STI is not properly filled or if voids form during oxide deposition, leakage increases. Wet oxide etching steps that follow sometimes create lateral undercutting beneath the hard mask, leading to "wing" defects that degrade device performance.

### Gate-All-Around Transistors and the Move to 3 nm

As fin width becomes increasingly difficult to control, the industry is transitioning to **gate-all-around** (GAA) transistors, also called nanowire or nanosheet FETs. Instead of a single fin, a GAA device is built from multiple thin silicon nanosheets, each typically 5–10 nm thick and 20–40 nm wide, stacked vertically. A gate electrode wraps around each sheet. The advantage is that gate length and sheet width are defined by separate lithography steps, decoupling the two critical dimensions. This is significant because gate length is determined by a hard mask pattern (which can be tightly controlled), while sheet width is determined by a thin-film thickness (which is controlled during deposition and can be quite uniform across the wafer).

Fabricating GAA structures involves depositing alternating layers of silicon and a sacrificial material (typically silicon-germanium, SiGe, with higher germanium content). After lithography and fin patterning, the fin stack is released from the substrate at the bottom and held at the top via a sacrificial anchor. Then, the SiGe layers are selectively etched away using a dilute HF solution, undercut leaving free-standing silicon nanosheets. The gate oxide is then grown on these nanosheets, and the gate electrode wraps around them. Finally, the sacrificial anchor is removed.

The challenge here is maintaining precise sheet thickness and width during the release etch. If HF etch conditions are not carefully controlled—temperature, concentration, ventilation—the etch rate can vary spatially across the wafer, leading to nonuniform sheet thickness. Additionally, the nanosheets must remain intact during etching; if a sheet cracks or fractures, leakage increases and the device fails electrical test. Stress engineering—adjusting the Si/SiGe stack composition and thermal annealing—is used to relieve stress and prevent fracturing.

Another critical step is **gate-free-lying nanosheet inspection** via transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Defects in the nanosheet stack—missing sheets, thickness variations, cracks—are revealed by cross-sectional TEM. Early-stage process development relies heavily on TEM cross-sections; as the process matures, sampling plans are reduced to manageable numbers (e.g., 5–10 wafers per week).
