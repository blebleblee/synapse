# Lesson 2: Analog Circuits, Signal Conditioning, Mixed-Signal Design

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The transition from digital logic to the analog domain represents a fundamental shift in engineering thinking. While digital circuits operate in discrete, quantized states—where a signal is either logic high or logic low, and timing margins are measured in nanoseconds relative to clock edges—analog circuits inhabit a continuous realm where every millivolt, every picofarad, and every microsecond of phase shift carries physical meaning and consequences. Consumer electronics exist at the boundary between these two worlds. A smartphone's microphone must convert acoustic vibrations into electrical signals; a display driver must convert digital pixel values into precise voltage levels that control light emission; a battery management system must measure and condition charging currents with sub-percent accuracy. These tasks cannot be accomplished by digital logic alone. They demand a mastery of analog signal conditioning, amplification, and filtering—the practical engineering discipline that translates real-world phenomena into signals that digital systems can process, and digital commands back into effects that can control the physical world.

The core invariant underlying all analog circuit design is the principle of impedance and energy transfer. Unlike digital circuits, where we typically care only whether a signal represents a logic state, analog circuits demand that we reason about the source impedance, load impedance, and the power budget available for signal conditioning. When you connect a microphone (which generates signals on the order of tens of millivolts with source impedance in the kilohm range) directly to an ADC input pin, you create a signal path where the microphone's output is immediately loaded by the ADC's sampling capacitance. This causes the microphone's output voltage to droop, introduces nonlinear distortion, and prevents you from achieving the SNR (signal-to-noise ratio) that the ADC specification promises. An amplifier stage positioned between the microphone and the ADC solves this problem by presenting a high input impedance to the microphone (so it sees minimal loading) while presenting a low output impedance to the ADC (so it can drive the ADC's sampling network without drooping). This impedance transformation is not optional; it is fundamental to achieving performance.

Noise, thermal drift, and nonlinearity form the second pillar of analog circuit invariants. In digital circuits, we largely ignore thermal effects and treat components as ideal switches. In analog circuits, every resistor generates thermal (Johnson) noise proportional to its resistance and absolute temperature. Every semiconductor junction has a temperature coefficient that causes its gain, offset voltage, and threshold voltage to drift with temperature. A precision instrumentation amplifier specified at 25°C may exhibit 100 µV of offset voltage drift per degree Celsius—meaning that if your circuit operates in a car interior where temperature varies from −10°C to +60°C, the offset drift alone can be 7 mV, which may completely swamp the signal you are trying to measure. Nonlinearity arises from every active device: operational amplifiers have finite open-loop gain (typically 100,000 to 10,000,000 V/V) that varies with frequency; transistors exhibit Early effect (finite output impedance) and body effect in MOS devices; diodes and the Schottky barriers in mixed-signal ICs introduce exponential I-V curves that distort signals. A consumer electronics design that ignores these effects produces circuits that work beautifully in the lab at room temperature but fail in the field.

The third invariant is bandwidth and stability. Analog circuits almost always employ negative feedback to improve linearity, reduce output impedance, and set gain precisely. An op-amp configured as a non-inverting amplifier with a gain of 100 V/V uses negative feedback to reduce the open-loop gain (which might be 1 million V/V and highly nonlinear) to a closed-loop gain of exactly 100 V/V. However, every op-amp has internal frequency compensation that introduces poles in its transfer function. At low frequencies, the op-amp's gain is high; as frequency increases, the gain rolls off. If you apply positive feedback (or if the negative feedback path introduces excessive phase lag), you can create a closed-loop pole in the right half of the s-plane, causing the circuit to oscillate. A circuit that oscillates is not merely slow or distorted—it may generate RF noise that interferes with wireless receivers, consume excessive power, and damage connected components. Stability analysis is therefore not an optional afterthought; it is a core design requirement that must be verified during the circuit design phase through Bode plot analysis, phase margin calculation, and often through SPICE simulation.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

### 2.1 Operational Amplifiers: The Foundational Building Block

An operational amplifier is a high-gain voltage amplifier with two inputs (inverting and non-inverting) and one output. The ideal op-amp has infinite open-loop gain (meaning the output changes infinitely for any difference between the two inputs), infinite input impedance (so it draws no current from its input sources), zero output impedance (so it can drive any load), and infinite bandwidth. Real op-amps deviate from this ideal in ways that matter profoundly for circuit design.

The LM358, a ubiquitous general-purpose op-amp used in countless consumer electronics applications, exemplifies the practical reality. Its open-loop gain is typically 100,000 V/V (100 dB) but varies with frequency and supply voltage. Its input offset voltage (the difference between the voltages that must be applied to the two inputs to make the output exactly zero) is typically 40 mV, with a temperature coefficient of 0.5 mV/°C. Its input bias current—the current that must flow into each input to bias the input stage—is typically 45 nanoamps. These specifications seem small until you realize that in a transimpedance amplifier (where you are converting current to voltage), an input bias current of 45 nA flowing through a 1 MΩ feedback resistor produces an offset voltage of 45 mV, which may be comparable to your signal.

The internal architecture of a practical op-amp consists of three stages. The input stage is a differential pair of transistors that amplifies the difference between the inverting and non-inverting inputs while rejecting signals common to both inputs (this is the Common-Mode Rejection Ratio, or CMRR, typically 70–100 dB). The intermediate gain stage provides most of the open-loop gain and includes frequency compensation. The output stage is a push-pull buffer that can source and sink current into the load. Understanding this structure is crucial because it explains why op-amp performance varies with supply voltage, temperature, and load conditions.

Consider a practical non-inverting amplifier configuration, where the non-inverting input receives the signal to be amplified, the inverting input is connected to a feedback network, and the output is also connected to this feedback network. The gain is determined by the feedback resistor ratio: $G = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_i}$, where $R_f$ is the feedback resistor and $R_i$ is the input resistor to ground from the inverting input. If you want a gain of 10 V/V and you choose $R_i = 1 \text{ k}\Omega$ and $R_f = 9 \text{ k}\Omega$, you might assume you have solved the problem. However, several practical concerns emerge immediately. First, the resistor values determine the input impedance: the inverting input "sees" $R_i$ to ground, and resistor tolerances (typically ±1% for standard film resistors) cause the gain to vary from 9.9 to 10.1 V/V across a production run. If you need tighter tolerance, you must use precision resistors (0.1% tolerance, but cost increases 5–10×). Second, the resistor values determine the noise floor: each resistor generates thermal noise with spectral density $\sqrt{4kTR}$, where $k$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ is absolute temperature, and $R$ is resistance. A 1 MΩ resistor generates about 129 nV/√Hz of noise, while a 1 kΩ resistor generates 4.1 nV/√Hz. In a precision measurement circuit, high resistor values can dominate the noise budget. Third, the resistor values interact with the op-amp's closed-loop bandwidth: the -3dB bandwidth of a non-inverting amplifier is approximately $f_{-3dB} = \frac{GBW}{G}$, where $GBW$ is the gain-bandwidth product (typically 1 MHz for a general-purpose op-amp like the LM358). If you use an LM358 with a gain of 10 V/V, your bandwidth is limited to about 100 kHz.

The practical design workflow for an op-amp circuit begins with the system-level requirements. A microphone preamplifier in a smartphone must amplify signals from a MEMS microphone (output impedance ~10 kΩ, signal level ~50 mV) to a level suitable for an ADC (typically 1–2 V). The signal path must accommodate audio frequencies (20 Hz to 20 kHz minimum, extended to 48 kHz or higher for high-quality recording). The power supply is typically 3.3 V or lower, and the total current consumption must not exceed a few milliamperes to preserve battery life. You might select an op-amp like the OPA2333, a low-power, rail-to-rail op-amp optimized for battery-powered applications. Its gain-bandwidth product is only 500 kHz (compared to 1 MHz for the LM358), but its quiescent current is 0.5 mA per channel versus 2 mA for the LM358—a 4× reduction that translates directly to battery life.

Once the op-amp is selected, you design the feedback network. A gain of 20 V/V (26 dB) is typical for the first stage of a microphone preamp, with additional gain provided by subsequent stages if needed. Using the non-inverting configuration with $G = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_i}$, you might choose $R_i = 10 \text{ k}\Omega$ and $R_f = 190 \text{ k}\Omega$ to achieve a gain of 20 V/V. These values are a compromise between several competing concerns: they are large enough to avoid excessive current draw from the op-amp output (important for low-power operation) but small enough that the thermal noise is not excessive. The input impedance seen by the microphone is now the parallel combination of $R_i$ and the op-amp's input impedance (typically tens of megohms for a CMOS input stage), which is dominated by $R_i$ = 10 kΩ. This is still acceptable for a microphone with 10 kΩ output impedance; the microphone sees a load of approximately 10 kΩ, and the voltage divider formed by the microphone's source impedance and the amplifier's input impedance causes only a few percent of signal attenuation.

The stability of this circuit depends on the frequency response of the feedback network. If the feedback network is purely resistive (which it is in this example), then the phase shift is minimal, and the circuit is stable. However, if you add a capacitor across $R_f$ to implement a low-pass filter (which is almost always done in practice to remove high-frequency noise and EMI), the feedback impedance becomes frequency-dependent: $Z_f(s) = \frac{R_f}{1 + sR_fC_f}$. At low frequencies, $Z_f \approx R_f$; at high frequencies, $Z_f \approx 1/(sC_f)$, which approaches zero. This frequency-dependent impedance introduces phase lag in the feedback path. The closed-loop transfer function of the circuit becomes a second-order system with a pole introduced by the feedback capacitor. If the pole frequency is not carefully chosen relative to the op-amp's open-loop response, the circuit can become unstable.

A practical stability analysis uses the Bode plot method. For the LM358 (typical of many general-purpose op-amps), the open-loop gain vs. frequency plot shows a dominant pole at a low frequency (perhaps 10 Hz), which causes the gain to roll off at 20 dB/decade. At the gain-crossover frequency (where the open-loop gain equals the closed-loop gain), the phase margin—the angle by which the open-loop phase lags −180°—determines stability. For unconditional stability, the phase margin should exceed 45°, and ideally should be 60° or more. If the phase margin falls below 45°, the circuit may exhibit ringing or oscillation. In the microphone preamp example, with a closed-loop gain of 20 V/V and an op-amp with 1 MHz gain-bandwidth product, the gain-crossover frequency is approximately 50 kHz. At this frequency, the op-amp's phase response should not deviate too far from the ideal response implied by the dominant pole, or the phase margin will be inadequate.

To ensure stability in practice, feedback capacitors are selected such that the feedback network's pole is well below the gain-crossover frequency. A common rule of thumb is to place the feedback capacitor pole at one-tenth of the gain-crossover frequency. In this example, you might choose $C_f$ such that $f_p = \frac{1}{2\pi R_f C_f} \approx 5 \text{ kHz}$. With $R_f = 190 \text{ k}\Omega$, this gives $C_f \approx 167 \text{ pF}$. This capacitor introduces a high-frequency zero in the feedback path, which improves the phase margin and ensures stability. However, it also reduces the bandwidth of the amplifier: the -3dB frequency of the closed-loop gain is determined by the input network and the feedback network, and placing a pole in the feedback network inevitably reduces the bandwidth. This is a fundamental tradeoff: stability requires frequency compensation, but compensation reduces bandwidth.

### 2.2 Filters: Active and Passive Implementations

Filtering is ubiquitous in consumer electronics. Every analog signal path includes filters to remove unwanted frequency components. A microphone preamplifier includes a high-pass filter to remove DC offsets and subsonic frequencies that are inaudible but can cause clipping; it includes a low-pass filter to remove ultrasonic frequencies and out-of-band noise that would otherwise alias into the audio band when the signal is digitized. Power supplies include filters to remove ripple; sensor interfaces include filters to reduce high-frequency noise that would obscure the measurement.

Passive filters consist only of resistors, capacitors, and inductors. A first-order RC low-pass filter—simply a resistor and capacitor in series, with the output taken across the capacitor—has a transfer function $H(s) = \frac{1}{1 + sRC}$. At frequencies well below the corner frequency $\omega_c = 1/(RC)$, the capacitor impedance is very large, and the output voltage approximately equals the input voltage (attenuation is minimal). At frequencies well above the corner frequency, the capacitor impedance becomes small, and the output voltage approaches zero (attenuation is strong). At the corner frequency itself, the output is attenuated by 3 dB (approximately 0.707× the input), and the phase lag is 45°. The roll-off rate in the stopband is 20 dB/decade—for every ten-fold increase in frequency above the corner frequency, the gain decreases by 20 dB.

Passive filters are simple and require no power supply, which makes them attractive for certain applications. However, they have several critical limitations. First, they present a load to the driving source: the resistor in an RC filter draws current from the source, and the current varies with frequency (due to the capacitor's frequency-dependent impedance). If the source has high impedance (as many analog sensors do), the voltage divider formed by the source impedance and the filter resistor can significantly attenuate the signal. Second, passive filters have insertion loss: if you cascade multiple stages of passive filtering (which is often necessary to achieve steeper roll-off), each stage loads the previous stage, and the total insertion loss can be several dB or more. Third, passive filters can be damaged by transient overvoltages: if a high-voltage transient appears on the input, it must pass through the resistor and capacitor to ground, and the series resistor can limit the current and protect the circuit. However, if the transient is severe, it can still cause damage. Fourth, the component values of passive filters are typically large (hundreds of kilohms to megohms of resistance), which generates substantial thermal noise.

Active filters use op-amps to implement frequency-selective circuits with gain, minimal loading, and lower noise. A Sallen-Key low-pass filter, a configuration widely used in consumer electronics, consists of two resistors and two capacitors in the signal path, plus an op-amp configured as a unity-gain buffer. The transfer function is $H(s) = \frac{\omega_c^2}{s^2 + \sqrt{2}\omega_c s + \omega_c^2}$, a second-order system with a Butterworth response (maximally flat passband, 40 dB/decade roll-off). The Sallen-Key configuration has several advantages: the op-amp presents high impedance to the driving source (so it does not load the source), the output impedance is low (so the filter can drive multiple stages without degradation), and the filter can have unity gain or even slight peaking in the passband to compensate for signal attenuation in subsequent stages.

A practical example illustrates the design process. Suppose you are designing an anti-aliasing filter for an audio ADC that samples at 48 kHz. The Nyquist frequency is 24 kHz, and signals above this frequency will alias into the audio band if not attenuated. Audio signals extend to 20 kHz, so you need a filter that passes 20 kHz with minimal attenuation and strongly attenuates frequencies above 24 kHz (to minimize aliasing error). A second-order Butterworth filter with a corner frequency of 22 kHz is a reasonable choice. This gives a -3dB frequency of 22 kHz, and at 24 kHz (the Nyquist frequency), the attenuation is approximately 8.6 dB. This is not aggressive enough to prevent aliasing of strong out-of-band signals; a fourth-order filter (two cascaded second-order stages) would provide better attenuation (34 dB at the Nyquist frequency), but at the cost of added complexity and steeper phase response near the passband edge, which can introduce audio artifacts if not carefully designed.

You might choose a fourth-order Butterworth filter with a corner frequency of 22 kHz, implemented as two cascaded Sallen-Key stages. For each stage, the component values are determined by the corner frequency and the desired Q factor (for a Butterworth filter, Q = 0.707 for each stage). If you select $C = 10 \text{ nF}$ (a standard capacitor value), then $R = \frac{1}{2\pi f_c C} = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 22000 \times 10 \times 10^{-9}} \approx 724 \Omega$. You would round this to a standard resistor value like 680 Ω or 750 Ω. The capacitor tolerance (typically ±10% for ceramic capacitors, which are cheap and widely available, or ±5% for film capacitors, which are more expensive) directly translates to corner frequency tolerance: a 10% tolerance on the capacitor produces a 10% tolerance on the corner frequency. If this tolerance is unacceptable, you would select tighter tolerance components or trim the filter by adding a variable resistor (potentiometer).

The Sallen-Key filter requires op-amps with sufficient bandwidth: the op-amp must be able to pass the filter passband without attenuation and must have adequate stability margin. For a filter with a corner frequency of 22 kHz, a general-purpose op-amp with a 1 MHz gain-bandwidth product is adequate (the corner frequency is much lower than the gain-bandwidth product). However, if you were designing a filter for a higher-speed ADC (say, 500 kHz sampling rate), you would need an op-amp with proportionally higher bandwidth.

### 2.3 Impedance Matching and Transmission Line Effects

In digital circuits operating at moderate clock frequencies (below 100 MHz), signals propagate along PCB traces without significant distortion, and we typically ignore transmission line effects. In analog circuits, especially those handling high-frequency signals, radio-frequency applications, or signals traveling over long cables, transmission line effects become critical.

A transmission line is characterized by its characteristic impedance $Z_0$, which depends on the geometry of the conductor and the dielectric properties of the medium. For a microstrip trace on a PCB (a conductor above a ground plane), the characteristic impedance is approximately $Z_0 \approx \frac{87}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}} \log\left(\frac{5.98h}{0.8w}\right)$ ohms, where $\varepsilon_r$ is the relative permittivity of the dielectric, $h$ is the height of the trace above the ground plane, and $w$ is the trace width. For a typical PCB with FR-4 dielectric ($\varepsilon_r \approx 4.5$), a trace 5 mils wide, 1 mil above a ground plane, the characteristic impedance is approximately 50 Ω.

When a signal is applied to one end of a transmission line, it propagates along the line at a velocity determined by the dielectric constant: $v = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}$. For FR-4, this is approximately 0.6c, or about 180 million meters per second. If the signal reaches the far end of the transmission line and encounters a load impedance that differs from the characteristic impedance, part of the signal is reflected back toward the source. The reflection coefficient is $\Gamma = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}$, where $Z_L$ is the load impedance. If $Z_L = Z_0$ (matched load), then $\Gamma = 0$ and there is no reflection. If $Z_L = \infty$ (open circuit), then $\Gamma = 1$ and the entire signal is reflected. If $Z_L = 0$ (short circuit), then $\Gamma = -1$ and the signal is reflected with opposite polarity.

Reflections cause signal integrity problems. If a digital signal undergoes reflection, the reflected wave travels back to the source and can cause overshoot, undershoot, ringing, and false logic transitions. In analog circuits, reflections cause distortion and can introduce out-of-band frequency components that interfere with nearby circuits. For this reason, high-speed analog signals—particularly RF signals and high-speed ADC/DAC interfaces—must be terminated with resistances equal to the characteristic impedance of the transmission line to absorb reflections and prevent standing wave patterns.

In consumer electronics, impedance matching is most critical in two scenarios. First, in RF circuits (wireless transceivers, antenna feeds, power amplifiers), the transmission lines carrying high-frequency signals must be impedance-matched to ensure efficient power transfer and minimize reflections. A typical RF PCB is designed with 50 Ω transmission lines, and all components in the RF signal path are impedance-matched. Second, in high-speed digital interfaces (DDR memory buses, SerDes links, USB 3.0, Ethernet), the data lines must be impedance-matched to minimize reflections and ensure reliable data transmission. For these interfaces, the trace impedance is carefully controlled during PCB layout, and the source and load impedances are adjusted (through series or parallel resistances) to match the line impedance.

In contrast, low-speed analog signals (audio, sensor signals, DC measurements) do not require impedance matching because the wavelength of these signals is much larger than the circuit dimensions. A 20 kHz audio signal has a wavelength (in free space) of about 15 km; on a PCB, it travels more slowly, so the wavelength is even larger. The PCB dimensions are millimeters to centimeters, which is much smaller than the wavelength. Signals at such low frequencies propagate as quasi-static fields, and reflections are negligible.

### 2.4 Noise: Sources, Characterization, and Mitigation

Noise is unwanted signal variation that limits the dynamic range of analog circuits. In a digital circuit, noise is largely irrelevant as long as the signal remains above the logic threshold. In an analog circuit, noise directly degrades the measurement accuracy or signal quality. A 12-bit ADC has a theoretical resolution of 1/(2^12) = 0.0244% of the full-scale range. If the input signal is corrupted by noise that is comparable to or larger than the LSB (Least Significant Bit) of the ADC, the digital output will randomly fluctuate, and averaging or filtering is required to recover the true signal.

Noise sources in analog circuits fall into several categories. Thermal noise (also called Johnson noise) is generated by the random thermal motion of charge carriers in any resistive element.

The thermal noise power spectral density is given by $4kTR$ (V²/Hz), where $k$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ is absolute temperature, and $R$ is the resistance. This noise is white (frequency-independent) and increases with resistance and temperature. Shot noise arises from the discrete nature of charge carriers crossing potential barriers (in diodes, BJTs, and photodiodes) and has a spectral density proportional to $\sqrt{I}$, where $I$ is the DC current. Flicker noise (1/f noise) dominates at low frequencies and is caused by surface traps and defects in semiconductor devices; its power spectral density is inversely proportional to frequency. In mixed-signal systems, additional noise coupling mechanisms include digital switching noise injected through shared power rails, ground bounce from high di/dt switching currents, and substrate coupling in integrated circuits where digital switching modulates the substrate potential. Mitigation strategies are mechanism-specific: thermal noise is reduced by lowering source impedance, increasing signal bandwidth (which reduces the integrated noise power), and cooling the circuit; shot noise is minimized by reducing DC bias currents where possible; flicker noise is addressed by AC coupling to shift the signal spectrum above the 1/f corner frequency, or by using chopper stabilization and correlated double sampling techniques; coupling of digital noise into analog paths is suppressed through careful power supply decoupling, separate analog and digital ground planes with a single-point tie, physical separation of analog and digital circuit blocks on the PCB, and the use of differential signaling to reject common-mode interference. The noise floor of the entire signal chain must be budgeted from the sensor through amplification, filtering, and ADC conversion, ensuring that each stage contributes negligible noise relative to the quantization noise of the final digitizer or the fundamental noise limit of the sensor itself.
