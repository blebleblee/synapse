# Lesson 4: Power Management, Battery Systems, Thermal Design

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The design of power delivery and thermal management in consumer electronics represents one of the most consequential engineering challenges in modern product development. Unlike computation or signal processing, which operate within discrete, bounded domains, power delivery and thermal behavior couple electromagnetically and thermodynamically across the entire system architecture. A lithium-ion cell delivering current through a buck regulator dissipates heat in both the switching MOSFETs and the magnetic core; that heat diffuses through the PCB, through thermal vias, into copper planes, and ultimately toward heat sinks or the product enclosure. The system's ability to extract that heat determines whether the device operates within safe temperature margins or enters thermal throttling, battery protection cutoffs, or catastrophic failure modes.

The fundamental constraint is energy conservation. In any switching regulator, power flowing from input to output must be accounted for: $P_{in} = P_{out} + P_{loss}$. For a 95% efficient buck converter stepping 12V down to 3.3V at 5A, the input power is 19.5W, output power is 16.5W, and losses are 3W. Those 3W appear as resistive heating in semiconductor junctions, magnetic cores, and PCB traces. In a smartphone with a 3000mAh lithium-ion cell operating at 3.7V nominal, total available energy is roughly 11.1Wh. If the device draws 1A average current at 3.3V output (3.3W), the battery will deplete in approximately 3.3 hours without any thermal headroom or thermal throttling events. However, if the switching regulator itself dissipates 0.5W, the true power draw is 3.8W, reducing battery life to 2.9 hours. The seemingly small efficiency gap compounds into measurable, user-visible degradation.

Thermal design is inseparable from power management because temperature directly affects component performance and longevity. Lithium-ion cells exhibit reduced capacity at cold temperatures (below 0°C) and accelerated degradation at elevated temperatures (above 45°C). Semiconductor junctions see increased leakage current and reduced switching speed as temperature rises. A MOSFET's on-resistance, $R_{ds(on)}$, increases roughly 0.3% to 0.5% per degree Celsius above 25°C. For a power switch dissipating $I^2 R_{ds(on)}$ losses, a 50°C rise translates to a 15% to 25% increase in power dissipation—a positive feedback loop that can spiral without adequate thermal management. The same exponential relationship governs battery self-discharge rate: a 10°C temperature rise roughly doubles the monthly self-discharge percentage.

Consumer electronics operate within tightly constrained thermal and voltage budgets. A smartphone cannot exceed 43°C on the rear surface (user touch comfort and regulatory limits) while simultaneously running CPU-intensive workloads and charging the battery. A laptop's thermal design power (TDP) is fixed by the cooling system capacity—typically 45W to 65W for mobile processors—which forces power management firmware to throttle CPU frequency and voltage whenever the die temperature approaches 100°C. This creates a second-order coupling: processor power draw depends on frequency (roughly cubic in modern nodes), which depends on temperature setpoint, which depends on cooling capacity, which depends on thermal path design.

The invariants guiding power management and thermal design are:

**Energy Conservation:** All input power must exit the system as useful output work or dissipated heat. In a battery-powered device, the cumulative energy loss directly reduces operating time. Every percentage point of efficiency improvement extends battery life proportionally.

**Thermal Equilibrium:** At steady state, heat generation rate (in watts) equals heat extraction rate. If a 2W regulator dissipates continuously but the thermal path can only extract 1.5W, junction temperature rises until leakage and additional losses close the gap—but by then the device may be outside safe operating margins.

**Impedance Matching:** Power delivery efficiency depends on minimizing the impedance between the energy source (battery) and the load. High loop inductance causes voltage ripple, necessitating bulk and ceramic capacitors. Poor layout creates ground planes with slots or breaks, increasing impedance and regulator bandwidth requirements. Each impedance mismatch steals efficiency and increases noise.

**Thermal Capacity vs. Dissipation Rate:** The thermal time constant ($\tau = C_{th} / G_{th}$, where $C_{th}$ is heat capacity in J/K and $G_{th}$ is thermal conductance in W/K) determines how quickly a component's temperature rises in response to increased power dissipation. A smartphone SoC die might have $\tau \approx 2$–5 seconds; a laptop CPU with a large heatsink might be 10–20 seconds. During transient workloads (gaming, video encoding), temperature can spike before steady-state thermal equilibrium is reached, mandating predictive throttling.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

### 2.1 Switching Regulators: Topology and Operation

Switching regulators convert DC voltage at high efficiency by using semiconductor switches (MOSFETs) to chop input voltage and filter the result through passive LC networks. Unlike linear regulators, which dissipate excess voltage as resistive heat, switching regulators transfer energy in discrete packets, minimizing conduction losses.

The **buck converter** (step-down) is the foundational topology. During the "on" phase, a high-side MOSFET (typically called $Q_1$) connects the input voltage directly to the LC filter node. Current flows through the MOSFET, inductor, and load, storing magnetic energy in the inductor and charging the output capacitor. The MOSFET carries current $I_L$; power dissipation in the switch is $P_{on} = I_L^2 R_{ds(on)} D T_s$, where $D$ is duty cycle and $T_s$ is switching period. During the "off" phase, the high-side MOSFET opens and the inductor's stored energy forces current to continue flowing through a low-side MOSFET (or diode) to ground. This freewheeling phase prevents the inductor voltage from spiking and allows the output capacitor to supply the load.

The output voltage in steady state is $V_{out} = V_{in} \cdot D$, where duty cycle $D = t_{on} / T_s$. A 5V input stepped to 1.8V output requires $D = 1.8 / 5 = 0.36$, meaning the high-side MOSFET conducts 36% of the switching period. The inductor current ripple, $\Delta I_L = V_{in} \cdot D \cdot (1-D) / (L \cdot f_s)$, determines the size of the output capacitor needed to maintain voltage ripple within specification. Smaller inductance or higher switching frequency reduces ripple but increases switching losses and EMI.

The **boost converter** (step-up) inverts the energy transfer path. During the "on" phase, the MOSFET connects the input directly to ground through the inductor, storing energy. The output capacitor alone supplies the load during this phase, so the output voltage must be higher than input—the converter "boosts" voltage. During the "off" phase, the MOSFET opens, and the inductor's stored energy adds to the input voltage, driving the diode (or synchronous rectifier) forward and charging both the output capacitor and the load. The output voltage relationship is $V_{out} = V_{in} / (1 - D)$. For a 3.3V input boosted to 5V, the required duty cycle is $D = 1 - 3.3/5 = 0.34$. Boost converters are essential in battery-powered systems where the battery voltage may drop from 4.2V (fully charged) to 2.7V (nearly depleted) but the system requires a stable 3.3V or 5V rail.

The **buck-boost converter** combines both topologies, accepting either step-up or step-down requirements. The isolated buck-boost topology uses two separate energy transfer paths; the non-isolated topology shares a common magnetic element. For consumer applications, the non-isolated configuration—sometimes called a "single-inductor buck-boost" or SIBB—offers simplicity. However, the output voltage has inverted polarity ($V_{out} = -V_{in} \cdot D / (1-D)$), which requires level shifting if a positive output is needed. Practical implementations often use isolated topologies (flyback, forward converters) in high-voltage or multi-output scenarios, but these trade efficiency for flexibility and are less common in mobile devices.

**Synchronous rectification** dramatically improves efficiency by replacing the freewheeling diode (Schottky or fast-recovery silicon) with a MOSFET controlled by the converter's gate driver. A typical Schottky diode exhibits a forward voltage drop of 0.3V to 0.5V; for a 5A buck converter, this translates to 1.5W to 2.5W of continuous losses. Synchronous MOSFETs in modern processes exhibit $R_{ds(on)}$ values of 2–5mΩ, yielding conduction losses of 50–250mW at 5A—a 5× to 10× improvement. The tradeoff is gate drive complexity: the low-side MOSFET must be driven with precise timing and isolated gate voltage (typically bootstrapped from the output node) to avoid shoot-through, where both high-side and low-side devices conduct simultaneously, creating a low-impedance path from input to ground.

### 2.2 Linear Regulators and Dropout Behavior

Linear regulators (LDOs—low-dropout regulators) offer simplicity and low noise at the cost of efficiency. An LDO consists of a control loop that modulates a series-pass transistor to maintain output voltage equal to a bandgap reference plus a feedback divider offset. The regulator dissipates $(V_{in} - V_{out}) \cdot I_{out}$ as heat; a typical smartphone might use a 3.3V LDO powered from a 5V USB charger, dissipating 1.7W per amp of load. In contrast, a 95%-efficient buck converter dissipates only 0.26W per amp under the same conditions.

LDOs excel in low-current applications (microamps to tens of milliamps) where switching converter losses and switching noise dominate, and in noise-sensitive analog circuits (audio amplifiers, precision ADC references). Modern LDO designs achieve dropout voltages below 200mV at 100mA, compared to earlier generations requiring 500mV or more. The control loop bandwidth is typically 100kHz to 1MHz, fast enough to reject input ripple and transient load changes but slow enough to avoid oscillation with the output bypass capacitor. Frequency compensation is critical: improper capacitor selection can cause ringing or instability.

### 2.3 Lithium-Ion Battery Chemistry and Characteristics

Lithium-ion cells dominate consumer electronics because of their high energy density (150–250Wh/kg), no memory effect, and low self-discharge rate. Unlike older nickel-cadmium or lead-acid chemistry, lithium-ion cells are fundamentally electrochemical: positive charge carriers (lithium ions) shuttle between the anode and cathode through an organic electrolyte, while electrons flow through an external circuit to power the load.

The open-circuit voltage (OCV) of a lithium-ion cell depends on the state of charge (SoC). At SoC = 100% (fully charged), OCV ≈ 4.2V for most commercial cells. At SoC = 0% (fully discharged), OCV ≈ 2.7V to 3.0V depending on chemistry. The relationship between OCV and SoC is nonlinear: the first 20% of discharge (4.2V to 3.9V) represents only 10% of usable capacity, while the middle 60% (3.9V to 3.2V) contains the majority of capacity. The final 20% is flat (3.2V to 2.7V) and must be carefully monitored to prevent over-discharge, which damages the cell irreversibly.

Internal resistance increases with cycle count and age. A new cell might exhibit 50mΩ to 100mΩ internal resistance; after 500 charge cycles, resistance can increase to 150mΩ to 200mΩ. During a 3A discharge from a 500-cycle cell with 150mΩ internal resistance, voltage sag under load is $V_{sag} = 3A \times 150m\Omega = 450mV$. If the cell voltage is 3.2V at open circuit, the terminal voltage during discharge drops to 2.75V, below the typical low-voltage cutoff of 2.7V for many systems. This forced shutdown is a safety mechanism: discharging below 2.7V risks lithium plating on the anode surface during subsequent charge, creating internal shorts.

Temperature profoundly affects lithium-ion performance. At 0°C, the electrochemical kinetics slow dramatically; capacity at 0°C is typically only 50% to 60% of rated capacity at 25°C. Charging a cold cell (below 5°C) is particularly hazardous because the reduced ion diffusion rate causes lithium plating instead of normal intercalation into the anode. Charging circuits must prevent charging below 5°C; most smartphone chargers include thermistors or rely on battery management system (BMS) cutoffs. At 45°C and above, accelerated side reactions between the electrolyte and electrode materials reduce cycle life dramatically. A cell cycled at 45°C accumulates roughly twice the degradation per cycle compared to 25°C cycling.

### 2.4 Battery Management Systems (BMS)

A battery management system is a microcontroller-based subsystem that monitors cell voltage, temperature, current, and state of charge to ensure safe, reliable operation. Modern BMS architectures follow a hierarchical model: a primary BMS monitor sits on the battery pack itself, communicating via I2C or a proprietary serial interface to the main system controller.

The BMS performs several critical functions. **Voltage monitoring** involves measuring each cell's voltage (or in multi-cell packs, the entire pack voltage) with 1% to 2% accuracy. Overvoltage protection cuts charging at a preset threshold (typically 4.25V ±50mV per cell) to prevent electrolyte decomposition and cell venting. Undervoltage protection stops discharging below 2.7V to prevent lithium plating and mechanical damage from gas evolution inside the cell.

**Current monitoring** is performed using a current-sense resistor (typically 10mΩ to 50mΩ) and a high-resolution ADC, often with a dedicated integrated sense amplifier. Charging current is limited to prevent excessive heating; maximum charge current is typically 1C to 2C (where C is the cell's capacity in amp-hours). A 3000mAh cell charges at 3A to 6A maximum. Discharging current is typically allowed to reach 3C to 5C for short durations (gaming, peak CPU load) but must be curtailed if temperature or voltage deviates from safe ranges.

**State of charge estimation** is the most algorithmically complex BMS function. True SoC cannot be measured directly; instead, it is estimated using coulomb counting (integrating current over time), voltage-based lookup tables, or Kalman filtering. Coulomb counting accumulates error over time due to ADC quantization and sensor drift; a 1% monthly error rate is typical. To prevent unbounded drift, the BMS periodically "synchronizes" to a known state: when the cell is fully charged (voltage = 4.2V and charge current drops below a threshold, typically 50mA), SoC is reset to 100%. When the cell reaches the low-voltage cutoff, SoC is reset to 0%. Between these anchor points, coulomb counting estimates SoC.

**Thermal management** by the BMS involves measuring pack temperature with an internal thermistor and cutting charging or discharging current if temperature exceeds safe limits. Typical limits are 0°C to 5°C minimum for charging and −20°C to 60°C for discharging. If temperature is high (>50°C), charging current is reduced; if temperature is low (<10°C), charging is disabled entirely. Discharging does not have a lower temperature limit in most designs, but power output is reduced at cold temperatures to protect the cell chemistry.

A concrete BMS implementation in a smartphone might use a dedicated fuel-gauge IC (such as Texas Instruments' bq27xxx series or Maxim's MAX17xxx series) integrating a current-sense amplifier, coulomb counter, voltage ADC, and state machine. The microcontroller reads SoC, remaining capacity, and fault flags via I2C at 1Hz to 10Hz intervals. When SoC reaches 1%, the system initiates graceful shutdown; when SoC reaches 0%, a secondary hardware watchdog cuts power directly to prevent over-discharge.

### 2.5 Charge Control Architecture

Charging a lithium-ion cell requires careful voltage and current regulation to prevent damage. The standard charging profile is **constant-current, constant-voltage (CC-CV)**. During the constant-current phase, a constant current source (typically 0.5C to 1C, or 1.5A to 3A for a 3000mAh cell) charges the cell until voltage reaches 4.2V. The charging circuit is a boost converter or a charging IC that steps the input voltage (5V USB or USB-C Power Delivery) down to match the battery voltage and sources current through the limiting resistor.

During the constant-voltage phase, the charger maintains 4.2V ±50mV on the cell while current naturally decreases as the cell charges. Most charging circuits implement this using a control loop: if cell voltage exceeds 4.2V, the charging current is reduced by lowering the charging MOSFET's gate drive or bypassing charge current through a parallel resistor. When charging current drops below a threshold (typically 20mA to 100mA depending on application), the cell is considered fully charged and the charger switches to trickle or float mode, maintaining 4.2V with minimal current to compensate for self-discharge.

For modern fast-charging systems (2A to 5A input), the charging path must account for cable resistance, connector resistance, and PCB trace resistance. A USB-C cable with 40mΩ resistance carrying 3A drops 120mV; if the charger is located far from the battery connector, an additional 50mΩ in PCB traces adds another 150mV drop. The charger's sense amplifier must reference the voltage at the battery terminals, not at the input connector, to compensate for these losses and maintain accurate cell voltage.

### 2.6 Thermal Modeling and Heat Path Analysis

Thermal design begins with understanding the heat generation sources and their paths to the ambient environment. In a smartphone, dominant heat sources are the SoC (main processor), power regulation ICs, and battery. Each source dissipates power as heat, and that heat flows through a series of resistances (thermal impedances) to reach the ambient air or user's hand.

Thermal resistance is analogous to electrical resistance: $\Delta T = P \times R_{th}$, where $\Delta T$ is temperature difference in Kelvin, $P$ is power in watts, and $R_{th}$ is thermal resistance in K/W. A MOSFET with junction-to-case thermal resistance of 0.5K/W and dissipating 1W will have a 0.5K temperature rise between the semiconductor junction and the package case. A 2W power regulator with 0.3K/W junction-to-case resistance and mounted on a PCB with 0.8K/W case-to-ambient resistance will reach a steady-state junction temperature of:

$$T_J = T_{ambient} + P \times (R_{jc} + R_{ca}) = 25°C + 2W \times (0.3 + 0.8)K/W = 27.2°C$$

In practice, thermal paths are more complex. The regulator MOSFET's heat flows into the die attach copper, then through the package leads into the PCB copper (power planes), then through thermal vias into a central heat sink or through conduction into the enclosure material. Thermal vias are small copper-filled holes that conduct heat from inner layers to the top surface; typical designs use 0.3mm diameter vias spaced 0.5mm apart under power devices.

Thermal modeling of complex assemblies requires numerical simulation using finite element analysis (FEA), but hand calculations suffice for first-order design. The SoC die in a smartphone might dissipate 4W to 5W during heavy computation. The package (typically a ball-grid-array, BGA, with hundreds of solder balls) has a junction-to-package-top thermal resistance of roughly 0.5K/W to 1K/W. The PCB, assuming two ounces of copper (70μm thick) with good thermal vias connecting to a large copper plane, contributes 0.2K/W to 0.5K/W. The enclosure interface (typically plastic with a thin heat-conducting film or metal bracket) adds 1K/W to 3K/W. Summing these resistances, the total junction-to-ambient thermal resistance is 1.7K/W to 5.5K/W, depending on assembly quality. At maximum dissipation (5W), steady-state junction temperature reaches 33°C to 53°C above ambient, potentially triggering throttling in high-ambient environments.

Heat sinks accelerate heat removal by increasing the surface area available for convective heat transfer to air. The convective heat transfer coefficient for natural convection (no fan) is roughly 5W/(m²·K) to 10W/(m²·K); for forced air with a fan, 50W/(m²·K) to 200W/(m²·K). A small finned aluminum heatsink with 50cm² effective surface area improves the case-to-ambient resistance from 3K/W (smooth plastic surface) to 0.4K/W (with fins). However, in consumer devices, space and aesthetic constraints limit heatsink size. Most smartphones use thin copper heat spreaders (2mm to 5mm thick) bonded to the rear enclosure, providing modest improvement without adding bulk.

**Thermal interface materials (TIMs)** bridge the gap between the semiconductor package and the heatsink. Poor contact between surfaces creates an air gap, which has extremely poor thermal conductivity (0.024W/(m·K)) compared to aluminum (160W/(m·K)) or copper (400W/(m·K)). Thermal pastes (silicone-based compounds with ceramic fillers) offer 3W/(m·K) to 8W/(m·K); thermal pads (compressed elastomers with conductive fillers) offer 1W/(m·K) to 5W/(m·K). The thermal resistance of a TIM is $R_{TIM} = t / (k \times A)$, where $t$ is thickness, $k$ is thermal conductivity, and $A$ is contact area. A 0.5mm thick thermal pad with k=3W/(m·K) covering a 1cm × 1cm device has $R_{TIM} = 0.0005m / (3W/(m·K) \times 0.0001m^2) = 1.67K/W$—potentially larger than the device's own thermal resistance. Proper TIM selection and application pressure are critical; too thin and air pockets form; too thick and the TIM resistance dominates.

**Phase-change materials (PCMs)** store latent heat by melting and re-solidifying, buffering temperature swings during transient workloads. A PCM with 200kJ/kg latent heat and 1kg mass can absorb 200kJ (55Wh) of energy during melting. In a smartphone scenario with a 2W transient dissipation for 30 seconds (60kJ), a PCM thermal battery can hold junction temperature nearly constant by storing that energy in the phase change. However, once melted, the PCM must re-solidify before accepting more transient heat, limiting its utility to periodic workloads. Modern smartphones occasionally use microencapsulated paraffin wax or salt hydrate PCMs integrated into thermal interface layers, though the benefit is marginal for typical usage patterns.

### 2.7 Electrothermal Coupling and Dynamic Thermal Management

Power dissipation and temperature are tightly coupled: increased temperature causes increased leakage current (exponentially with temperature), which causes increased power dissipation, which causes further temperature increase. This positive feedback can lead to thermal runaway. In a modern 5nm CMOS process, leakage power might be 10% of total power at 25°C but 40% at 85°C. A CPU dissipating 5W at 25°C might dissipate 6W at 85°C due to leakage alone, even with no increase in clock frequency or workload.

The relationship between leakage current and temperature follows an exponential law: $I_{leak} \propto T \times e^{-E_a / (k_B T)}$, where $E_a$ is activation energy and $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant. For typical CMOS, doubling temperature (from 300K to 600K in absolute scale) increases leakage roughly 10× to 100×. This creates a hard temperature ceiling in power-limited systems: as temperature approaches the thermal shutdown threshold (100°C to 105°C for most semiconductors), leakage becomes the dominant power dissipation component, and further increases in clock frequency or computation cause minimal additional performance gain while risking thermal shutdown.

Dynamic thermal management (DTM) is the firmware strategy of reducing processor frequency and voltage dynamically to maintain temperature below safe limits. When the die temperature sensor (integrated into the SoC) reads above a threshold (typically 95°C to 100°C), the DTM controller reduces CPU frequency and core voltage via dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS). Reducing frequency from 3GHz to 2GHz cuts dynamic power roughly proportional to frequency (since capacitive switching power is $P = C V^2 f$), dropping total power by 30% to 50%. The processor also reduces voltage as frequency drops, providing additional quadratic power savings. A 2GHz / 1.0V operating point dissipates roughly half the power of a 3GHz / 1.1V point.
