# Operational Verification Labs: Motor Control

## Objective & Operational Lab Setup

Motor control systems represent the convergence of power electronics, electromagnetic physics, real-time embedded control theory, and pulse-width modulation (PWM) switching architectures. The operational verification laboratories that follow demand a practitioner's mastery of both the analog power stage—where high-current semiconductor switching converts DC bus voltages into three-phase AC waveforms or H-bridge reversal logic—and the digital control layer, where microcontroller interrupt service routines execute field-oriented control (FOC) transforms, PID compensation loops, and encoder feedback integration at deterministic 10–20 kHz update rates. This laboratory curriculum adopts an 80% hands-on, 20% theoretical grounding philosophy: you will physically wire motor drive circuits, tune commutation timing with oscilloscope cursors positioned on back-EMF zero crossings, and instrument gate drive signals with differential voltage probes to capture shoot-through transients that destroy MOSFETs in microseconds. The goal is not to derive Laplace-domain transfer functions in isolation but to develop the muscle memory and diagnostic intuition required to commission a three-phase brushless DC motor controller from first power-on through closed-loop velocity regulation under mechanical load disturbances.

Your laboratory bench must be equipped with a dual-channel benchtop DC power supply capable of delivering 24–48 VDC at 5 A minimum, a four-channel digital oscilloscope with differential voltage probes rated for high common-mode rejection (CMRR > 80 dB) to safely measure floating gate drive signals, a handheld digital multimeter with true-RMS AC measurement for phase current verification, and a benchtop function generator for injecting synthetic encoder quadrature signals during open-loop testing. The motor testbed itself consists of a three-phase brushless DC (BLDC) outrunner motor with 14 pole pairs and a rated KV constant of 400 RPM/V, mechanically coupled to a magnetic particle brake or friction disk dynamometer to apply calibrated load torque. The motor controller hardware is built around the STM32G474 microcontroller—selected for its 170 MHz Cortex-M4F core, hardware floating-point unit, and advanced motor control peripherals including six-channel center-aligned PWM generation, ADC channels with injected conversion triggering synchronized to PWM valley points, and a hardware encoder interface with quadrature decoder and index pulse capture. The power stage employs three half-bridge gate driver ICs (Texas Instruments DRV8323RS) driving six N-channel MOSFETs (Infineon IPP041N10N5) arranged in a three-phase inverter topology, with phase current sensing implemented via inline 10 mΩ shunt resistors and differential operational amplifiers (AD8418A) providing galvanic isolation and gain of 20 V/V.

Before executing any laboratory exercise, verify that your DC power supply current limit is set to 3 A to prevent catastrophic failure during initial commissioning, confirm that all oscilloscope probe ground leads are connected to the controller's star-point ground reference to avoid ground loop oscillations, and ensure that the motor shaft is mechanically uncoupled from any load to permit safe open-loop testing. The embedded firmware foundation is built on STM32CubeIDE with the X-CUBE-MCSDK motor control software development kit, which provides hardware abstraction layers for timer PWM configuration, ADC injected group triggering, and Clarke/Park transform primitives. You will modify and recompile this firmware to implement custom control algorithms, enabling direct observation of how register-level timer configuration—such as the TIM1_CCR1 capture-compare register that determines phase-A high-side MOSFET duty cycle—translates into physical three-phase voltage waveforms measured with oscilloscope probes on the motor windings.

The operational verification methodology in these laboratories follows a structured escalation from open-loop voltage-mode control, where you manually command six-step commutation patterns and measure back-EMF timing with oscilloscope cursors, to closed-loop field-oriented control with cascaded current and velocity PID loops. Each exercise culminates in a quantitative performance audit where you record settling time, steady-state error, and current total harmonic distortion (THD) in a structured spreadsheet, comparing empirical results against theoretical predictions derived from motor parameters extracted via DC resistance measurement and inductance characterization with an LCR meter. This audit-driven approach ensures that abstract control theory—Laplace transforms, Bode plots, root locus diagrams—remains grounded in observable hardware behavior: the 2.5 kHz acoustic whine that signals PWM interference coupling into encoder signals, the 8 A current spike visible on your oscilloscope when inadequate dead-time insertion causes shoot-through conduction, and the 15% velocity overshoot that demands PID derivative gain reduction.

## Exercise 1: Foundational Practical Implementation

### Open-Loop Six-Step Commutation and Back-EMF Timing Verification

The foundational motor control exercise establishes your ability to generate synchronized three-phase voltage waveforms using microcontroller timer peripherals and verify correct electromagnetic commutation by capturing back-EMF zero-crossing events on a digital oscilloscope. You will configure the STM32G474's TIM1 advanced control timer to produce three pairs of complementary PWM signals with programmable dead-time insertion, route these signals through gate driver ICs to the three-phase MOSFET bridge, and manually sequence through the six electrical commutation states that define brushless DC motor operation. This open-loop implementation—where rotor position is commanded rather than measured—allows direct observation of the cause-and-effect relationship between PWM duty cycle, applied phase voltage, and resulting motor shaft velocity, providing tactile feedback that closed-loop algorithms often obscure.

Begin by confirming that your STM32G474 microcontroller clock tree is configured for 170 MHz system clock operation with the PLL multiplier set to achieve maximum timer resolution. Open STM32CubeIDE and create a new project targeting the NUCLEO-G474RE development board, enabling TIM1 in PWM generation mode with complementary outputs. Navigate to the timer configuration panel and set the prescaler register TIM1_PSC to zero, which configures the timer to increment at the full 170 MHz system clock rate, yielding a timer tick period of approximately 5.88 nanoseconds. Configure the auto-reload register TIM1_ARR to a value of 8499, producing a PWM carrier frequency of 20 kHz—calculated as 170 MHz ÷ (8499 + 1) = 20 kHz—which sits above the human auditory range to minimize acoustic noise while providing sufficient resolution for duty cycle modulation. Enable complementary output channels TIM1_CH1, TIM1_CH2, and TIM1_CH3 with active-high polarity, and configure the break and dead-time register TIM1_BDTR to insert 500 nanoseconds of dead time between high-side and low-side MOSFET transitions. This dead-time value is calculated based on the MOSFET gate charge of 45 nC and the DRV8323RS gate driver source/sink current capability of 2 A, yielding a switching transition time of approximately 22.5 nanoseconds; the 500 ns dead time provides a safety margin factor of 22× to prevent shoot-through conduction caused by parasitic capacitance discharge.

With timer configuration complete, implement the six-step commutation state machine in the main control loop. Define a six-element lookup table that encodes the MOSFET switching states for each electrical sector, where each 60-degree electrical segment requires a unique combination of two active phases and one floating phase. For example, sector 1 corresponds to phase A connected to positive DC bus (high-side MOSFET on), phase B connected to ground (low-side MOSFET on), and phase C floating (both MOSFETs off). Translate these logical states into timer capture-compare register writes: for sector 1, set TIM1_CCR1 to the desired duty cycle value (e.g., 4250 for 50% duty), set TIM1_CCR2 to zero to maintain phase B low-side active, and disable TIM1_CH3 output to float phase C. Implement a timed state machine that advances through all six sectors at a fixed electrical frequency of 10 Hz, creating one complete electrical revolution every 100 milliseconds. Since your 14-pole-pair motor requires 14 electrical revolutions per mechanical revolution, this produces a mechanical shaft speed of approximately 43 RPM—slow enough for visual confirmation of rotation direction.

Before applying power to the motor, configure your oscilloscope to capture all three phase-to-neutral voltage waveforms simultaneously. Connect oscilloscope channel 1 to motor phase A terminal with a 10× passive probe, channel 2 to phase B, and channel 3 to phase C. Configure all channels for DC coupling with 10 V/division vertical scale and set the timebase to 20 ms/division to capture two complete electrical cycles. Set the trigger source to channel 1 with a rising edge threshold at 12 V—corresponding to the midpoint of your 24 V DC bus—and enable infinite persistence display mode to overlay multiple commutation cycles and verify pattern repeatability. Now apply 24 VDC from your benchtop power supply with the current limit firmly set to 3 A, and execute the open-loop commutation firmware. Observe the characteristic trapezoidal voltage waveforms on all three phases, where each phase dwells at the positive rail for 120 electrical degrees, floats at the motor's self-generated back-EMF voltage for 60 degrees, and connects to ground for the remaining 120 degrees. Use the oscilloscope cursor function to measure the time interval between successive rising edges on phase A; this period should match your programmed 100 ms electrical cycle time within ±2 ms, accounting for microcontroller interrupt latency and timer reload jitter.

With basic commutation verified, transition to back-EMF timing analysis by reducing the PWM duty cycle to 25% (TIM1_CCR1 = 2125) and increasing the electrical frequency to 50 Hz, which raises mechanical shaft speed to approximately 214 RPM. At this higher velocity, the motor generates sufficient back-EMF voltage—proportional to angular velocity and the motor's KV constant of 400 RPM/V—to be clearly visible on the floating phase during its 60-degree inactive period. Reconfigure your oscilloscope trigger to single-shot mode and position cursors to measure the back-EMF zero-crossing instant, which occurs when the floating phase voltage passes through half the DC bus voltage (12 V for a 24 V supply). This zero-crossing event provides the fundamental timing reference for sensorless motor control algorithms: in a properly synchronized system, the next commutation transition should occur exactly 30 electrical degrees (one-sixth of a cycle) after the zero-crossing, ensuring that the rotating stator magnetic field remains optimally aligned with the rotor's permanent magnet flux. Use the oscilloscope's cursor delta function to measure the time interval from zero-crossing to the subsequent commutation state change visible as a sharp voltage transition on any phase. For 50 Hz electrical frequency (20 ms period), the 30-degree delay should equal 1.67 ms. If your measured interval deviates by more than ±200 µs, adjust the commutation state machine timing to compensate, iteratively converging on optimal alignment.

Document your findings in a structured spreadsheet with columns for electrical frequency, PWM duty cycle, measured back-EMF zero-crossing time, theoretical 30-degree delay, and the resulting timing error. Repeat measurements at electrical frequencies of 25 Hz, 50 Hz, 75 Hz, and 100 Hz, corresponding to mechanical speeds of 107, 214, 321, and 428 RPM. Plot timing error versus electrical frequency to identify systematic commutation delay introduced by microcontroller interrupt latency—typically 5–10 µs on the STM32G474—which becomes proportionally more significant as electrical frequency increases. This empirical characterization provides the calibration data required for Phase Exercise 2, where you will implement predictive commutation timing that compensates for these measured delays to maintain optimal torque production across the full speed range.

### Current Sensing Calibration and Injected ADC Synchronization

Accurate three-phase current measurement forms the foundation of field-oriented control, enabling real-time calculation of the torque-producing current component and implementation of overcurrent protection that prevents MOSFET thermal runaway. This exercise establishes your ability to configure the STM32G474's ADC injected conversion group to sample phase currents synchronously with PWM valley points—the brief interval when the low-side MOSFET is conducting and the shunt resistor voltage accurately represents phase current—and to calibrate the analog signal chain from shunt resistor through differential amplifier to ADC input, accounting for amplifier offset voltage, gain error, and ADC integral nonlinearity.

The three-phase current sensing circuit employs 10 mΩ, 2-watt metal strip shunt resistors installed in series with the low-side MOSFET source terminals, creating a ground-referenced voltage drop proportional to phase current according to Ohm's law: V_shunt = I_phase × 0.010 Ω. These shunt voltages feed AD8418A differential amplifiers configured for a gain of 20 V/V, which amplify the millivolt-level signals to a range compatible with the STM32G474's 3.3 V ADC input span while providing galvanic isolation and common-mode rejection. For a maximum phase current of 5 A, the shunt voltage reaches 50 mV, yielding a differential amplifier output of 1.0 V—well within the ADC's 0–3.3 V linear range with substantial headroom for overrange detection. However, real-world operational amplifiers exhibit input offset voltage typically in the range of ±2 mV, which translates to a 40 mV output error and a perceived 80 mA current measurement offset—unacceptable for precision torque control. You will characterize and compensate for this offset through a systematic calibration procedure performed with the motor electrically disconnected.

Configure the STM32G474's ADC1 peripheral for injected conversion mode, which allows ADC sampling to be hardware-triggered by TIM1 PWM events without CPU intervention, ensuring deterministic sample timing synchronized to the PWM carrier valley. In STM32CubeIDE's ADC configuration panel, enable ADC1 injected group with three channels: PA0 (ADC1_IN1) for phase A current, PA1 (ADC1_IN2) for phase B, and PA2 (ADC1_IN3) for phase C. Set the sampling time to 2.5 ADC clock cycles—the minimum value that ensures complete settling of the ADC's internal sample-and-hold capacitor—yielding a total conversion time of 10 ADC clocks (2.5 for sampling + 7.5 for 12-bit successive-approximation conversion). With the ADC clock configured for 42.5 MHz (system clock divided by 4), this produces a conversion duration of 235 nanoseconds, fast enough to complete all three phase current measurements within the 5 µs low-side MOSFET on-time that occurs during a 20 kHz PWM cycle with 50% duty cycle.

Configure TIM1's trigger output (TRGO) to generate an ADC trigger event at the PWM valley point by setting the master mode selection register TIM1_CR2.MMS to "compare pulse" mode tied to TIM1_CCR4. Load TIM1_CCR4 with a value of 200, positioning the ADC trigger 200 timer ticks (1.18 µs) after the low-side MOSFET turn-on transition, allowing adequate time for current settling through the MOSFET's channel resistance and the shunt resistor's parasitic inductance. In the ADC1 configuration, set the external trigger source for injected conversions to TIM1_TRGO and enable trigger detection on the rising edge. This hardware linkage ensures that ADC sampling occurs automatically at the optimal instant within each PWM cycle, eliminating the need for software polling and guaranteeing sub-microsecond timing precision.

With ADC configuration complete, implement the offset calibration routine. Disable all six MOSFETs by clearing the TIM1 master output enable bit, ensuring zero motor current flow, and acquire 1024 consecutive ADC samples for each phase. Average these samples to compute the mean offset for each channel, then store these values in non-volatile FLASH memory as calibration constants. For example, if phase A's ADC raw value averages 2048 counts (corresponding to 1.65 V at the ADC input with 12-bit resolution over 3.3 V range) under zero-current conditions, the offset correction requires subtracting 2048 from all subsequent ADC readings before applying the conversion formula. The corrected current calculation becomes: I_phase = ((ADC_raw - ADC_offset) × 3.3 V / 4096 counts) / (20 V/V × 0.010 Ω), where the denominator represents the combined gain of the differential amplifier and shunt resistor.

Verify offset calibration accuracy by applying a known DC current through one motor phase using your benchtop power supply configured as a current source. Temporarily disconnect phase A from the motor controller and connect it to the positive terminal of your power supply, with the supply's negative terminal connected to the controller's ground reference. Set the current limit to precisely 2.000 A as measured with your handheld true-RMS multimeter in series, and enable the supply. Trigger an ADC conversion and read the corrected digital value; it should indicate 2.00 A ±0.05 A, representing 2.5% full-scale accuracy. If the error exceeds this threshold, measure the actual shunt resistor value with a four-wire kelvin connection on your LCR meter—manufacturing tolerance may place it at 9.8 mΩ or 10.2 mΩ rather than the nominal 10 mΩ—and adjust the calibration formula's denominator accordingly. Repeat the verification at 1.0 A and 4.0 A to characterize linearity across the measurement range; any deviation from a perfectly linear response indicates ADC integral nonlinearity, which can be corrected through a piecewise linear lookup table if high precision is required.

Complete the current sensing verification by commanding a 50% duty cycle six-step commutation pattern at 25 Hz electrical frequency with the motor mechanically coupled to your dynamometer, applying a light load torque of 0.1 N·m. Observe the phase current waveforms in real time using the STM32CubeIDE's live variable watch window, or export data to a CSV file for offline analysis. Plot the three-phase currents on a common time axis; you should observe the characteristic quasi-square wave pattern with peak amplitudes of approximately 2.5 A and flat-top regions during each phase's active conduction interval. Use the oscilloscope to simultaneously capture the PWM gate drive signal on channel 1 and the corresponding ADC-measured current on channel 2 (output as a PWM-encoded analog signal via a spare timer channel for this diagnostic purpose), verifying that the ADC sampling instant occurs precisely at the PWM valley as designed. Any timing misalignment—visible as a sawtooth modulation on the current waveform—indicates incorrect TRGO configuration and will cause torque ripple and current control instability in subsequent closed-loop exercises.

## Exercise 2: Parameter Variation & Empirical Analysis

### Velocity Control Loop Tuning via Ziegler-Nichols Method

Transitioning from open-loop commutation to closed-loop velocity regulation requires the implementation of a digital PID controller that compares measured shaft speed from the quadrature encoder against a commanded setpoint and adjusts the motor's applied voltage to minimize the error. The Ziegler-Nichols tuning method provides a systematic, empirical procedure for determining PID gain coefficients—proportional K_p, integral K_i, and derivative K_d—through controlled observation of closed-loop system response, avoiding the laborious derivation of motor transfer functions from first principles while yielding robust performance across a wide range of operating conditions. This exercise develops your proficiency in instrumenting the velocity control loop with real-time telemetry, inducing sustained oscillations through aggressive proportional gain, and calculating optimal PID parameters from measured oscillation period and amplitude.

Begin by implementing a basic velocity control structure in your STM32G474 firmware. Configure TIM2 in encoder mode to interface with your motor's quadrature encoder outputs, which generate two square wave signals phase-shifted by 90 electrical degrees, allowing the timer hardware to automatically decode both rotation direction and incremental position. Enable TIM2 quadrature decoder mode with both edges counted, yielding four counts per encoder line; for a typical 500-line encoder, this produces 2000 counts per mechanical revolution, or a position resolution of 0.18 degrees. Implement a 1 kHz velocity estimation loop that reads the TIM2 counter value, computes the difference from the previous sample, and converts to RPM using the formula: RPM = (Δcounts / 2000 counts/rev) × (1000 samples/sec × 60 sec/min), where Δcounts represents the encoder count change over the 1 ms sampling interval. Apply a first-order low-pass filter with a time constant of 10 ms to the raw velocity estimate, reducing quantization noise from the discrete encoder edges: velocity_filtered = 0.9 × velocity_filtered_previous + 0.1 × velocity_raw.

Construct the PID controller with the standard discrete-time form: u(n) = K_p × e(n) + K_i × Σe(n) + K_d × (e(n) - e(n-1)), where u(n) is the control output (PWM duty cycle), e(n) is the velocity error (setpoint minus measured RPM), and the sampling interval is 1 ms. Initially set K_i and K_d to zero, implementing a pure proportional controller to establish the system's ultimate gain K_u—the proportional gain value at which the closed-loop system oscillates with constant amplitude. Set the velocity setpoint to 300 RPM and command the motor to spin with no load. Incrementally increase K_p from an initial conservative value of 0.01, recompiling and flashing the firmware after each adjustment, until you observe sustained oscillations in the velocity telemetry. These oscillations manifest as a sinusoidal variation in measured RPM around the 300 RPM setpoint, with peak-to-peak amplitude typically 20–50 RPM and a period of 200–500 ms depending on motor inertia and loop delay.

Capture the oscillatory response using STM32CubeIDE's built-in SWV (Serial Wire Viewer) data tracing capability, which streams variable values over the debug interface without perturbing real-time control loop timing. Configure SWV to sample the velocity_measured and velocity_setpoint variables at 1 kHz and export 10 seconds of data to a CSV file. Import this data into a spreadsheet application and generate a time-domain plot with RPM on the vertical axis and time in seconds on the horizontal axis. Use the spreadsheet's peak-finding function to identify consecutive oscillation peaks and calculate the period T_u, defined as the time interval between successive maxima. For example, if peaks occur at t = 2.234 s, 2.587 s, and 2.941 s, the average period is T_u = ((2.587 - 2.234) + (2.941 - 2.587)) / 2 = 0.3535 s. Record the ultimate gain K_u at which these sustained oscillations occur; typical values for a small BLDC motor with encoder feedback range from K_u = 0.8 to K_u = 2.5, depending on system inertia and electrical time constant.

Apply the Ziegler-Nichols PID tuning formulas to calculate optimal gain coefficients. For a classical PID controller, the Ziegler-Nichols method prescribes K_p = 0.6 × K_u, K_i = 2 × K_p / T_u, and K_d = K_p × T_u / 8. For example, if your measured values are K_u = 1.8 and T_u = 0.354 s, the calculated gains become K_p = 1.08, K_i = 6.10, and K_d = 0.048. Implement these calculated gains in your firmware, recompile, and observe the closed-loop step response by commanding an abrupt setpoint change from 300 RPM to 500 RPM. The velocity should rise smoothly to the new setpoint with approximately 10–25% overshoot and settle to within ±2% of the final value in 2–4 oscillation periods, demonstrating the quarter-amplitude damping characteristic of Ziegler-Nichols tuned systems.

Evaluate PID controller performance quantitatively by defining four key metrics: rise time t_r (time to reach 90% of the setpoint change), settling time t_s (time to enter and remain within ±2% of the setpoint), percent overshoot PO = ((peak_value - setpoint) / setpoint) × 100%, and steady-state error e_ss = |setpoint - average_final_value|. Record these metrics in a spreadsheet and compare against theoretical benchmarks: Ziegler-Nichols tuning typically yields PO ≈ 10–20%, t_s ≈ 4–6 × T_u, and e_ss < 1% of setpoint due to integral action. If steady-state error exceeds 3%, verify that integral anti-windup is implemented correctly; the integrator accumulator must be clamped when the control output saturates at 0% or 100% duty cycle to prevent unbounded error accumulation during prolonged saturation events.

### Load Disturbance Rejection and Torque Bandwidth Measurement

A high-performance velocity controller must actively reject external load torque disturbances—sudden changes in mechanical load that would otherwise cause speed deviations—by rapidly adjusting motor current to compensate. This exercise quantifies your controller's disturbance rejection capability by applying calibrated load torque steps using the magnetic particle brake dynamometer and measuring the resulting transient velocity deviation and recovery time. The empirical load-to-speed transfer function extracted from this data reveals the closed-loop system's torque control bandwidth, a critical specification for applications such as CNC machine tools and robotic manipulators where cutting forces or external contacts must not disrupt commanded motion trajectories.

Configure your magnetic particle brake dynamometer for closed-loop torque control by connecting its control input to a benchtop power supply with adjustable output voltage, where the applied voltage is proportional to brake torque according to the manufacturer's calibration curve (typically 0.1 N·m per volt for a small benchtop unit). Verify the torque calibration by coupling the motor to the brake, commanding a steady-state motor speed of 400 RPM, and incrementally adjusting the brake voltage from 0 V to 5 V in 1 V steps. At each step, allow 10 seconds for thermal and aerodynamic settling, then record the steady-state velocity from your encoder. Plot velocity versus brake voltage; the slope of this line, measured in RPM per volt, inverts to yield the effective load sensitivity in N·m per RPM, which should match the motor's torque constant K_t (approximately 0.024 N·m/A for a 400 KV motor) divided by the current control loop gain.

With torque calibration complete, prepare the disturbance rejection test by commanding a steady-state motor speed of 500 RPM with zero brake torque. Configure your oscilloscope or data acquisition system to capture encoder velocity at 1 kHz sampling rate, triggered by a digital I/O pulse that you will generate manually to synchronize with the load application event. Manually adjust the brake power supply voltage from 0 V to 3 V as rapidly as possible—ideally in less than 100 ms to approximate a step disturbance—while observing the motor's velocity response. The velocity will instantaneously droop by 30–60 RPM as the mechanical load suddenly extracts energy from the rotor's kinetic inertia, then gradually recover as the PID controller increases motor current to restore equilibrium. Capture at least 5 seconds of post-disturbance data to fully observe the transient recovery, and export to a CSV file for quantitative analysis.

Analyze the disturbance response waveform to extract key performance metrics. Define the peak velocity deviation Δv_peak as the maximum absolute velocity error occurring immediately after load application, typically 50–100 ms after the step. Define the recovery time t_recovery as the interval from load step application to the instant when velocity error falls below 5% of Δv_peak and remains there, indicating that the controller has successfully compensated for the new load condition. Calculate the load disturbance rejection ratio LDRR = Δv_peak / Δτ_load, where Δτ_load is the applied torque step in N·m (3 V × 0.1 N·m/V = 0.3 N·m in this example). A well-tuned velocity controller with adequate torque bandwidth should achieve LDRR < 100 RPM per N·m and t_recovery < 500 ms for a 0.3 N·m disturbance; if your measured values exceed these thresholds, the PID integral gain K_i is likely too low, causing sluggish compensation of sustained load errors.

Quantify the closed-loop torque bandwidth by repeating the load step test at frequencies spanning 0.5 Hz to 20 Hz, using a function generator to modulate the brake power supply voltage with a square wave rather than manual adjustment. For each test frequency, measure the peak-to-peak velocity ripple amplitude and phase lag relative to the load modulation waveform. Plot the amplitude response in dB (20 × log10(velocity_ripple / load_amplitude)) versus frequency on a logarithmic horizontal axis, yielding a Bode magnitude plot of the load-to-speed transfer function. The −3 dB bandwidth—the frequency at which velocity ripple amplitude falls to 0.707 of its low-frequency value—defines the system's torque disturbance rejection capability; typical values range from 5 Hz for a lightly damped system to 25 Hz for an aggressively tuned controller with high integral gain. If bandwidth is insufficient for your application, increase K_i by 50% and remeasure, iterating until the desired bandwidth is achieved or stability margins are exhausted, indicated by high-frequency oscillations visible in the velocity telemetry.

### Field-Oriented Control Implementation and d-q Current Decoupling

Field-oriented control (FOC), also known as vector control, represents the state-of-the-art in three-phase motor drive algorithms, decoupling the electromagnetically coupled three-phase stator currents into two orthogonal components—direct-axis current i_d, which controls magnetic flux, and quadrature-axis current i_q, which generates torque—through coordinate transformations based on real-time rotor position feedback. This exercise guides you through the implementation of Clarke and Park transforms in embedded firmware, the tuning of dual cascaded PI current controllers, and the empirical verification that torque production is indeed linearly proportional to i_q while remaining independent of i_d, validating the theoretical foundation of FOC.

The mathematical foundation of FOC rests on two sequential coordinate transformations. The Clarke transform converts the three-phase stator currents i_a, i_b, i_c into a two-phase stationary reference frame α-β using the transformation i_α = i_a and i_β = (i_a + 2×i_b) / √3, under the constraint that i_a + i_b + i_c = 0 for a balanced three-phase system. The Park transform subsequently rotates the α-β frame into a reference frame d-q aligned with the rotor's magnetic field, using the rotor angle θ obtained from the encoder: i_d = i_α × cos(θ) + i_β × sin(θ) and i_q = -i_α × sin(θ) + i_β × cos(θ). These transforms are computationally intensive, requiring four trigonometric function evaluations per control cycle; on the STM32G474, implement them using the ARM CMSIS-DSP library's hardware-accelerated CORDIC coprocessor, which computes sine and cosine in 14 clock cycles (82 ns at 170 MHz) rather than the 50+ cycles required for software trigonometric libraries.

Implement the FOC control structure as a dual-loop cascaded architecture. The outer velocity loop compares encoder feedback against the velocity setpoint and outputs a commanded i_q current using the PID gains tuned in the previous exercise. The inner current loop operates at 20 kHz—synchronized with the PWM carrier—and employs two independent PI controllers: one regulates i_d to zero (maintaining constant magnetic flux without field weakening), and the other drives i_q to match the commanded value from the velocity loop. Each PI controller outputs a voltage command v_d or v_q, which undergoes inverse Park and inverse Clarke transforms to yield three-phase voltage commands v_a, v_b, v_c, finally converted to PWM duty cycles by dividing by the DC bus voltage: duty_a = (v_a / V_bus + 1) / 2, mapping the ±V_bus voltage range to 0–100% duty cycle.

Begin current loop tuning by disabling the velocity loop and manually commanding a fixed i_q setpoint of 2.0 A while maintaining i_d at 0 A. Set both current PI controllers to purely proportional mode with K_p = 0.5 and K_i = 0, then step the i_q command from 2.0 A to 3.0 A while capturing the actual measured i_q current with SWV data tracing at 20 kHz sample rate. The current should rise toward 3.0 A but settle at a lower steady-state value due to the absence of integral action, which is necessary to eliminate DC error. Observe the rise time and any overshoot; if the response is critically damped with no overshoot and a rise time of 2–5 ms, the proportional gain is appropriate. Now add integral action by setting K_i = 500 (units of A/V·s) and repeat the step response test. The current should now converge precisely to the commanded 3.0 A with zero steady-state error, though you may observe a small overshoot of 5–10%. If overshoot exceeds 20% or oscillations appear, reduce K_i by half and retest.

Verify the fundamental FOC principle—that torque is proportional to i_q alone—through a controlled empirical test. Mechanically couple the motor to your dynamometer and configure the brake for constant torque mode at 0.2 N·m. Command the motor to spin at 300 RPM using the outer velocity loop, and observe the steady-state i_q current required to maintain this speed; it should stabilize at approximately 8.3 A, calculated from the torque equation τ = K_t × i_q, where K_t ≈ 0.024 N·m/A. Now manually inject a d-axis current offset by commanding i_d_setpoint = 1.0 A (while maintaining the velocity loop active), effectively misaligning the stator magnetic field from optimal perpendicularity with the rotor. Observe that the velocity loop automatically increases i_q to approximately 8.8 A to compensate for the reduced torque efficiency caused by the non-zero i_d, confirming that torque production is indeed governed solely by i_q. Plot i_q versus steady-state velocity across a range of brake torque settings from 0.1 N·m to 0.5 N·m; the resulting linear relationship with slope K_t validates the FOC model.

## Exercise 3: Stress Testing, Boundary Failure & Recovery Protocol

### Overcurrent Fault Detection and Current-Limiting Strategy

High-current motor drive systems operate continuously at the edge of semiconductor thermal limits, where a sustained overcurrent event—caused by rotor stall, short-circuit fault, or control loop instability—can elevate MOSFET junction temperature beyond the absolute maximum rating of 175°C in less than 50 milliseconds, triggering catastrophic failure through bond wire liftoff or silicon die fracture. This exercise establishes robust overcurrent protection through hardware-triggered fault detection synchronized with ADC measurements, firmware-based current limiting that gracefully reduces motor torque before hard shutdown, and a systematic recovery protocol that diagnoses the fault cause and restores operation without manual intervention.

The hardware overcurrent detection architecture employs the STM32G474's analog comparator peripherals, which continuously monitor phase current signals and generate an immediate interrupt when threshold crossing occurs, bypassing the relatively slow ADC conversion pipeline that incurs 10–20 µs latency. Configure COMP1, COMP2, and COMP3 to monitor the three phase current amplifier outputs (PA0, PA1, PA2) against a fixed reference voltage set to 2.4 V, corresponding to a fault threshold of 6.0 A—calculated as (2.4 V / 20 V/V gain) / 0.010 Ω. Enable the comparator output inversion to generate an active-high fault signal, and route this signal to TIM1's break input (TIM1_BKIN), which provides hardware-automatic PWM shutdown within a single timer clock cycle (5.88 ns) of fault detection, far faster than any software interrupt handler could achieve.

Implement the firmware overcurrent interrupt handler to capture diagnostic telemetry and initiate controlled shutdown. When TIM1_BKIN activates, the hardware immediately forces all six PWM outputs to their inactive state (as configured in TIM1_BDTR), open-circuiting all MOSFETs and allowing the motor's inductive energy to freewheel through the body diodes. The hardware also sets the TIM1_SR break interrupt flag, which triggers an ISR entry within 8 CPU cycles (47 ns at 170 MHz). In the ISR, immediately read all three ADC current values and the TIM1 counter register to record the fault instant with microsecond precision, then set a global fault_state flag to prevent any subsequent PWM re-enabling until the fault condition has been analyzed and cleared. Log the fault data to a circular buffer in RAM with entries including timestamp, phase currents, rotor angle, commanded velocity, and the active control state machine phase; this fault history proves invaluable for post-mortem analysis of intermittent failures.

Implement a current-limiting strategy that prevents hard faults through predictive torque reduction. In the main 1 kHz control loop, monitor the magnitude of the i_q current command and compare against a soft limit threshold of 5.0 A—set 1.0 A below the hard fault threshold to provide headroom for current overshoot during transient load steps. If the i_q command exceeds this soft limit for more than 100 consecutive milliseconds, indicating sustained operation at high torque rather than a brief transient, activate the current limiter by clamping i_q_command to 5.0 A and simultaneously reducing the velocity setpoint by 10% to decrease the load torque demand. This graceful degradation strategy maintains partial motor functionality during overload conditions—critical for applications like electric vehicles climbing steep grades—while protecting the power electronics from thermal damage.

Verify overcurrent protection functionality through a controlled stall test. With the motor spinning at 400 RPM under no load, abruptly command the dynamometer brake to apply maximum torque (5 V control voltage), mechanically stalling the rotor within 200–300 ms as the motor's kinetic energy dissipates into the brake. Observe the firmware telemetry to confirm that i_q rises rapidly as the velocity controller attempts to maintain speed, triggers the soft current limit at 5.0 A within 150 ms, and eventually crosses the 6.0 A hard limit threshold when the rotor fully stalls and back-EMF drops to zero, causing phase currents to spike according to i = V_applied / R_phase. The hardware comparator should trip within 10 µs of the 6.0 A threshold crossing, shutting down PWM outputs and preventing MOSFET damage. Use a thermal camera or thermocouple probe on the MOSFET package to verify that junction temperature remains below 100°C; if temperature exceeds 120°C, the fault detection threshold is set too high or response time is inadequate.

Implement an automatic fault recovery state machine that intelligently restarts the motor after transient faults without operator intervention. After a fault trigger, enter a diagnostic phase that samples all three phase currents for 50 ms with PWM outputs disabled; if currents decay to below 0.5 A—indicating a transient overload rather than a persistent short circuit—transition to the recovery phase. Gradually ramp PWM duty cycle from 0% to the pre-fault commanded value over 500 ms while monitoring phase currents; if current remains below the soft limit throughout the ramp, declare the fault cleared and resume normal operation. If current again exceeds the hard limit during recovery, increment a retry counter and retry the recovery procedure up to three times with exponentially increasing wait intervals (1 s, 2 s, 4 s). After three failed recovery attempts, transition to a safe shutdown state that requires external reset, preventing infinite retry loops that could thermally destroy the hardware. Log all fault and recovery events to non-volatile EEPROM for field diagnosis.

### Encoder Signal Integrity and Commutation Accuracy Under Vibration

Quadrature encoder feedback provides the high-resolution position measurement essential for field-oriented control, but the optical or magnetic encoder disk's precision can be compromised by mechanical vibration, electromagnetic interference coupling into the encoder signal cables, and temperature-induced drift in the encoder's analog signal conditioning circuits. This exercise subjects your motor drive system to representative environmental stressors—mechanical shock, PWM-induced EMI, and thermal cycling—while monitoring encoder signal quality through real-time analysis of position quantization error, commutation timing jitter, and velocity estimation noise, establishing the robustness margins required for industrial deployment.

Begin by instrumenting encoder signal integrity with oscilloscope capture of the raw A and B quadrature channels. Connect oscilloscope channel 1 to the encoder A output with a 500 MHz passive probe to preserve edge transition fidelity, and channel 2 to the encoder B output. Configure the oscilloscope for infinite persistence XY mode, displaying A on the horizontal axis and B on the vertical axis; this Lissajous figure should form a perfect circle when the encoder signals are ideal 90-degree phase-shifted sinusoids, or a square when using digital quadrature outputs. Any deviation from this ideal pattern—elliptical distortion indicating phase error, radial fuzzing indicating amplitude modulation by vibration, or discontinuous jumps indicating signal dropout—reveals encoder signal degradation.

With the motor spinning at 500 RPM under no load, mount a handheld impact driver against the motor's mounting bracket and deliver controlled mechanical shocks with 1-second intervals while capturing the encoder XY Lissajous pattern. Observe whether the pattern remains stable or exhibits transient distortions correlated with the shock events. Quantify the position error magnitude by enabling the oscilloscope's FFT function on the encoder A channel and measuring the amplitude of spurious frequency components at twice and four times the fundamental motor rotation frequency—these harmonics indicate encoder eccentricity or mounting misalignment that converts radial runout into position measurement error. For a 500-line encoder at 500 RPM (8.33 Hz mechanical frequency), the second harmonic at 16.66 Hz should be at least 40 dB below the fundamental to ensure position accuracy better than 1 degree.

Evaluate electromagnetic interference immunity by commanding high-frequency PWM duty cycle modulation. Modify the firmware to superimpose a 1 kHz sinusoidal dither on the PWM duty cycle with ±10% amplitude, creating wideband switching noise that radiates from the motor power cables and couples capacitively and inductively into the encoder signals. Monitor the encoder counter in the firmware's 1 kHz control loop and calculate the instantaneous velocity by differentiating position; compute the standard deviation of the velocity estimate over 10-second windows and compare against the baseline noise floor measured with static duty cycle. If the velocity standard deviation increases by more than 50% during PWM dithering, EMI is degrading encoder signals, requiring improved cable shielding (use twisted-pair or coaxial encoder cables with 360-degree shield termination at both ends) or differential encoder receivers with common-mode rejection.

Characterize commutation timing accuracy under encoder noise by correlating measured back-EMF zero-crossings with encoder-predicted rotor position. Configure the firmware to sample one floating motor phase voltage (using an ADC channel configured for continuous conversion) during the motor's inactive commutation sector and timestamp the instant when the ADC value crosses the half-bus voltage threshold, indicating a back-EMF zero-crossing. Simultaneously record the encoder-derived rotor angle at this instant; for ideal commutation, the electrical angle should be precisely 30 degrees (one-sixth of an electrical cycle) after the previous commutation state transition. Accumulate 100 consecutive zero-crossing events into a histogram of electrical angle error, and calculate the standard deviation; well-functioning systems achieve σ < 2 electrical degrees. If σ exceeds 5 degrees, encoder quantization error is insufficient for the motor's pole count—upgrade to a 1024-line encoder to double resolution.

Subject the motor drive to thermal cycling by operating continuously at 80% of rated current for 30 minutes in an ambient temperature chamber set to 40°C, then rapidly cooling to 10°C with forced air while maintaining motor operation. Monitor encoder error metrics throughout the thermal transient; some magnetic encoders exhibit temperature coefficients as high as 0.05 degrees per °C, producing 1.5 degrees of systematic position error over a 30°C excursion. If thermally induced position error exceeds commutation timing tolerance, implement temperature compensation by storing a lookup table of encoder offset versus temperature (using the STM32G474's internal temperature sensor) and applying the correction in the Park transform angle calculation: θ_corrected = θ_encoder + offset_table[temperature].

### Closed-Loop System Identification and Stability Margin Extraction

Quantitative assessment of control loop stability margins—gain margin, phase margin, and delay margin—provides objective evidence that your motor drive system will remain stable under worst-case operating conditions including component parameter variation, manufacturing tolerance, and aging degradation. This exercise employs empirical frequency response measurement through real-time injection of sinusoidal perturbations into the closed-loop system, extraction of Bode magnitude and phase plots through FFT analysis, and calculation of stability margins from the measured open-loop transfer function, establishing confidence that the system possesses adequate robustness for production deployment.

The frequency response measurement technique injects a small-amplitude sinusoidal velocity setpoint perturbation spanning 0.1 Hz to 100 Hz while recording both the commanded setpoint and the measured encoder velocity. Implement a firmware function that generates a discrete-time sine wave using the formula setpoint(n) = setpoint_nominal + A × sin(2π × f × n × Δt), where A = 50 RPM is the perturbation amplitude (10% of the 500 RPM nominal setpoint), f is the test frequency swept logarithmically from 0.1 Hz to 100 Hz, n is the sample index, and Δt = 0.001 s is the 1 kHz control loop period. For each test frequency, inject at least 20 complete cycles to allow transient response to decay, then capture 10 additional cycles of both setpoint and measured velocity data with SWV tracing at 1 kHz, exporting to CSV format.

Process the captured time-domain data to extract frequency response magnitude and phase. Import each frequency's CSV file into MATLAB, Python with NumPy/SciPy, or GNU Octave, and compute the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of both the setpoint and velocity signals using the FFT algorithm. Extract the DFT bin corresponding to the test frequency and calculate the magnitude ratio H(jω) = |DFT(velocity)| / |DFT(setpoint)| and phase difference φ(jω) = angle(DFT(velocity)) - angle(DFT(setpoint)). Repeat for all test frequencies spanning 0.1 Hz to 100 Hz with 10 points per decade (30 total frequencies), and plot the results on a Bode diagram with magnitude in dB (20 × log10(H)) on the top subplot and phase in degrees on the bottom subplot, both versus frequency on a logarithmic horizontal axis.

Analyze the measured Bode plots to extract stability margins. Identify the gain crossover frequency f_gc—the frequency at which |H(jω)| = 1 (0 dB)—and read the phase at this frequency from the lower Bode plot. The phase margin PM is defined as PM = 180° + φ(f_gc); well-designed control systems achieve PM > 45 degrees, ensuring adequate damping and tolerance to time delays introduced by ADC sampling, computation latency, and PWM dead time. For example, if the measured phase at gain crossover is -135 degrees, the phase margin is 180° - 135° = 45 degrees, representing marginal stability. If PM < 30 degrees, reduce PID derivative gain K_d by 50% to decrease high-frequency phase lag, and remeasure the frequency response to confirm improvement.

Calculate the gain margin by identifying the phase crossover frequency f_pc—the frequency at which φ(jω) = -180 degrees—and reading the magnitude at this frequency. The gain margin GM is defined as GM = 1 / |H(f_pc)|, expressed in dB as 20 × log10(GM); robust systems achieve GM > 6 dB (factor of 2), ensuring that the closed-loop system tolerates doubling of open-loop gain without instability. If your measured GM is less than 6 dB, the system is excessively aggressive; reduce PID proportional gain K_p by 20% and revalidate. Conversely, if GM exceeds 15 dB, the system is overly conservative and could achieve faster response by increasing K_p, though this should be done cautiously to avoid sacrificing robustness.

Validate the extracted stability margins through deliberate destabilization experiments. Artificially reduce the phase margin by inserting a pure time delay into the control loop: modify the firmware to buffer encoder velocity measurements in a FIFO queue and read the value from 5 ms in the past rather than the current sample, introducing a 5 ms pure delay. This delay contributes phase lag according to φ_delay = -360° × f × delay, which equals -18 degrees at 1 Hz, -90 degrees at 5 Hz, and -180 degrees at 10 Hz. Remeasure the frequency response with this artificial delay inserted and confirm that the phase crossover frequency shifts downward, reducing both phase and gain margins. If the deliberately destabilized system exhibits sustained oscillations or grows unstable—indicated by unbounded oscillations in velocity telemetry—your original stability margins were inadequate; increase PID integral time constant (reduce K_i) by 30% to add phase lead.

## Operational Reference Guide & Diagnostic Checklist

### Pre-Operation Hardware Verification Sequence

Before energizing any motor drive system, execute a systematic hardware checkout procedure to detect assembly errors, component failures, or configuration mistakes that could cause immediate damage upon power application. Begin with a visual inspection of all solder joints on the three-phase inverter board using a 10× illuminated magnifier, verifying that each MOSFET source, drain, and gate pin exhibits a smooth, concave solder fillet with no bridging to adjacent pads. Inspect the phase current shunt resistors for mechanical stress fractures—visible as hairline cracks in the metal strip—which can occur during board assembly and increase resistance by 50% or more, distorting current measurements and causing thermal runaway.

With the power supply disconnected, measure all critical resistances with a four-wire kelvin connection on your DMM. Verify that each motor phase winding exhibits 0.8–1.2 Ω DC resistance (for a typical small BLDC motor) between any two phase terminals, with the third terminal floating; if resistance exceeds 2 Ω, the motor winding has high-resistance connections or partial turn-to-turn shorts. Measure the gate-to-source resistance of each MOSFET; it should exceed 10 MΩ, confirming that the gate oxide is intact. Any reading below 1 MΩ indicates gate oxide breakdown, requiring MOSFET replacement. Measure the DC bus capacitance by configuring your DMM for capacitance measurement and probing the positive and negative bus terminals; the measured value should match the sum of all parallel bulk capacitors within ±20%, typically 200–500 µF for a small motor drive.

Verify gate driver functionality using your oscilloscope before connecting the motor. Apply 12 VDC to the gate driver power supply rail and configure the microcontroller to output a 1 kHz, 50% duty cycle PWM signal on one high-side gate driver input. Connect an oscilloscope probe (DC-coupled, 10× attenuation) to the corresponding high-side MOSFET gate terminal and verify that the gate voltage swings from 0 V to approximately 10 V (the gate driver's bootstrap supply voltage) with rise/fall times less than 50 ns. Any gate voltage below 8 V indicates insufficient gate drive strength or excessive gate charge; verify that the gate driver source current rating (typically 2 A for DRV8323RS) is adequate for the MOSFET's total gate charge (45 nC for IPP041N10N5). Repeat for all six gate driver channels, confirming complementary operation: when the high-side gate is at 10 V, the low-side gate must be at 0 V, with the programmed dead time (500 ns) visible as intervals when both gates are low.

### Common Failure Modes and Root Cause Analysis

Motor drive systems fail through predictable failure modes that manifest as characteristic signatures in telemetry data and oscilloscope waveforms, enabling rapid root cause diagnosis when armed with pattern recognition training. Commutation timing errors—caused by encoder miscalibration, software commutation delay, or incorrect pole pair count configuration—produce the signature of reduced torque output accompanied by increased phase current THD and audible cogging at low speeds. Capture phase current waveforms with your oscilloscope and visually compare against the ideal quasi-square wave pattern; commutation errors introduce notches, spikes, or DC offsets in the current waveform corresponding to intervals when the stator field is misaligned with the rotor magnets, causing current flow that generates radial force (cogging torque) rather than tangential force (useful torque).

Gate driver shoot-through—the catastrophic condition where both high-side and low-side MOSFETs conduct simultaneously, effectively shorting the DC bus through near-zero impedance and drawing hundreds of amperes limited only by MOSFET on-resistance and PCB trace inductance—leaves unambiguous evidence in post-mortem hardware inspection. Failed MOSFETs exhibit visible damage to the silicon die, often visible as a crater when the package is decapsulated, and measure less than 1 Ω drain-to-source resistance in both directions due to parasitic body diode destruction. Prevention requires rigorous dead-time configuration: calculate minimum dead time as t_dead_min = t_rise + t_fall + t_margin, where t_rise and t_fall are the MOSFET datasheet turn-on and turn-off delay times (typically 15 ns and 20 ns) and t_margin is a safety factor of 200–500 ns to account for component tolerance and PCB trace inductance effects.

Encoder signal integrity failures manifest as velocity estimation noise, position quantization artifacts, or complete loss of position feedback when the encoder A/B signal amplitude falls below the microcontroller's digital input threshold (typically 0.8 × VDD = 2.64 V for 3.3 V I/O). Diagnose encoder failures by probing the raw A and B signals with your oscilloscope while the motor spins; degraded signals exhibit reduced amplitude (below 2.0 V peak), asymmetric rise/fall times indicating capacitive loading or ground bounce, or complete dropout for intervals exceeding 100 µs. Electromagnetic interference from PWM switching couples into encoder cables through capacitive and inductive mechanisms; mitigation requires twisted-pair shielded encoder cables with shield grounded at the encoder end only (avoiding ground loops), ferrite common-mode chokes on both A and B signals immediately adjacent to the microcontroller, and differential line receivers (such as AM26C32) rather than single-ended CMOS inputs to achieve 50 dB improvement in common-mode rejection.

Thermal failures occur when inadequate heatsinking allows MOSFET junction temperature to exceed the 175°C absolute maximum rating, accelerating electromigration that increases on-resistance and eventually causes catastrophic failure. Calculate required heatsink thermal resistance from the power dissipation equation P_diss = I_rms² × R_ds_on, where I_rms is the root-mean-square phase current (3.5 A for 5 A peak sinusoidal current) and R_ds_on is the MOSFET on-resistance at elevated temperature (4.1 mΩ at 125°C for IPP041N10N5), yielding P_diss = 3.5² × 0.0041 = 50 mW per MOSFET. With six MOSFETs dissipating 300 mW total and ambient temperature of 40°C, the junction temperature rises to T_j = T_ambient + P_diss × (θ_jc + θ_cs + θ_sa), where θ_jc = 0.5 °C/W (junction-to-case), θ_cs = 0.2 °C/W (case-to-sink with thermal compound), and θ_sa is the heatsink thermal resistance. To maintain T_j below 125°C (providing 50°C margin below absolute max), solve for θ_sa_max = (125 - 40) / 0.3 - 0.7 = 283 - 0.7 = 282.3 °C/W, easily achieved with even small extruded aluminum heatsinks.
