# Lesson 4: Feedback Control—Servo, Stepper Systems

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The fundamental challenge of motor control in precision systems reduces to a deceptively simple question: how do we command a rotating machine to arrive at a specific angular position, maintain a defined velocity, or generate a precise torque, despite mechanical friction, load disturbances, electrical noise, and the inherent electromechanical lag between command and response? The answer lies in closed-loop feedback control, where sensors continuously measure the motor's actual state and a control algorithm computes corrective action to minimize the error between desired and measured performance. This feedback paradigm separates rudimentary open-loop motor driving—where we apply voltage and hope for repeatable behavior—from true servo systems that achieve sub-micron positioning in CNC mills, sub-arcsecond telescope pointing, and sub-millisecond torque regulation in robotic manipulators.

At the heart of any feedback control system sits the error signal, defined as the instantaneous difference between the setpoint (desired state) and the process variable (measured state). In position control, this error is angular or linear displacement in encoder counts or micrometers. In velocity control, the error is expressed in revolutions per minute or radians per second. In torque control, the error manifests as the difference between commanded and measured current in the motor windings, since electromagnetic torque is directly proportional to stator current in both DC and AC synchronous machines. The control algorithm—most commonly a Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) compensator—transforms this error into a corrective command that drives the power stage, modulating voltage or current to steer the motor back toward the setpoint.

The proportional term multiplies the instantaneous error by a gain constant, providing immediate corrective action proportional to how far the system has deviated. A purely proportional controller, however, suffers from steady-state error: if friction or a constant load opposes motion, the motor will settle at a position slightly offset from the target because the proportional term alone cannot generate sufficient sustained drive to overcome the disturbance. The integral term accumulates error over time, effectively remembering past deviations and building up a corrective offset that eliminates steady-state error. Yet integral action introduces its own pathology: windup, where the integrator saturates during large transient errors (such as a step change in setpoint), causing overshoot and oscillation when the system finally approaches the target. The derivative term anticipates future error by measuring the rate of change, providing damping that counteracts overshoot and reduces settling time. The art and science of PID tuning lies in balancing these three competing forces: proportional gain for responsiveness, integral gain for accuracy, and derivative gain for stability.

Servo systems and stepper systems represent two fundamentally different philosophies for achieving closed-loop motion control. A servo motor—whether a brushed DC machine, a brushless DC (BLDC) motor, or an AC synchronous motor—is inherently a continuous-rotation device that requires an external position or velocity sensor (encoder, resolver, or Hall-effect sensor) to close the feedback loop. The servo drive continuously compares the commanded position or velocity to the measured feedback, adjusting phase currents or armature voltage in real time. A stepper motor, by contrast, is an open-loop device by construction: its rotor advances in discrete angular steps (typically 1.8° or 0.9° per full step for a 200- or 400-step-per-revolution machine) in response to sequenced phase excitation. Stepper motors can operate without encoders in applications where load inertia is predictable and step loss is tolerable, but high-performance systems increasingly employ encoders to detect and correct missed steps, transforming the stepper into a quasi-servo hybrid.

The distinction between these architectures shapes everything downstream: servo systems excel in high-speed, high-torque applications where smooth motion and dynamic load rejection are paramount, but they require complex commutation algorithms and real-time feedback processing. Stepper systems offer simplicity and low cost, deterministic positioning without feedback (in theory), and inherent holding torque at rest, but they suffer from resonance at certain speeds, limited torque at high velocity, and catastrophic failure modes (step loss) under overload. Understanding when to choose one over the other—and how to design feedback loops that maximize performance while maintaining stability—defines the competency boundary between novice and expert motor control engineers.

The invariants governing feedback control stability derive from classical control theory, rooted in the mathematics of Laplace transforms and frequency-domain analysis. Every electromechanical system possesses a transfer function relating input (command voltage or current) to output (position, velocity, or torque), characterized by poles and zeros that determine dynamic response. A system is stable if all closed-loop poles reside in the left half of the complex plane, ensuring that transient responses decay exponentially rather than grow without bound. Stability margins—gain margin and phase margin—quantify how much additional gain or phase shift the system can tolerate before oscillating. A robust servo system maintains a phase margin of at least 45 degrees and a gain margin of 6 dB or more, providing resilience against parameter variations, sensor noise, and modeling errors.

Feedback sensors introduce their own sources of error and latency. Optical encoders generate quadrature pulse trains (A and B channels phase-shifted by 90 electrical degrees) that microcontrollers decode into incremental position counts, but quantization error limits resolution to one encoder count, and mechanical vibration can induce false edge transitions. Resolvers—rotating transformers that output sine and cosine signals proportional to shaft angle—offer immunity to electromagnetic interference and absolute position within one mechanical revolution, but they require analog-to-digital conversion and trigonometric computation (arctangent) to extract angle, introducing computational delay. Absolute encoders embed position information in serial protocols (SSI, BiSS, EnDat) or parallel Gray-code patterns, eliminating the need to home on startup, but they add protocol decoding latency and cost.

The mechanical compliance of couplings, lead screws, and gearboxes introduces additional poles into the control loop transfer function, reducing phase margin and inviting resonance. A direct-drive motor coupled rigidly to a load approximates a second-order system with a single dominant resonance determined by the combined inertia and stiffness. Inserting a flexible coupling or a long transmission shaft creates a two-mass system with an anti-resonance (zero) followed by a higher-frequency resonance (pole), complicating PID tuning and often necessitating notch filters or state-space controllers to achieve stable, high-bandwidth performance.

Microstepping, the practice of subdividing each full step of a stepper motor into smaller increments by modulating phase currents sinusoidally, transforms a discrete-step device into a quasi-analog positioner. A 200-step stepper driven with 1/16 microstepping theoretically achieves 3,200 steps per revolution, or 0.1125° per microstep. However, microstepping does not improve absolute positioning accuracy; it merely interpolates between full-step positions, and magnetic detent torque ripple causes the rotor to prefer full-step and half-step positions. Microstepping does reduce resonance, audible noise, and mechanical shock, making it indispensable in applications such as 3D printers and camera gimbals where smooth motion outweighs ultimate positioning precision.

Motion profiling—the generation of time-optimal position trajectories subject to velocity and acceleration constraints—determines how quickly a system can traverse from one setpoint to another without exceeding motor torque limits, inducing mechanical shock, or violating mechanical safety interlocks. A trapezoidal velocity profile accelerates at constant rate, coasts at maximum velocity, then decelerates symmetrically, producing a position trajectory resembling an S-curve. S-curve profiles (also called jerk-limited profiles) smooth the acceleration transitions by ramping acceleration itself, reducing excitation of mechanical resonances at the cost of longer trajectory times. Real-time motion controllers implement these profiles in firmware, recalculating position commands at rates of 1 kHz to 20 kHz to maintain smooth servo tracking.


## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The canonical feedback control loop for a servo motor system comprises five functional blocks: the trajectory generator, the PID compensator, the power stage (motor drive amplifier), the motor and mechanical load, and the feedback sensor. The trajectory generator outputs a time-varying position command and, optionally, velocity and acceleration feedforward terms. The compensator computes the error between commanded and actual position, applies PID gains, and sums feedforward terms to produce a torque or current command. The power stage converts this command into phase voltages or currents that drive the motor windings. The motor converts electrical power into mechanical torque, accelerating or decelerating the load inertia. The encoder or resolver measures the resulting shaft position and velocity, closing the loop.

```text
┌─────────────────┐       ┌─────────────────┐       ┌─────────────────┐
│   Trajectory    │       │  PID Controller │       │   Power Stage   │
│   Generator     ├──────>│   + Feedforward ├──────>│  (H-Bridge or   │
│  (position cmd) │       │   (torque cmd)  │       │   3-Phase PWM)  │
└─────────────────┘       └────────┬────────┘       └────────┬────────┘
                                   │                         │
                                   │ error                   │ voltage
                                   │                         v
                          ┌────────┴────────┐       ┌─────────────────┐
                          │                 │       │  Motor + Load   │
                          │                 │<──────┤  (mechanical    │
                          │                 │ pos   │   inertia J)    │
                          └─────────────────┘       └────────┬────────┘
                                   ^                         │
                                   │                         │
                                   │                         │
                          ┌────────┴────────┐                │
                          │  Encoder or     │<───────────────┘
                          │  Resolver       │
                          └─────────────────┘
```

In a discrete-time digital controller, the PID algorithm executes at fixed sample intervals (typically 100 μs to 1 ms for servo loops, 10 ms to 100 ms for less demanding applications). The proportional term is straightforward: multiply the current error by the proportional gain Kp. The integral term accumulates error over time by summing successive error samples multiplied by the sample period Ts and the integral gain Ki, yielding a running sum that persists as long as steady-state error remains. The derivative term estimates the rate of change of error by computing the difference between the current error and the previous error, dividing by Ts, and multiplying by the derivative gain Kd. In practice, the derivative term is often applied to the negative of the measured position (rather than the error) to avoid derivative kick when the setpoint changes abruptly.

The discrete-time PID equation in its textbook form is:

```text
u[k] = Kp * e[k] + Ki * Ts * Σ(e[i]) + (Kd / Ts) * (e[k] - e[k-1])
```

where u[k] is the control output at sample k, e[k] is the error at sample k, and the summation runs over all past samples. Anti-windup protection clamps the integrator sum when the control output saturates, preventing the integrator from accumulating error during periods when the actuator cannot respond (such as when voltage or current limits are reached). A common anti-windup strategy is back-calculation: if the commanded output exceeds the saturation limit, the integrator is adjusted by feeding back the difference between the saturated and unsaturated outputs through a gain term.

Velocity feedforward injects a term proportional to the commanded velocity directly into the control output, reducing the burden on the PID loop to track constant-velocity motion. Acceleration feedforward similarly adds a term proportional to commanded acceleration, compensating for inertial lag. The complete control law with feedforward becomes:

```text
u[k] = Kp * e[k] + Ki * Ts * Σ(e[i]) + (Kd / Ts) * (e[k] - e[k-1])
       + Kv * v_cmd[k] + Ka * a_cmd[k]
```

where Kv is the velocity feedforward gain (ideally equal to the motor's velocity constant in appropriate units) and Ka is the acceleration feedforward gain (ideally equal to the load inertia). Feedforward terms transform a tracking problem into a disturbance rejection problem, dramatically improving following error during motion while leaving the PID loop to handle only deviations caused by friction, load torque, and model mismatch.

Encoder feedback arrives as quadrature pulses on two digital input channels, A and B. By monitoring the sequence of transitions—whether A leads B or B leads A—the microcontroller determines the direction of rotation. Each transition on either channel represents one encoder edge, and a quadrature decoder counts edges to track position. A 1000-line optical encoder generates 1000 pulses per revolution on channel A, but by decoding both edges of both channels (4× quadrature decoding), the effective resolution becomes 4000 counts per revolution, or 0.09° per count. Most modern microcontrollers integrate hardware quadrature decoder peripherals that update position counters automatically, offloading the CPU and ensuring no pulses are missed even during interrupt latency.

Index pulses, designated the Z channel, provide a once-per-revolution reference that enables absolute position homing. The controller monitors the Z pulse and latches the current encoder count, establishing a known zero position. In multi-turn absolute positioning, the system must either retain position across power cycles (using non-volatile memory) or execute a homing sequence on startup, commanding the motor to seek a mechanical limit switch or optical home sensor.

Resolvers output two analog voltages, sine and cosine, modulated by a high-frequency carrier (typically 2 kHz to 10 kHz). The resolver-to-digital converter (RDC) demodulates these signals and computes the arctangent to extract the shaft angle. High-resolution RDCs achieve 12-bit to 16-bit angular resolution, corresponding to 0.088° to 0.0055° per LSB. The arctangent computation introduces latency (tens to hundreds of microseconds), but resolvers offer ruggedness and immunity to particulate contamination that would cripple optical encoders in harsh environments such as aerospace actuators and oil drilling equipment.

Stepper motor control diverges from servo control in that the fundamental control variable is not voltage or current magnitude, but rather the sequencing and timing of phase excitation. A bipolar stepper motor contains two independent phase windings, each requiring bidirectional current flow. Full-step excitation energizes one phase or both phases simultaneously in a repeating four-state sequence: A+ (phase A forward), B+ (phase B forward), A− (phase A reverse), B− (phase B reverse). Each transition advances the rotor by one full step (1.8° for a 200-step motor). Half-stepping interleaves single-phase and dual-phase states, doubling resolution to 400 steps per revolution. Microstepping subdivides steps further by driving phase currents with sinusoidal waveforms, typically implemented via pulse-width modulation (PWM) from a microcontroller digital-to-analog converter (DAC) or a dedicated stepper driver IC with integrated current regulation.

The commanded phase currents for microstepping follow:

```text
I_A = I_max * cos(θ)
I_B = I_max * sin(θ)
```

where θ is the electrical angle (related to the step number by the motor's electrical period) and I_max is the rated phase current. A 1/16 microstepping driver updates θ in increments of 2π/16 per full step, yielding 3200 microsteps per revolution. The stepper driver's current-regulation loop, typically implemented with a chopper circuit operating at 20 kHz to 50 kHz, measures phase current via sense resistors and modulates the H-bridge duty cycle to maintain the commanded current despite back-EMF and winding resistance variations.

Stepper motor torque is maximum at zero speed (holding torque) and declines with increasing velocity due to inductive reactance limiting current slew rate. The pull-out torque curve, published in motor datasheets, defines the maximum load torque the motor can sustain at each speed without stalling. Engineers must ensure that the load torque, including acceleration torque (inertia times angular acceleration), never exceeds the pull-out torque curve, or the motor will lose synchronization and stall. Microstepping reduces mid-band resonance by smoothing torque ripple, but it does not fundamentally alter the pull-out torque characteristic, which remains dominated by winding inductance and drive voltage.

Closed-loop stepper systems add an encoder and a position error detector. If the encoder position deviates from the commanded step count by more than a threshold (typically one to four full steps), the controller injects corrective steps to resynchronize. This hybrid approach preserves the simplicity of step-and-direction control while providing the reliability of feedback, preventing catastrophic step loss in high-acceleration or variable-load applications such as pick-and-place machines and packaging lines.


## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Achieving high bandwidth and tight position accuracy in a servo system demands rigorous attention to loop timing, quantization effects, and mechanical resonances. The control loop sample rate establishes the Nyquist frequency above which the controller cannot respond to disturbances. A 1 kHz sample rate limits the achievable closed-loop bandwidth to roughly 100 Hz to 200 Hz, depending on phase margin requirements. Increasing the sample rate to 10 kHz or 20 kHz permits bandwidths exceeding 1 kHz, but at the cost of increased computational load and reduced noise immunity, since sensor quantization noise is amplified at high derivative gains.

Encoder quantization introduces a sawtooth position error with amplitude equal to one encoder count. For a 4000-count-per-revolution encoder, this quantization is 0.09°, or approximately 1.57 milliradians. If the load radius is 50 mm, the linear position quantization at the load is 78.5 μm. High-resolution applications such as photolithography stages or telescope drives employ encoders with 10,000 to 100,000 counts per revolution, reducing quantization to submicron levels. Interpolation of analog quadrature signals can further subdivide encoder counts electronically, but this approach is sensitive to encoder imperfections such as DC offset, amplitude imbalance, and harmonic distortion.

Current-loop bandwidth in servo amplifiers must exceed position-loop bandwidth by at least a factor of five to ensure the current (torque) command tracks the PID output without introducing additional phase lag. A position loop running at 1 kHz with 100 Hz bandwidth requires a current loop bandwidth of at least 500 Hz, implying a current-loop sample rate of 5 kHz to 10 kHz. Cascade control architectures implement this hierarchy explicitly, with an inner current loop regulating phase currents and an outer position loop commanding torque via current references.

PID tuning in practice begins with proportional gain. Starting from zero, the engineer increases Kp until the system exhibits sustained oscillation or unacceptable overshoot in response to a step command. The gain is then reduced by a factor of two to three, providing a stable proportional-only baseline. Next, integral gain Ki is increased gradually until steady-state error vanishes and the system settles within the desired tolerance. Excessive integral gain causes overshoot and ringing; anti-windup protection mitigates but does not eliminate this effect. Finally, derivative gain Kd is introduced to dampen overshoot and reduce settling time. Derivative gain amplifies high-frequency noise, so it is often filtered with a first-order low-pass filter (derivative-on-measurement rather than derivative-on-error) or limited to a maximum value.

Ziegler-Nichols tuning, a classical empirical method, determines PID gains from the ultimate gain Ku (the proportional gain at the onset of sustained oscillation) and the ultimate period Tu (the oscillation period at Ku). The method prescribes:

```text
Kp = 0.6 * Ku
Ki = 1.2 * Ku / Tu
Kd = 0.075 * Ku * Tu
```

This recipe often produces aggressive tuning suitable for step response but marginal phase margin, so practitioners frequently scale the gains down by 30% to 50% for production systems where robustness trumps speed.

Frequency-domain tuning leverages Bode plots of the open-loop transfer function to visualize gain and phase margins. The engineer measures or models the plant transfer function (motor, load, and sensor), then designs the compensator to achieve desired crossover frequency (where open-loop gain equals unity) and phase margin (phase angle at crossover plus 180°). Software tools such as MATLAB Control System Toolbox or Python Control library automate Bode analysis and permit rapid iteration on compensator designs, including lead-lag filters, notch filters, and state-space observers.

Mechanical resonances manifest as peaks in the frequency response, indicating frequencies where the system preferentially oscillates. A flexible coupling or a long lead screw may introduce a resonance at 50 Hz to 200 Hz, causing the closed-loop system to ring or become unstable. Notch filters placed in the feedback path or the command path attenuate gain at the resonant frequency, restoring stability at the cost of reduced bandwidth. A digital notch filter with center frequency f₀ and quality factor Q is implemented as a biquad IIR filter with coefficients derived from the bilinear transform:

```text
H(z) = (b₀ + b₁ z⁻¹ + b₂ z⁻²) / (1 + a₁ z⁻¹ + a₂ z⁻²)
```

where the coefficients are functions of f₀, Q, and the sample rate. Notch filters must be tuned precisely to the resonance frequency; mistuning by even a few percent reduces effectiveness and may introduce phase lag that degrades stability margins elsewhere.

Stepper motor resonance occurs when the step rate coincides with the rotor's natural mechanical frequency, typically between 100 Hz and 300 Hz for NEMA 17 and NEMA 23 motors. At resonance, the rotor oscillates wildly, torque drops precipitously, and steps may be lost. Microstepping mitigates resonance by smoothing torque ripple, but it does not eliminate the fundamental mechanical mode. External damping—such as viscous fluid dampers or elastomeric couplings—dissipates energy and suppresses resonance amplitude. Alternatively, the motion profile can ramp through the resonant frequency quickly, spending minimal time at the critical speed.

Step loss in open-loop stepper systems is silent and catastrophic: once the rotor falls behind the commanded position by more than roughly two full steps, the motor loses synchronization entirely, either stalling or slewing uncontrollably. Detecting step loss without an encoder requires indirect methods such as monitoring motor current (a stalled motor draws excessive current) or back-EMF (which vanishes when the rotor stops). Closed-loop stepper systems detect step loss immediately via encoder feedback and inject corrective steps, but the corrective motion itself may violate motion profile constraints or trigger limit switches if the position error is large.

Torque ripple, caused by cogging (magnetic detent torque when unpowered) and commutation errors (imperfect current waveforms), degrades positioning accuracy and induces vibration. Cogging torque in BLDC motors arises from the interaction between permanent magnets and stator slot openings, producing a periodic torque variation at six times (for a three-phase motor) the electrical frequency. Commutation torque ripple results from non-sinusoidal back-EMF or imperfect current regulation. Field-oriented control (FOC) minimizes commutation ripple by aligning stator currents with rotor flux, while skewed stator laminations or fractional-slot winding designs reduce cogging at the mechanical design level.

Friction, both static (stiction) and dynamic (Coulomb and viscous), opposes motion and introduces nonlinearity into the control loop. Stiction causes the motor to remain stationary despite small command signals, then lurch forward once the torque exceeds the breakaway threshold. This produces limit cycling at low speeds and steady-state position error. Friction compensation techniques include adding a constant torque offset to overcome Coulomb friction, feeding forward a velocity-dependent torque to cancel viscous friction, and employing dither signals (small, high-frequency oscillations) to keep the system moving and avoid stiction.

Thermal management limits continuous motor torque below the peak rated torque. A servo motor's thermal time constant ranges from minutes to tens of minutes, meaning that short-duration peak torque bursts do not immediately overheat the windings, but sustained operation at high torque raises winding temperature until thermal protection trips or insulation degrades. Duty cycle calculations, which integrate the square of motor current over time (I²t), predict thermal rise and ensure the motor operates within safe limits. Active cooling via forced air or liquid cooling extends continuous torque capability, but at the cost of added complexity and potential coolant leaks.

Electrical noise couples into encoder signals via capacitive or magnetic pickup, producing false edges that corrupt position measurements. Differential signaling (RS-422 or LVDS) for encoder channels provides common-mode noise rejection, but shielded twisted-pair cables and careful grounding are essential in electrically noisy environments such as industrial motor drives adjacent to variable-frequency drives (VFDs) or switching power supplies. Optical isolation between encoder inputs and the microcontroller ground plane breaks ground loops and prevents transient voltage spikes from damaging logic circuits.

Aliasing occurs when sensor signals or disturbances contain frequency components above the Nyquist frequency (half the sample rate), causing high-frequency content to fold back into the control bandwidth as low-frequency artifacts. Anti-aliasing filters—low-pass analog filters placed before analog-to-digital converters—attenuate high-frequency content, but they introduce phase lag that reduces phase margin. The trade-off between aliasing suppression and phase margin drives the choice of sample rate: faster sampling permits steeper anti-aliasing filters with less in-band phase shift.

Jitter in control loop timing, caused by interrupt latency variations, task preemption, or communication delays, destabilizes high-bandwidth loops by introducing phase noise. Real-time operating systems (RTOS) with deterministic interrupt response and hardware timer peripherals configured for precise periodic execution minimize jitter. In multi-axis systems, synchronizing control loops across axes ensures coordinated motion and prevents cross-coupling errors, often achieved by triggering all axis controllers from a common hardware timer or etherCAT distributed clock.

Quantization in the digital-to-analog converter (DAC) that generates motor drive voltages or the PWM duty cycle that synthesizes average voltage limits the smallest controllable torque increment. A 12-bit DAC driving a ±10 V motor amplifier quantizes voltage into 4096 steps, or approximately 4.88 mV per step. If the motor's torque constant is 0.1 Nm/A and the current sense gain is 1 A/V, the minimum torque step is roughly 0.488 mN·m. For high-precision systems, 16-bit DACs or high-resolution PWM (20-bit or greater, achievable with dithering techniques) reduce quantization to negligible levels.

Integral windup during large setpoint changes or sustained actuator saturation causes overshoot and oscillation when the system finally reaches the target. Conditional integration—freezing the integrator when the error exceeds a threshold or when the actuator saturates—provides a simple remedy. Back-calculation anti-windup, as described earlier, adjusts the integrator in real time to prevent accumulation during saturation. Gain scheduling, where PID gains adapt based on operating conditions (such as reducing integral gain during large errors), offers a more sophisticated solution but complicates implementation and tuning.

Cascaded control loops—position outer loop, velocity middle loop, current inner loop—provide structural separation of timescales and improve disturbance rejection. The current loop runs fastest (5 kHz to 20 kHz), regulating motor current with millisecond response times. The velocity loop operates at an intermediate rate (1 kHz to 5 kHz), commanding current to achieve desired velocity. The position loop runs slowest (100 Hz to 1 kHz), commanding velocity to track position trajectories. Each loop is tuned independently, with the inner loops designed to appear as unity-gain elements to the outer loops, simplifying analysis and ensuring robust stability across the cascade.

State-space control and observers, including Kalman filters and Luenberger observers, estimate unmeasured states (such as load torque disturbances or velocity between encoder counts) and enable advanced control strategies such as pole placement and Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) design. These methods require accurate plant models and computational resources beyond simple PID, but they unlock superior performance in high-precision applications such as semiconductor wafer stages and hard disk drive head positioning.

Real-world servo tuning is an iterative process of experimentation, measurement, and refinement. Engineers begin with coarse tuning on the benchtop, then refine gains under representative load conditions, thermal states, and environmental disturbances. Logging position error, control effort, and sensor signals during operation reveals pathologies invisible in static testing. Frequency sweeps—commanding the system with sinusoidal position references across a range of frequencies—generate empirical Bode plots that validate models and expose unmodeled dynamics. This empirical rigor separates functional prototypes from production-ready systems that achieve specified performance across the full operating envelope.
