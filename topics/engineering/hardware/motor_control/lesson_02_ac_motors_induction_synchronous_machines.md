# Lesson 2: AC Motors—Induction, Synchronous Machines

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The alternating current motor represents one of humanity's most elegant transformations of electrical energy into mechanical work, a conversion governed by the immutable principles of electromagnetic induction discovered by Faraday and refined by Tesla, Steinmetz, and countless power engineers who followed. Unlike the brushed DC motor, which requires physical commutation through carbon contacts that spark, wear, and ultimately fail, the AC motor achieves continuous rotation through the creation of a rotating magnetic field in the stator windings—a field that drags the rotor along through electromagnetic coupling without any sliding electrical contact. This fundamental architectural advantage enables AC motors to dominate industrial automation, electric vehicle propulsion, HVAC compressor systems, and any application where reliability, power density, and controllability converge.

At the heart of every AC motor lies the principle of the rotating magnetic field. When three sinusoidal currents, phase-shifted by 120 electrical degrees, flow through three sets of stator windings arranged symmetrically around a cylindrical air gap, the resultant magnetic flux vector traces a perfect circle in space as time advances. This rotating field possesses a synchronous speed determined solely by the supply frequency and the number of magnetic pole pairs: $N_s = \frac{120 f}{P}$, where $N_s$ is synchronous speed in revolutions per minute, $f$ is electrical frequency in hertz, and $P$ is the total number of poles. For a 60 Hz industrial supply driving a four-pole motor, synchronous speed becomes 1800 RPM—a speed that serves as the reference frame for understanding rotor behavior in both induction and synchronous machine topologies.

The induction motor deviates from this synchronous speed through a phenomenon called slip. The rotor, composed of either laminated iron bars short-circuited at the ends (squirrel cage construction) or wound coils accessible through slip rings, experiences a time-varying magnetic flux whenever its rotational speed differs from the stator field's synchronous velocity. By Lenz's law, this relative motion induces currents in the rotor conductors, and those currents interact with the stator field to produce torque. The magnitude of this induced current—and therefore the developed torque—depends directly on the slip magnitude: $s = \frac{N_s - N_r}{N_s}$, where $N_r$ represents actual rotor speed. At standstill, slip equals unity, inducing maximum rotor current and producing high starting torque but also dissipating substantial power as $I^2R$ heating in the rotor bars. As the rotor accelerates toward synchronous speed, slip decreases, reducing induced rotor currents and eventually reaching an equilibrium where electromagnetic torque precisely balances the mechanical load. Critically, an induction motor can never reach true synchronous speed; if it did, there would be no relative motion, no induced currents, and consequently no torque to sustain rotation against even infinitesimal friction.

The synchronous motor family abandons this slip mechanism entirely. By establishing a DC magnetic field on the rotor—either through permanent magnets in the permanent magnet synchronous motor (PMSM) or through salient pole reluctance variations in the synchronous reluctance motor—the rotor locks into phase with the stator's rotating field and spins at exactly synchronous speed under steady-state conditions. The PMSM rotor embeds high-energy rare-earth magnets (neodymium-iron-boron or samarium-cobalt) that create a constant-magnitude magnetic dipole. When this dipole lags the stator field by an angle $\delta$ (the torque angle or load angle), a restoring torque proportional to $\sin(\delta)$ pulls the rotor back into alignment, allowing the motor to produce continuous torque while maintaining synchronous speed. The synchronous reluctance motor eschews magnets altogether, instead shaping the rotor lamination stack with alternating regions of high and low magnetic permeability—flux barriers and flux paths—that create anisotropic reluctance. The stator field magnetizes these salient poles, and the rotor aligns with the field to minimize reluctance, producing torque proportional to the sine of twice the displacement angle.

The electrical characteristics of these machines dictate their per-phase equivalent circuit models. For the induction motor, the stator winding appears as a series resistance $R_s$ and leakage inductance $L_{ls}$, feeding a mutual magnetizing inductance $L_m$ in parallel with the reflected rotor circuit. This reflected rotor branch consists of a slip-dependent resistance $\frac{R_r}{s}$ and rotor leakage inductance $L_{lr}$, where the division by slip elegantly captures the fact that rotor power dissipation ($I_r^2 R_r$) and mechanical power output ($I_r^2 R_r \frac{1-s}{s}$) together account for the air-gap power transferred across the electromagnetic coupling. When slip approaches unity at startup, $\frac{R_r}{s}$ becomes simply $R_r$, presenting low impedance that draws large inrush currents—often five to seven times rated current—necessitating soft-start strategies in applications where supply infrastructure cannot tolerate such transients.

The PMSM equivalent circuit simplifies the rotor representation by replacing the slip-dependent impedance with a back-EMF voltage source proportional to rotor speed and magnet flux linkage: $e_{back} = k_e \omega_m$, where $k_e$ is the motor voltage constant and $\omega_m$ is mechanical angular velocity in radians per second. This back-EMF opposes the applied stator voltage, and the difference drives current through the stator resistance and synchronous inductance. The synchronous inductance typically decomposes into direct-axis ($L_d$) and quadrature-axis ($L_q$) components when analyzed in the rotor reference frame, a distinction that becomes critical for field-oriented control algorithms. In surface-mount PMSMs, where magnets affix to the rotor outer diameter, $L_d \approx L_q$, yielding minimal reluctance torque and torque production dominated by the interaction between stator current and permanent magnet flux. In interior permanent magnet (IPM) motors, magnets embed within the rotor iron, creating significant saliency with $L_q > L_d$, enabling reluctance torque to augment magnet torque and extend the constant-power operating region—a feature exploited in electric vehicle traction drives.

Torque production in all AC motors traces back to the cross product of magnetic fields or, equivalently in the circuit domain, the interaction between current-carrying conductors and magnetic flux. For the induction motor, torque develops as $T = \frac{3 P}{2} \cdot \frac{V^2 R_r / s}{(R_s + R_r/s)^2 + (X_s + X_r)^2}$, revealing a characteristic torque-speed curve that rises from zero at synchronous speed, peaks at the breakdown torque when $\frac{R_r}{s} = \sqrt{R_s^2 + (X_s + X_r)^2}$, and decreases toward zero again at standstill due to high rotor impedance. The peak torque location, typically occurring at 10–25% slip depending on motor design, defines the motor's overload capacity and sets the boundary between stable and unstable operating regions. Operating beyond breakdown torque on the high-slip side invites catastrophic runaway deceleration, as increasing load causes further speed reduction, which decreases torque availability, perpetuating a collapse that can only be arrested by removing load or cycling power.

The PMSM torque equation in the rotating $dq$ reference frame expresses as $T = \frac{3 P}{2} [\lambda_{pm} i_q + (L_d - L_q) i_d i_q]$, where $\lambda_{pm}$ is the permanent magnet flux linkage, $i_d$ and $i_q$ are the direct and quadrature axis currents, and $P$ is pole pairs. The first term captures magnet torque, directly proportional to $i_q$ alone, while the second term represents reluctance torque arising from saliency. In surface-mount machines with negligible saliency, torque control reduces to the elegantly simple task of regulating quadrature current while maintaining $i_d = 0$, maximizing torque per ampere and minimizing resistive losses. In IPM machines, optimal torque generation often requires a negative $i_d$ component that weakens the net flux linkage—field weakening—allowing the motor to operate at speeds above the base speed where back-EMF would otherwise saturate the inverter's voltage delivery capability.

The transition from understanding these first-principles relationships to commanding real motors on the benchtop demands a control framework that decouples torque and flux, much as the separately excited DC motor naturally separates armature current (torque) from field excitation (flux). This requirement births field-oriented control (FOC), a transformation-based technique that projects the three-phase stator currents into a synchronously rotating reference frame aligned with the rotor flux vector, yielding independent control over torque-producing and flux-producing current components. The mathematical machinery involves the Clarke transform (converting three-phase $abc$ quantities to stationary two-phase $\alpha\beta$ coordinates) followed by the Park transform (rotating $\alpha\beta$ into the $dq$ frame using the instantaneous rotor position obtained from an encoder or sensorless estimator). With torque now proportional to a single current component, proportional-integral (PI) controllers in the $dq$ domain can regulate torque and flux with DC setpoints, avoiding the phase-tracking complexity of directly controlling sinusoidal currents in the stationary frame.

The practical realization of FOC on a microcontroller—typically an ARM Cortex-M4 or M7 with floating-point unit and hardware trigonometric accelerators—requires precise rotor position feedback at sampling rates exceeding 10 kHz to maintain control bandwidth sufficient for rejecting load disturbances and tracking speed transients. A quadrature encoder generates A and B channel pulses that increment or decrement a hardware counter register, with position resolution determined by counts per revolution (CPR); a 4096 CPR encoder on a 10-pole motor provides 8192 electrical counts per mechanical revolution, yielding 0.044 electrical degrees per count—adequate for smooth torque production but marginal for low-speed performance where quantization noise becomes perceptible. Higher-performance systems employ absolute encoders (BiSS, EnDat, or Tamagawa protocols) or resolvers paired with resolver-to-digital converters (RDCs) that deliver absolute position without homing sequences and immunity to power-cycle loss of commutation phasing.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The physical construction of an induction motor begins with the stator, a hollow cylinder of laminated electrical steel stacked axially and punched with slots that house insulated copper windings. The lamination thickness—typically 0.35 mm to 0.50 mm—minimizes eddy current losses induced by the time-varying flux, with each lamination insulated by a thin oxide or organic coating. The slots run parallel to the motor axis, and three sets of coils—corresponding to phases A, B, and C—occupy the slots in a distributed pattern designed to produce a sinusoidal magnetomotive force (MMF) distribution when energized. A two-pole motor features coils spanning 180 electrical degrees (half the circumference), while a four-pole motor employs coil spans of 90 mechanical degrees corresponding to 180 electrical degrees per pole pair. The end windings, where conductors transition between slots, extend beyond the lamination stack and represent parasitic inductance and resistance that contribute to copper losses but do not participate directly in torque production.

The squirrel-cage rotor, the most common induction motor construction, consists of a laminated iron stack with longitudinal slots near the outer diameter, into which aluminum or copper bars are cast or brazed. Short-circuiting end rings connect all bars at both axial ends, forming a cage structure with no external electrical connections. When the stator's rotating field sweeps past the rotor bars, it induces voltages according to Faraday's law: $\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt} = -\frac{d\Phi}{d\theta} \frac{d\theta}{dt}$, where $\frac{d\theta}{dt}$ represents the relative angular velocity between field and rotor (the slip speed). These induced voltages drive currents through the low-resistance cage, and the interaction of these currents with the stator flux produces a tangential force on each bar. Summing forces over all bars at a given radius yields the electromagnetic torque, which accelerates the rotor until slip-dependent torque balances load torque.

The number of rotor bars relative to stator slots influences harmonic content and parasitic torques. If the rotor bar count equals the stator slot count or a multiple thereof, the magnetic permeance variations synchronize, producing cogging torque—a pulsating torque independent of current that causes speed ripple and acoustic noise. Designers typically select rotor bar counts that avoid integer multiples and common factors with the stator slot number, often targeting a difference of ±1 or ±2 bars relative to slots to distribute harmonic interactions across the electrical cycle. Additionally, rotor bars may be skewed—twisted slightly along the axis—by one stator slot pitch to average out slotting harmonics and reduce torque ripple at the expense of slightly increased leakage inductance and reduced torque density.

The PMSM rotor architecture diverges fundamentally from the induction motor by replacing the passive cage with a permanent magnet structure that generates a constant rotor flux. In surface-mount configurations, arc-segment magnets adhere to the rotor outer diameter and face the air gap directly, typically retained by a carbon-fiber or Inconel sleeve to prevent demagnetization from centrifugal forces at high speeds. The magnet material—most commonly sintered neodymium-iron-boron (Nd₂Fe₁₄B)—exhibits energy products exceeding 350 kJ/m³ and coercivities beyond 1000 kA/m, enabling compact designs with high torque density. However, these magnets suffer from temperature sensitivity; the remanent flux density decreases approximately 0.11% per degree Celsius, and the coercivity drops more sharply, risking irreversible demagnetization if winding currents produce opposing fields during fault conditions or aggressive field weakening. Maximum operating temperatures rarely exceed 150°C for standard grades, necessitating thermal management through forced air cooling, liquid cooling jackets, or finned motor housings with thermal interface materials that minimize junction-to-ambient thermal resistance.

Interior permanent magnet (IPM) rotors embed magnets within the lamination stack, forming flux barriers that create magnetic saliency. A typical IPM design arranges magnets in a V-shaped pattern or multi-layer configuration, with iron bridges separating magnets from the rotor surface to provide mechanical support. This geometry forces flux produced by stator currents to take high-reluctance paths through the bridges in the $d$-axis direction while allowing flux to flow more freely along the $q$-axis iron paths. The resulting inductance anisotropy ($L_q > L_d$) generates reluctance torque that supplements magnet torque, increasing power density by 15–30% compared to surface-mount designs. Furthermore, the magnetic flux from stator currents in the $d$-axis direction opposes the permanent magnet flux, enabling field weakening without excessive current; this feature allows IPM motors to operate at multiple times base speed while maintaining rated power, a capability essential for electric vehicle applications where the motor must deliver high torque at low speeds for acceleration and sustained power at highway speeds.

The synchronous reluctance motor eliminates permanent magnets entirely, relying solely on saliency to produce torque. The rotor laminations feature alternating regions of iron and air-filled flux barriers, creating a strongly anisotropic magnetic structure with $L_q$ often three to five times greater than $L_d$. Because there is no permanent magnet flux linkage ($\lambda_{pm} = 0$), the torque equation simplifies to $T = \frac{3 P}{2} (L_d - L_q) i_d i_q$, revealing that torque arises purely from the interaction of $d$-axis and $q$-axis currents with the reluctance variation. This architecture eliminates concerns about magnet demagnetization, reduces material costs by avoiding rare-earth elements, and simplifies high-temperature operation. However, the absence of permanent magnet torque demands higher stator currents for equivalent torque output, increasing resistive losses and reducing efficiency by 2–5 percentage points compared to PMSMs. Synchronous reluctance motors find niches in applications where cost and thermal robustness outweigh efficiency premiums, such as HVAC fans, pumps, and appliances.

The electromagnetic air gap separating stator and rotor critically determines motor performance characteristics. Typical air gaps range from 0.3 mm to 1.0 mm in small motors (under 10 kW) and expand to 1.5 mm to 3.0 mm in larger industrial machines. A smaller air gap reduces the magnetizing current required to establish working flux density, improving power factor and efficiency, but tightens manufacturing tolerances and increases susceptibility to rotor-stator contact during thermal expansion or bearing wear. The air gap also sets the machine's effective inductance and time constants; a large air gap increases the magnetizing inductance and the rotor time constant $\tau_r = \frac{L_r}{R_r}$, slowing the motor's dynamic response to torque commands and reducing the bandwidth of speed control loops.

Phase winding connections determine the motor's voltage and current ratings. A star (wye) connection ties one end of each phase winding to a common neutral point, with the other ends brought out to the three-phase terminals. This configuration allows the motor to be driven either phase-to-neutral (line voltage divided by $\sqrt{3}$) or phase-to-phase, and the line current equals the phase current. A delta connection connects the end of each phase winding to the start of the next, forming a closed triangle with no neutral point, and the line current exceeds the phase current by $\sqrt{3}$. For a given torque output, a delta-connected motor draws $\sqrt{3}$ times the line current at $1/\sqrt{3}$ the line voltage compared to the same motor in star configuration, effectively reducing the voltage requirement but increasing current—a tradeoff exploited in dual-voltage motors that can be reconfigured for 230 V or 460 V operation by switching between delta and star connections.

The drive electronics responsible for synthesizing three-phase currents consist of a three-phase inverter bridge, typically implemented with insulated-gate bipolar transistors (IGBTs) or silicon carbide (SiC) MOSFETs. Each phase leg comprises a high-side switch and a low-side switch, with the motor phase terminal connected to the midpoint between switches. A DC bus voltage, derived from rectified AC mains or a battery pack, supplies the inverter, and pulse-width modulation (PWM) commands control the switch duty cycles to approximate sinusoidal phase voltages. The PWM switching frequency—commonly 10 kHz to 20 kHz for IGBT drives and 20 kHz to 50 kHz for SiC drives—represents a tradeoff between switching losses (increasing with frequency) and harmonic distortion (decreasing with frequency). Higher switching frequencies reduce acoustic noise by pushing the primary switching harmonics beyond the audible range and enable higher control loop bandwidths, but they also increase electromagnetic interference (EMI) that must be filtered to comply with conducted and radiated emission standards (e.g., CISPR 11, FCC Part 15).

Space vector modulation (SVM), the dominant PWM algorithm for motor drives, recognizes that the eight possible switch states (including two zero vectors where all phases connect to the same rail) define vertices of a hexagon in the $\alpha\beta$ plane, and any desired voltage vector within this hexagon can be synthesized by time-averaging the two adjacent vertices plus a zero vector. SVM delivers approximately 15% greater DC bus utilization compared to sinusoidal PWM by fully exploiting the hexagonal boundary, allowing the motor to reach higher speeds before entering the field-weakening region. The SVM algorithm computes the sector, determines the duty cycles for the two active vectors and the zero vector, and distributes the zero vector time between the two available zero states to center the pulses within each PWM period—a technique that minimizes switching transitions and reduces losses.

Current sensing closes the control loop by measuring instantaneous phase currents, typically using inline shunt resistors or Hall-effect current transducers. Shunt resistors, placed in series with the inverter low-side switches or the DC bus return path, generate a voltage proportional to current that is amplified by a differential op-amp and sampled by the microcontroller's analog-to-digital converter (ADC). Shunt sensing offers accuracy better than 1%, excellent linearity, and minimal temperature drift, but it dissipates power ($I^2 R$) and requires careful PCB layout to reject common-mode noise coupled through the motor cable. Typical shunt resistances range from 0.5 mΩ to 5 mΩ, chosen to balance signal amplitude (higher resistance yields better signal-to-noise ratio) against power dissipation (lower resistance reduces heat). At 30 A phase current and 1 mΩ shunt resistance, power dissipation reaches 0.9 W per phase, totaling 2.7 W for three-phase sensing—a marginal penalty in kilowatt-class drives but significant in battery-powered applications where every watt of auxiliary loss depletes range.

The ADC sampling must synchronize precisely with the PWM cycle to avoid measuring currents during switching transitions, when diode recovery currents and parasitic ringing corrupt the measurements. Most motor control microcontrollers trigger ADC conversions at the peak or valley of the PWM triangle carrier, ensuring that all switches remain in a stable state. With 12-bit ADC resolution and a ±60 A measurement range, each LSB corresponds to 29 mA, providing adequate resolution for smooth torque control. Higher-end systems employ 16-bit delta-sigma ADCs that oversample and digitally filter the shunt voltage, achieving noise floors below 10 mA RMS and enabling precise current control even at light loads where resistive losses typically obscure efficiency.

Rotor position estimation in sensorless control schemes exploits the back-EMF or saliency signatures embedded in the motor's electrical response. For PMSMs and IPM motors, the induced back-EMF voltage provides an inherent position signal at moderate to high speeds, where back-EMF magnitude exceeds the voltage drop across winding resistance. An observer algorithm—commonly a sliding-mode observer or extended Kalman filter—processes the measured phase currents and applied voltages to estimate the back-EMF vector, whose angle in the $\alpha\beta$ plane directly reveals rotor position. However, at low speeds and standstill, back-EMF amplitude diminishes to zero, and position estimation relies instead on inductance saliency. High-frequency voltage injection, typically a 500 Hz to 2 kHz signal superimposed on the fundamental excitation, probes the rotor's $d$-axis and $q$-axis inductances. The resulting high-frequency current response exhibits an amplitude modulation that encodes the rotor position angle, which a demodulation algorithm extracts using bandpass filters and arctangent operations. This sensorless technique enables smooth operation down to zero speed, at the cost of audible noise from the injection frequency and increased computational load on the microcontroller.

The computational burden of FOC at 20 kHz PWM frequency imposes stringent real-time requirements on the microcontroller. A complete control cycle—reading ADC samples, executing Clarke and Park transforms, running PI controllers, performing inverse Park and SVM, and updating PWM compare registers—must complete within 50 microseconds. On an ARM Cortex-M4 clocked at 168 MHz, the Clarke and Park transforms consume approximately 1 microsecond when implemented with floating-point arithmetic (leveraging the FPU), while the two PI controllers (one for $i_d$, one for $i_q$) each require 0.5 microseconds. The inverse Park and SVM operations add another 2 microseconds, and ADC readout plus peripheral updates consume 1 microsecond, totaling 5 microseconds of core algorithm execution. This leaves substantial margin for higher-level speed controllers, communication protocol handlers, and safety monitoring tasks, but the architecture demands careful interrupt prioritization to prevent lower-priority tasks from preempting the time-critical current control interrupt service routine (ISR).

Memory architecture also shapes performance. The core FOC algorithm benefits from placing lookup tables (sine/cosine for Park transforms) in tightly coupled memory (TCM) with single-cycle access latency, avoiding the multi-cycle penalties of flash wait states. A 360-element sine table with 16-bit entries occupies 720 bytes, and caching the most recent Park transform angle reduces redundant trigonometric computations when the rotor position changes less than one electrical degree between samples. Some advanced implementations leverage the CMSIS-DSP library, which provides SIMD-optimized implementations of vector operations that exploit the ARM NEON extensions available on Cortex-A series processors or the single-instruction-multiple-data (SIMD) instructions on Cortex-M7 cores, achieving 2× to 4× throughput improvements for matrix multiplies and trigonometric functions.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Pushing AC motor systems toward their performance limits requires confronting the thermal, electromagnetic, and control-theoretic constraints that define the operational envelope. The first and most pervasive limiter is winding temperature, governed by the interplay of copper losses, iron losses, and heat transfer coefficients. Copper losses dominate at low speeds, scaling as $I^2 R_{phase}$, where resistance increases approximately 0.4% per degree Celsius due to copper's positive temperature coefficient. A motor rated for 30 A RMS at 25°C ambient with 0.2 Ω phase resistance dissipates 180 W as heat in the windings. If the thermal resistance from winding to ambient is 0.5°C/W (typical for a TEFC—totally enclosed fan-cooled—frame), the winding temperature rises 90°C, reaching 115°C. Operating at 40 A RMS (33% overload) increases losses to 320 W and winding temperature to 185°C, approaching the 200°C rating of Class H insulation but risking accelerated aging. Insulation life halves for every 10°C increase above rated temperature, a relationship codified in the Arrhenius equation for thermally activated degradation processes.

Iron losses, proportional to $f^{1.5}$ to $f^{2}$ depending on eddy current and hysteresis contributions, escalate rapidly at high speeds. A motor designed for 3600 RPM (60 Hz) operation experiences approximately 2.25× higher iron losses when driven at 5400 RPM (90 Hz) in the field-weakening region, even if torque output decreases proportionally to maintain constant power. This loss increase compounds the thermal challenge, particularly in interior permanent magnet motors where rotor magnets are embedded within iron and cannot be directly cooled by airflow. Liquid cooling jackets surrounding the stator outer diameter offer superior thermal performance, reducing winding-to-coolant thermal resistance below 0.1°C/W, but they introduce sealing complexity, potential coolant leakage, and increased system weight—a tradeoff accepted in high-performance traction motors but avoided in cost-sensitive industrial applications.

Derating curves quantify these thermal limits across the torque-speed plane. A typical motor delivers rated torque continuously from zero speed to base speed, where back-EMF equals the inverter's voltage delivery capability. Above base speed, the motor enters field weakening, and torque decreases hyperbolically to maintain constant power. At twice base speed, available torque halves; at three times base speed, torque falls to one-third. However, the high-speed region also imposes current limits because the inverter's maximum modulation index constrains the fundamental voltage amplitude to approximately 0.612 times the DC bus voltage (sinusoidal PWM) or 0.707 times DC bus (SVM). Attempting to exceed this limit forces the controller to over-modulate, injecting low-order harmonics into the phase currents that produce pulsating torque and acoustic noise. In PMSMs, aggressive field weakening can drive $i_d$ to magnitudes exceeding $i_q$, increasing total RMS current and copper losses while producing less torque—a regime with degraded efficiency that some controllers prohibit through software torque limiters.

Mechanical resonances impose another performance boundary. Every motor-load system exhibits torsional resonances where the rotor inertia, shaft compliance, and load inertia form a spring-mass system with natural frequencies typically between 10 Hz and 200 Hz. Exciting these resonances during rapid torque transients produces oscillatory speed errors that the velocity controller may inadvertently amplify if its bandwidth intersects the resonant frequency. A proportional-integral (PI) velocity controller with 50 Hz bandwidth attempting to track a step speed command on a system with a 60 Hz torsional resonance will exhibit sustained oscillations as the controller interprets the mechanical ringing as a tracking error and applies corrective torque that reinforces the oscillation. Mitigation strategies include notch filters tuned to the resonant frequency, which attenuate the controller's gain at resonance by 20 dB to 40 dB while preserving bandwidth elsewhere, or acceleration feedforward terms that preemptively apply the torque required for speed changes without waiting for error accumulation.

Electrical resonances between the motor inductance and inverter capacitance can also destabilize the system. The LC tank formed by motor leakage inductance (typically 0.5 mH to 5 mH) and inverter DC bus capacitance (100 µF to 2000 µF) exhibits a resonant frequency $f_{res} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$. For a motor with 2 mH leakage inductance and 500 µF bus capacitance, the resonance occurs at 159 Hz. If the current control loop's bandwidth approaches or exceeds this frequency, oscillations appear in the measured currents, and the controller may become unstable. Conservative current controller design targets bandwidths no higher than one-fifth the resonant frequency, but this limits dynamic torque response. Active damping techniques inject a corrective voltage proportional to the rate of current change, effectively introducing a virtual resistance that damps the LC resonance without physical resistive losses.

Encoder resolution limitations manifest as quantization noise in the position feedback signal, particularly at low speeds where the number of encoder counts per control cycle becomes small. A 1024-count-per-revolution (CPR) encoder on a motor spinning at 60 RPM generates 1024 pulses per revolution, or 1.024 counts per millisecond. If the control loop samples position every 50 microseconds (20 kHz), each sample interval captures only 0.051 counts on average, and the instantaneous velocity estimate derived from count differences fluctuates wildly between zero and twice the average velocity. Velocity estimation algorithms address this by increasing the observation window (measuring counts over 1 ms to 10 ms) at the cost of introducing phase lag that reduces control bandwidth, or by implementing Kalman filters that fuse position measurements with torque commands and motor model predictions to produce smooth velocity estimates. High-resolution encoders (10,000 CPR or greater) alleviate quantization noise but cost 3× to 5× more than standard encoders and impose higher interrupt rates on the microcontroller.

Sensorless control algorithms face their own pathologies. Back-EMF observers perform well at speeds above 10% of rated speed but fail catastrophically at standstill, where signal-to-noise ratio collapses. High-frequency injection schemes work at zero speed but introduce audible whine and are vulnerable to magnetic saturation; if the motor is heavily loaded and the stator flux density approaches saturation, the injected high-frequency signal experiences nonlinear distortion that corrupts the position estimate. Hybrid observers that transition from injection-based estimation at low speeds to back-EMF-based estimation at high speeds must carefully manage the handoff region, typically between 5% and 15% of base speed, to avoid discontinuities in the estimated position that cause torque pulsations or loss of synchronization.

Loss of synchronization—the motor falling out of step with the commanded field angle—represents a catastrophic failure mode unique to synchronous machines. If the load torque exceeds the motor's peak torque capability or if the rotor position estimate drifts excessively from the true position, the electromagnetic torque collapses, and the rotor decelerates rapidly. In sensor-based systems, this typically triggers an overcurrent fault as the controller attempts to restore torque by increasing current, but in sensorless systems, the observer may lose track entirely, causing the motor to spin freely or even reverse direction as the estimated field rotates independently of the rotor. Recovery requires re-synchronization: ramping the commanded electrical frequency to match the rotor's actual speed (estimated from residual back-EMF) and gradually re-establishing the torque angle. Some drives implement "flying start" algorithms that measure terminal voltages with all inverter switches disabled, compute the back-EMF frequency to infer rotor speed, and re-inject drive currents synchronized to the rotating rotor—a procedure essential for restarting fans or pumps that continue coasting after a momentary power interruption.

Bearing currents, induced by common-mode voltages from the PWM inverter, erode bearing races through electrical discharge machining (EDM), leaving characteristic fluting patterns that increase friction, generate noise, and precipitate premature failure. The inverter's switching action creates rapid voltage transitions at the motor terminals, and stray capacitance between stator windings and rotor distributes these transients such that the rotor floats at a voltage offset relative to the motor frame. When this voltage exceeds the dielectric breakdown threshold of the bearing lubricant film (15 V to 30 V), current discharges through the bearing, vaporizing microscopic regions of the raceway surface. Mitigation approaches include insulated bearings (ceramic balls or coated races), shaft grounding brushes that provide a low-impedance path to frame, and common-mode chokes at the inverter output that suppress high-frequency common-mode currents. Drives with SiC switches, which exhibit slew rates exceeding 50 V/ns compared to 5 V/ns for IGBTs, exacerbate bearing current issues, necessitating aggressive filtering or motor redesign with enhanced insulation.

Cable impedance and parasitic inductance between inverter and motor introduce voltage drops that degrade control accuracy. A 20-meter motor cable with 0.3 µH/m inductance presents 6 µH inductance per phase. At a 10 kHz PWM frequency, the inductive reactance $X_L = 2\pi f L = 0.377 \Omega$, comparable to the motor's winding resistance. This unmodeled impedance causes the actual motor terminal voltage to differ from the commanded voltage, introducing steady-state torque errors and limiting current control bandwidth. Advanced controllers compensate by measuring the voltage drop across the cable using additional sense wires routed alongside the power conductors, then adding the measured drop to the commanded voltage—a technique called four-wire voltage sensing. Alternatively, a feedforward compensation term estimates the cable drop as $V_{drop} = R_{cable} I + L_{cable} \frac{dI}{dt}$ and preemptively adjusts the modulation commands, improving torque accuracy from ±10% to ±2% without requiring extra wiring.

Harmonic distortion in the supply current drawn by the inverter's front-end rectifier generates reactive power and can violate grid interconnection standards. A conventional diode bridge rectifier with capacitive filtering draws current only during the peak of each AC cycle, producing a waveform with total harmonic distortion (THD) exceeding 80% and a power factor around 0.6. Larger drives (above 10 kW) often employ active front-end (AFE) rectifiers—three-phase PWM converters that synthesize sinusoidal input currents in phase with the grid voltage, achieving unity power factor and THD below 5%. AFE topologies also enable regenerative braking, where mechanical energy from decelerating loads is converted back to electrical energy and returned to the grid or stored in the DC bus capacitor, recovering energy that would otherwise dissipate as heat in braking resistors.

Testing and commissioning procedures validate motor control performance across the operational envelope. Locked-rotor tests measure winding resistance and leakage inductance by applying reduced voltage while mechanically restraining the rotor, recording current and voltage phasors. No-load tests characterize magnetizing inductance and core losses by driving the motor at rated voltage with no mechanical load, measuring input power and current. The difference between input power and estimated copper losses yields core loss, and the no-load current provides the magnetizing inductance. From these parameters, the per-phase equivalent circuit model is constructed, enabling accurate torque and efficiency predictions. Back-to-back testing, where two identical motors couple mechanically with one motoring and one generating, allows full-power thermal testing without dissipating the entire power output as heat—only the losses need be supplied from the grid—a technique valuable for qualifying large machines where dynamometer capacity or thermal dissipation constraints would otherwise prevent full-load testing.

Calibration of current sensors accounts for offset errors and gain mismatches that distort torque control. Offset errors, arising from op-amp bias currents and residual magnetization in Hall-effect transducers, introduce a DC component that the current controller interprets as actual current, producing involuntary torque even at zero commanded torque. A calibration routine samples the sensor outputs with the inverter disabled (all phases at high impedance) and records the average readings over several seconds; these offsets are subtracted from all subsequent measurements. Gain calibration compares measured currents against a precision current reference during a controlled test sequence, adjusting scaling factors until errors fall below 1%. Without calibration, torque ripple can reach 5% to 10% even with perfect PWM and control algorithms, manifesting as speed ripple and acoustic noise that compromise application performance.

Field-oriented control tuning balances responsiveness against stability. The $d$-axis and $q$-axis current controllers typically employ PI gains selected based on the motor's $L_d$, $L_q$, and $R_s$ parameters. A heuristic starting point sets the proportional gain $K_p = \frac{L}{T_{sample}}$ and the integral gain $K_i = \frac{R}{T_{sample}}$, where $T_{sample}$ is the current control loop period. For a motor with $L_q = 8$ mH, $R_s = 0.5 \Omega$, and $T_{sample} = 50 \mu s$, this yields $K_p = 160$ and $K_i = 10000$. These gains are then refined empirically by commanding step torque transients and observing the current response on an oscilloscope: underdamped responses indicate excessive gain (reduce $K_p$), while sluggish responses suggest insufficient gain (increase $K_p$). The velocity controller, operating at a slower rate (typically 1 kHz), uses gains 10× to 100× smaller to avoid exciting torsional resonances. A well-tuned system responds to a 50% rated torque step in under 1 millisecond with less than 10% overshoot, settling to steady-state within 5 milliseconds—performance adequate for servo positioning and CNC machining applications.

Predictive maintenance techniques monitor motor and drive health by tracking electrical signatures that correlate with impending failures. Stator winding insulation degradation reveals itself through increasing leakage current to ground, measurable via periodic megger testing (applying 500 V to 1000 V DC between windings and frame and recording insulation resistance). Values below 1 MΩ per kilovolt of rated voltage indicate moisture ingress or insulation breakdown. Bearing wear manifests as elevated vibration at the bearing defect frequencies: outer race defects produce spectral peaks at $f_{outer} = \frac{N_{balls}}{2} f_{shaft} (1 - \frac{d_{ball}}{d_{pitch}} \cos\alpha)$, where $N_{balls}$ is the number of rolling elements, $d_{ball}$ and $d_{pitch}$ are ball and pitch diameters, and $\alpha$ is the contact angle. Accelerometers mounted on the motor housing capture vibration spectra, and FFT analysis identifies these characteristic frequencies before catastrophic bearing seizure occurs. Demagnetization in PMSMs appears as a gradual increase in $i_d$ current required to maintain rated torque, indicating reduced permanent magnet flux linkage—a failure mode tracked by logging motor currents and torque estimates over months of operation.

Fault tolerance strategies enable continued operation despite sensor failures or inverter leg faults. A single-shunt current sensing topology measures only the DC bus current but reconstructs individual phase currents from the switching state and PWM duty cycles, eliminating two current sensors at the cost of increased computational complexity and restrictions on minimum duty cycles (typically 10% to 90%) to ensure measurable current pulses. In the event of an encoder failure, the controller can transition to sensorless operation using back-EMF or injection-based position estimation, accepting reduced low-speed performance in exchange for avoiding an immediate shutdown. Open-phase fault detection monitors for asymmetric currents and elevated torque ripple, indicative of a failed inverter leg or open winding; some drives reconfigure to operate with two phases by synthesizing rotating fields using the remaining healthy phases, delivering approximately 57% of rated torque—a limp-home mode sufficient for emergency pump shutdowns or vehicle safe-stop maneuvers.

The progression from first-principles electromagnetic theory through detailed architecture dissection to performance optimization and failure mode mitigation equips the hardware engineer with a holistic command of AC motor control. Whether commissioning a 500 kW industrial compressor drive, debugging torque ripple in an electric vehicle motor, or extracting the final percentage points of efficiency from a battery-powered actuator, the practitioner must navigate the interplay of electromagnetic forces, semiconductor switching dynamics, control algorithms, and thermomechanical constraints. Each layer—from lamination stack design to PWM modulation schemes to PI controller tuning—contributes to the system's performance envelope, and mastery demands fluency across this entire vertical integration.
