# Lesson 2: Detonation Physics, Shock Wave Propagation, Deflagration-to-Detonation Transition

## 1. First-Principles Intuition & Invariants

The detonation of an energetic material represents one of the most extreme and tightly coupled thermomechanical phenomena accessible to laboratory investigation. Unlike deflagration, where combustion propagates subsonically through heat transfer and molecular diffusion, a detonation wave couples a leading shock front with an immediately trailing reaction zone, forming a self-sustaining discontinuity that travels through the unreacted explosive at supersonic velocities ranging from 1,500 m/s in low-density propellants to over 9,000 m/s in dense military-grade secondary explosives. This coupling creates a feedback loop of extraordinary precision: the shock front compresses and heats the unreacted material to its ignition threshold, the subsequent exothermic decomposition releases energy that sustains the shock, and the entire structure propagates as a single coherent wave with remarkable stability across centimeters or meters of confinement.

The foundational insight governing steady-state detonation emerged in 1899 through the independent work of David Chapman and Émile Jouguet, who recognized that the shock wave and reaction zone must satisfy both hydrodynamic conservation laws and thermodynamic equilibrium constraints simultaneously. The Chapman-Jouguet condition states that a stable detonation propagates at the minimum velocity for which the Rayleigh line, representing mass and momentum conservation across the shock, is tangent to the Hugoniot curve describing all possible thermodynamic states accessible through shock compression. This tangency condition corresponds to the sonic point in the product flow frame: the detonation velocity $D$ equals the sum of the particle velocity $u$ and the local sound speed $c_s$ in the detonation products when evaluated at the reaction zone terminus. Mathematically, the CJ state satisfies $D = u + c_s$, which ensures that no rarefaction wave propagating at sound speed can overtake and disrupt the shock front from behind.

The physical elegance of the CJ solution lies in its boundary uniqueness. While an infinite family of shock states exists along the Hugoniot curve, only two special points permit self-sustained propagation: the upper Chapman-Jouguet point corresponding to detonation and the lower point describing deflagration. The detonation branch maximizes the rate of entropy production consistent with the available chemical energy, creating a pressure spike at the shock front that can exceed 300,000 atmospheres in condensed-phase explosives like HMX or RDX. The reaction zone immediately behind the shock spans only a few millimeters in length but undergoes pressure decay from the von Neumann spike—the peak shock pressure—down to the CJ pressure as chemical decomposition proceeds to completion. This rapid pressure gradient drives the hydrodynamic expansion that accelerates the product gases and couples energy back into the leading shock.

The Hugoniot curve itself encodes the equation of state of the explosive material under extreme compression. For an unreacted explosive characterized by initial density $\rho_0$, pressure $P_0$, and specific internal energy $e_0$, the Rankine-Hugoniot jump conditions relate the post-shock state variables through three conservation equations. Mass conservation requires $\rho_0 D = \rho_1 (D - u_1)$, where $\rho_1$ and $u_1$ denote the density and particle velocity immediately behind the shock. Momentum conservation yields $P_1 - P_0 = \rho_0 D u_1$, and energy conservation states that $e_1 - e_0 = \frac{1}{2}(P_1 + P_0)(v_0 - v_1)$, with $v$ representing specific volume. Eliminating the detonation velocity $D$ from these relations produces the Hugoniot equation $(e_1 - e_0) = \frac{1}{2}(P_1 + P_0)(v_0 - v_1)$, which defines a curve in pressure-volume space parametrized by the shocked state conditions. For reactive materials, the Hugoniot bifurcates into two branches: the unreacted Hugoniot describing inert shock compression and the reacted Hugoniot incorporating the exothermic heat release $Q$ that shifts the internal energy upward by the chemical energy liberated during decomposition.

The Zel'dovich-von Neumann-Döring model refines the CJ picture by resolving the internal structure of the detonation wave into three distinct zones: an inert shock front that instantaneously compresses the unreacted explosive to the von Neumann spike pressure, a finite-length reaction zone where chemical decomposition occurs under conditions of decreasing pressure and density, and a trailing Taylor expansion wave that isentropically decompresses the product gases. The ZND structure transforms the idealized CJ discontinuity into a continuous profile governed by differential equations coupling fluid dynamics with reaction kinetics. Defining a spatial coordinate $x$ moving with the detonation front, the steady-state equations express the rate of change of pressure, density, and reaction progress variable $\lambda$ as functions of the local thermodynamic state and the reaction rate law $\dot{\lambda} = k(P, T, \lambda)$. The reaction progress variable $\lambda$ ranges from zero in the unreacted material to unity at complete decomposition, and the rate function $k$ encapsulates the temperature-dependent Arrhenius kinetics modified by pressure effects and autocatalytic feedback mechanisms.

Solving the ZND equations requires iterative numerical integration starting from the von Neumann spike and marching backward toward the CJ plane. The boundary conditions at the shock front follow from the jump conditions applied to unreacted material, while the CJ condition $u + c_s = D$ provides the downstream boundary constraint. The reaction zone length $\Delta$, defined as the distance from the shock front to the point where $\lambda$ reaches 0.9 or 0.95, typically spans 0.1 to 10 millimeters depending on the explosive composition and confinement. High-nitrogen heterocyclic compounds like RDX exhibit reaction zones compressed to submillimeter scales due to their rapid decomposition kinetics, while ammonium nitrate fuel oil mixtures stretched to centimeter scales reflect the slower oxidation pathways inherent in oxygen-deficient compositions. The ZND profile also reveals the existence of a pathological sonic surface within the reaction zone for overdriven detonations, where the flow velocity relative to the shock front drops below the local sound speed, permitting hydrodynamic instabilities to seed transverse wave structures.

The transition from deflagration to detonation represents the most critical and least predictable phenomenon in explosive safety and initiation engineering. DDT occurs when a subsonic combustion wave, initially propagating through thermal diffusion and convective transport, accelerates through a sequence of instabilities until the flame front steepens into a shock wave capable of compressing the unreacted material beyond its ignition threshold. This transition unfolds across length scales spanning micrometers to meters and time scales ranging from microseconds to milliseconds, depending on the explosive morphology, confinement geometry, and the intensity of turbulent mixing in the flame brush. In granular or porous explosives, the hot product gases generated by deflagration flow through the interstitial voids, precompressing and heating the downstream material while simultaneously creating acoustic disturbances that coalesce into shock waves. When the local shock strength reaches the critical von Neumann spike pressure required for ignition, the deflagration abruptly transforms into a stable detonation propagating at the CJ velocity.

The run distance to detonation, defined as the axial propagation length required for DDT to complete, serves as the primary experimental metric characterizing an explosive's sensitivity to weak stimuli. Low-sensitivity industrial explosives like water-gel slurries may require confinement tubes exceeding one meter in length and sustained deflagration pressures above 100 MPa to achieve DDT, whereas sensitive primary explosives such as lead azide or silver acetylide transition within millimeters under minimal confinement. The critical diameter, a closely related parameter, specifies the minimum charge diameter below which detonation cannot propagate steadily due to lateral energy losses overwhelming the energy released by the reaction zone. For cylindrical charges confined in thin metal tubes, the critical diameter scales inversely with the explosive density and the magnitude of the heat release per unit volume. Military-grade compositions like Composition B or PBXN-109 exhibit critical diameters of 5 to 15 millimeters, while insensitive munitions formulations incorporating thermally stable binders may require diameters exceeding 50 millimeters to sustain detonation.

The shock sensitivity of an energetic material quantifies the minimum shock pressure, or equivalently the minimum input shock velocity, required to initiate a stable detonation. Sensitivity testing employs gap tests, where a donor charge generates a known shock pulse transmitted through an inert barrier of variable thickness before impinging on the acceptor explosive. The critical gap thickness, defined as the maximum barrier thickness through which the shock still initiates the acceptor, correlates with the von Neumann spike pressure and the reaction zone length. Highly sensitive secondary explosives like PETN or tetryl accept shocks attenuated by centimeter-thick Plexiglas barriers, corresponding to input shock pressures of 5 to 10 GPa, while insensitive high explosives like TATB require unattenuated shock pressures exceeding 20 GPa for reliable initiation. The shock sensitivity inversely correlates with the thermal stability of the molecule: compounds featuring strained ring structures, long nitrogen chains, or nitro groups ortho to each other decompose at lower activation energies and thus respond to weaker shock stimuli.

## 2. Core Architecture & Mechanics

The mathematical architecture underpinning detonation theory rests on the simultaneous solution of the Rankine-Hugoniot jump conditions, the equation of state for both unreacted and reacted phases, and the Chapman-Jouguet tangency constraint. Consider an explosive initially at rest with density $\rho_0 = 1.8 \, \text{g/cm}^3$, ambient pressure $P_0 = 0.1 \, \text{MPa}$, and specific internal energy $e_0 = 0$ defined relative to the reactant ground state. A detonation wave propagates through this material at velocity $D$, establishing a shocked state immediately behind the front characterized by pressure $P_1$, density $\rho_1$, and particle velocity $u_1$. The jump conditions provide three equations relating five unknowns, leaving two degrees of freedom that must be resolved through the equation of state and the energy balance incorporating the heat of detonation $Q$.

The mass conservation equation $\rho_0 D = \rho_1 (D - u_1)$ expresses the continuity of mass flux through the shock front. Rearranging yields the compression ratio $\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_0} = \frac{D}{D - u_1}$, which reveals that high detonation velocities coupled with modest particle velocities produce extreme density increases. For a typical high explosive with $D = 8000 \, \text{m/s}$ and $u_1 = 2000 \, \text{m/s}$, the compression ratio reaches $\frac{8000}{6000} = 1.33$, corresponding to a specific volume reduction of 25 percent at the shock front. The momentum jump condition $P_1 - P_0 = \rho_0 D u_1$ directly relates the pressure rise to the product of the initial density and the momentum flux $D u_1$. Substituting representative values yields $P_1 = 0.1 \times 10^6 + 1800 \times 8000 \times 2000 = 28.8 \times 10^9 \, \text{Pa} = 28.8 \, \text{GPa}$, a pressure exceeding 280,000 atmospheres and sufficient to convert carbon into diamond on nanosecond timescales.

The energy jump condition $(e_1 - e_0) + Q = \frac{1}{2}(P_1 + P_0)(v_0 - v_1)$ accounts for the chemical energy release $Q$ that distinguishes reactive shocks from inert compressions. For explosives, the heat of detonation typically ranges from 4 to 8 MJ/kg, corresponding to specific internal energy increases of $4 \times 10^6 \, \text{J/kg}$ to $8 \times 10^6 \, \text{J/kg}$. This energy input shifts the Hugoniot curve upward in pressure-volume space, creating the reacted Hugoniot that intersects the Rayleigh line at the CJ point. The Rayleigh line itself represents all states accessible through a shock of fixed velocity $D$ and has the slope $-\rho_0^2 D^2$ in the $P$-$v$ plane. The Chapman-Jouguet condition requires that this line be tangent to the reacted Hugoniot, which mathematically translates to the derivative condition $\frac{dP}{dv}\bigg|_{\text{CJ}} = -\rho_0^2 D^2$.

To construct the complete solution, one must adopt an equation of state model for the detonation products. The Jones-Wilkins-Lee equation of state, widely employed in hydrocode simulations, expresses the pressure as a function of specific volume $v$ and specific internal energy $e$ through the empirical form:

$$P = A \left(1 - \frac{\omega}{R_1 v}\right) e^{-R_1 v} + B \left(1 - \frac{\omega}{R_2 v}\right) e^{-R_2 v} + \frac{\omega e}{v}$$

The parameters $A$, $B$, $R_1$, $R_2$, and $\omega$ are fit to cylinder expansion test data and shock Hugoniot measurements for each explosive composition. For Composition B, representative JWL parameters take values $A = 524.2 \, \text{GPa}$, $B = 7.678 \, \text{GPa}$, $R_1 = 4.2$, $R_2 = 1.1$, and $\omega = 0.34$. The first two exponential terms capture the high-pressure repulsive interactions between product molecules, while the final term models the ideal gas limit approached at large specific volumes. The sound speed in the detonation products follows from the thermodynamic identity $c_s^2 = -v^2 \left( \frac{\partial P}{\partial v} \right)_s$, which requires evaluating the isentropic derivative of pressure with respect to volume. For the JWL form, this derivative couples both the exponential decay terms and the energy dependence, necessitating iterative solution techniques.

The ZND reaction zone structure emerges from the steady-state equations governing the spatial variation of thermodynamic and kinetic variables behind the shock front. Introducing a reaction progress variable $\lambda$ that ranges from zero at the von Neumann spike to unity at the CJ plane, the mass and momentum conservation equations combine to yield:

$$\frac{dP}{dx} = -\rho^2 D^2 \frac{dv}{dx}$$

$$\frac{du}{dx} = -v \frac{dP}{dx}$$

The energy equation, modified to include the rate of chemical energy release, becomes:

$$\frac{de}{dx} = -P \frac{dv}{dx} + Q \frac{d\lambda}{dx}$$

The reaction rate law $\frac{d\lambda}{dx} = \frac{1}{D} \dot{\lambda}$ connects the spatial gradient to the temporal rate, which is modeled through an Arrhenius form modified by pressure dependence:

$$\dot{\lambda} = k_0 (1 - \lambda)^n e^{-E_a / RT} P^\alpha$$

Here $k_0$ denotes the pre-exponential frequency factor, $n$ the reaction order, $E_a$ the activation energy, $R$ the universal gas constant, $T$ the local temperature, and $\alpha$ a pressure exponent typically ranging from 0.5 to 1.5. The temperature $T$ is obtained from the equation of state at each point $(P, v, e)$, closing the system of differential equations. Integrating from the von Neumann spike at $x = 0$ with initial conditions $(P_{\text{vN}}, v_{\text{vN}}, \lambda = 0)$ toward the CJ plane requires adaptive step-size numerical methods due to the stiff coupling between the hydrodynamic variables and the reaction kinetics across the microsecond timescales and millimeter length scales of the reaction zone.

The von Neumann spike pressure $P_{\text{vN}}$ exceeds the CJ pressure $P_{\text{CJ}}$ by a factor ranging from 1.5 to 3 depending on the explosive composition and the polytropic index of the products. For ideal explosives modeled with a gamma-law equation of state $P = (\gamma - 1) \rho e$, the ratio of spike to CJ pressure is analytically given by:

$$\frac{P_{\text{vN}}}{P_{\text{CJ}}} = \frac{\gamma + 1}{\gamma}$$

For detonation products with $\gamma \approx 3$, this ratio approaches 1.33, whereas for products exhibiting lower polytropic indices near $\gamma = 2.5$, the ratio increases to 1.4. The spike pressure governs the initiation threshold for shock-sensitive compositions: if the input shock pressure falls below $P_{\text{vN}}$, the subsequent reaction rate remains insufficient to sustain the detonation, and the wave decays into a decoupled shock-deflagration complex.

The critical diameter phenomenon arises from the competition between the chemical energy generation rate within the reaction zone and the lateral energy losses due to rarefaction waves propagating inward from the charge boundary. For a cylindrical charge of diameter $d$ confined in a rigid tube, the rarefaction wave travel time to the charge axis scales as $t_{\text{rare}} \sim d / c_s$, where $c_s$ denotes the sound speed in the detonation products. Simultaneously, the reaction zone residence time scales as $t_{\text{react}} \sim \Delta / D$, where $\Delta$ is the reaction zone length. Detonation fails when the rarefaction wave penetrates to the axis before the reaction completes, corresponding to the condition $t_{\text{rare}} < t_{\text{react}}$. Rearranging yields the critical diameter estimate:

$$d_{\text{crit}} \sim \Delta \frac{c_s}{D}$$

For high explosives with reaction zone lengths of 1 mm, detonation velocities of 8000 m/s, and product sound speeds of 3000 m/s, this scaling predicts critical diameters near 0.4 mm, significantly smaller than the experimentally observed values of 5 to 15 mm. The discrepancy arises because the simple scaling neglects the three-dimensional cellular structure of real detonations, where transverse waves and triple-point collisions on the shock front create local pressure and temperature fluctuations that modulate the reaction rate. The cellular structure introduces a characteristic transverse length scale $\lambda_{\text{cell}}$, and experiments demonstrate that the critical diameter correlates more strongly with $\lambda_{\text{cell}}$ than with the one-dimensional reaction zone length, yielding the empirical relation $d_{\text{crit}} \approx 13 \lambda_{\text{cell}}$ for cylindrical charges.

The measurement of detonation velocity in laboratory configurations employs ionization probes, optical fiber sensors, or piezoelectric transducers embedded at known axial positions within the explosive charge. A typical configuration places four probes spaced 25 mm apart along a cylindrical charge of diameter 25 mm and length 200 mm, initiated by a plane-wave lens or a high-explosive booster at one end. Each probe generates a voltage spike as the shock front arrives, and a high-speed digitizer records the arrival times with nanosecond resolution. Linear regression of the arrival times versus axial position yields the average detonation velocity over the instrumented region. For well-confined charges exceeding twice the critical diameter, the measured velocity converges to within 1 percent of the ideal CJ velocity calculated from thermochemical codes like CHEETAH or Cheetah-Detonation. Deviations exceeding 2 percent indicate either incomplete confinement, diameter-dependent velocity deficits near the critical diameter, or non-ideal effects such as aluminum combustion lags in metallized explosives.

The shock Hugoniot for unreacted explosives is experimentally determined through plate impact experiments using gas guns or explosively driven flyer plates. A flyer plate of known material and thickness, accelerated to velocities ranging from 500 to 3000 m/s, impacts a target assembly consisting of the explosive sample sandwiched between witness plates instrumented with velocity interferometers or piezoelectric gauges. The impact generates a shock wave in both the flyer and the target, and the symmetric impact condition relates the flyer velocity $V_f$ to the shock velocity $U_s$ and particle velocity $u_p$ through the impedance matching equations:

$$\rho_f U_{s,f} u_{p,f} = \rho_0 U_s u_p$$

$$u_{p,f} + u_p = V_f$$

Here $\rho_f$ and $U_{s,f}$ denote the density and shock velocity in the flyer material, while $\rho_0$, $U_s$, and $u_p$ characterize the target explosive. Solving these two equations for $U_s$ and $u_p$ as functions of the measured flyer velocity $V_f$ generates a series of $(U_s, u_p)$ data points that lie on the unreacted Hugoniot. The shock velocity in the explosive typically obeys a linear $U_s - u_p$ relation of the form $U_s = c_0 + s u_p$, where $c_0$ approximates the bulk sound speed and $s$ is a material-dependent slope parameter near 1.5 to 2.0 for most organic explosives. For HMX, representative parameters are $c_0 = 2700 \, \text{m/s}$ and $s = 1.72$.

Deflagration-to-detonation transition distance measurements employ transparent confinement tubes with high-speed photography or photomultiplier arrays to track the flame front acceleration. A granular explosive sample is loaded into a Plexiglas tube of inner diameter 25 to 50 mm and length 1 to 2 meters, with one end closed and the other end initiated by a low-energy igniter such as a nichrome bridge wire or laser spark. The deflagration initially propagates at velocities of 10 to 100 m/s, generating product gases that pressurize the tube and flow through the porous bed. Pressure transducers mounted at 100 mm intervals record the pressure-time history, revealing an initial quasi-static pressure buildup followed by a rapid acceleration phase. The transition point is identified by the abrupt jump in the flame front velocity to the CJ detonation velocity, accompanied by a sharp pressure spike exceeding 1 GPa. The run distance to detonation exhibits strong sensitivity to the packing density, particle size distribution, and tube diameter: loose packs with void fractions exceeding 50 percent transition more readily due to enhanced convective preheating, while dense packs approaching theoretical maximum density resist DDT due to reduced permeability.

## 3. High-Performance Optimization & Failure Modes

Optimizing energetic material compositions for specific detonation performance metrics requires balancing the oxygen balance, heat of formation, and density to maximize the CJ pressure and detonation velocity while maintaining acceptable sensitivity and thermal stability. The oxygen balance, defined as the excess or deficit of oxygen atoms relative to stoichiometric combustion to carbon dioxide and water, profoundly influences the product gas composition and the effective gamma of the detonation products. Zero oxygen balance formulations, such as RDX-TNT eutectic mixtures at 60/40 weight ratio, maximize the energy density by ensuring complete oxidation of the carbon and hydrogen content. Oxygen-deficient compositions, common in aluminized explosives, produce carbon monoxide and elemental carbon, lowering the effective gamma but increasing the total energy release through post-detonation aluminum combustion. Oxygen-rich formulations incorporating ammonium perchlorate or ammonium nitrate shift the product equilibrium toward nitrogen oxides and excess oxygen, raising gamma and the detonation velocity but reducing the total impulse.

The calculation of ideal detonation parameters employs thermochemical equilibrium codes that minimize the Gibbs free energy of the product mixture subject to elemental conservation constraints. The CHEETAH code, developed at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, couples the BKW or JCZ3 equation of state for the detonation products with NASA polynomials for the enthalpy and entropy of individual product species. Input to the code includes the empirical formula, density, and heat of formation of the unreacted explosive, along with the user-specified detonation pressure or volume boundary conditions. For a typical calculation, the user specifies the CJ condition, and the code iterates to find the pressure, temperature, and product composition at which the Rayleigh line is tangent to the product Hugoniot. Output includes the CJ detonation velocity, pressure, temperature, product gas composition, and the gamma-law exponent effective over the first 10 percent expansion. For RDX at density $1.80 \, \text{g/cm}^3$, CHEETAH predicts $D = 8750 \, \text{m/s}$, $P_{\text{CJ}} = 34.0 \, \text{GPa}$, and $T_{\text{CJ}} = 3800 \, \text{K}$, in excellent agreement with experimental cylinder test measurements.

Sensitivity reduction in insensitive munitions formulations leverages molecular design strategies that increase the activation energy for thermal decomposition and reduce the shock sensitivity by incorporating hydrogen bonding networks or pi-stacking interactions that stabilize the crystalline lattice. TATB (1,3,5-triamino-2,4,6-trinitrobenzene), the archetypical insensitive high explosive, exhibits a critical gap thickness exceeding 50 mm in standard gap tests due to its planar molecular structure and extensive intramolecular hydrogen bonding. The crystal structure of TATB features graphite-like layers held together by nitrogen-hydrogen bonds, creating a lattice with exceptional resistance to shear-induced hot spot formation. The activation energy for TATB thermal decomposition exceeds 50 kcal/mol, compared to 35 to 40 kcal/mol for RDX, requiring shock temperatures above 1000 K to initiate rapid decomposition. This insensitivity imposes performance penalties: TATB-based formulations deliver CJ pressures 20 to 30 percent lower than RDX-based compositions of equivalent density due to the reduced oxygen balance and heat of formation.

Failure modes in detonation propagation manifest as velocity deficits, detonation failure, or the development of irregular, multi-headed cellular structures that compromise axial symmetry. Diameter-dependent velocity deficits occur when the charge diameter approaches the critical value and lateral rarefaction waves penetrate deeply into the reaction zone, reducing the effective reaction rate by lowering the local pressure and temperature. Empirical correlations for the velocity deficit take the form:

$$\frac{D(d)}{D_{\infty}} = 1 - \frac{k d_{\text{crit}}}{d}$$

where $D(d)$ is the measured detonation velocity at diameter $d$, $D_{\infty}$ is the infinite-diameter limit, and $k$ is an empirical constant near 1 to 2. For cylindrical charges of RDX-based explosives, the velocity deficit reaches 5 percent at diameters three times the critical diameter and 2 percent at diameters five times critical. These deficits complicate precision detonation wave shaping in explosive lenses and require iterative adjustments to the lens contours to achieve the desired shock front curvature at the target plane.

Detonation failure occurs when the energy losses to the surroundings or through lateral expansion exceed the energy generation rate within the reaction zone, causing the wave to decouple into a leading shock followed by a slower deflagration or complete extinction. In unconfined charges initiated by weak boosters, failure manifests as a gradual velocity decay over propagation distances of 10 to 50 charge diameters, ultimately settling into a low-velocity deflagration regime. The transition from propagating detonation to failure is hysteretic: once failure occurs, significantly stronger initiation stimuli are required to re-establish detonation compared to the original initiation threshold. This hysteresis complicates the design of initiation trains for large-diameter insensitive explosives, where a multi-stage booster sequence incorporating progressively more sensitive intermediates is necessary to guarantee reliable transition to full detonation.

Cellular detonation structures, revealed through soot foil imprints on witness plates, demonstrate that real detonations are intrinsically three-dimensional and time-dependent, with transverse shock waves propagating perpendicular to the primary detonation direction. These transverse waves reflect off the charge boundary or collide with opposing transverse waves to form triple-point intersections, creating diamond-shaped cell patterns etched onto inert surfaces placed adjacent to the explosive. The cell size $\lambda_{\text{cell}}$ correlates inversely with the reaction rate: fast-reacting explosives like liquid nitromethane exhibit cell sizes below 1 mm, while slow-reacting emulsions display cells exceeding 50 mm. The cellular structure injects turbulence and heterogeneity into the reaction zone, locally amplifying the shock pressure at triple points by factors of 2 to 3 above the average CJ pressure. These localized pressure spikes govern the initiation of secondary charges in explosive trains, as the triple-point collisions generate the high-intensity stimuli required to exceed the von Neumann spike threshold in adjacent acceptor explosives.

The run distance to detonation in porous explosives scales with the square of the particle diameter and inversely with the permeability of the granular bed. Theoretical models based on compaction wave dynamics predict that the deflagration-driven flow compresses the initially loose powder bed into a dense plug, creating a pressure front that propagates ahead of the flame. When the compaction wave velocity exceeds the local sound speed in the porous medium, a shock wave nucleates and amplifies until it reaches the von Neumann spike pressure, triggering DDT. The critical criterion for shock formation is that the momentum flux of the product gases exceed the yield stress of the granular matrix times the pore velocity, expressed as:

$$\rho_g u_g^2 > \sigma_y \frac{u_{\text{pore}}}{c_0}$$

Here $\rho_g$ and $u_g$ denote the density and velocity of the combustion gases, $\sigma_y$ is the effective yield stress of the powder compact, $u_{\text{pore}}$ is the velocity of the compaction front, and $c_0$ is the sound speed in the unreacted solid. For granular HMX with particle sizes of 50 micrometers, the run distance to detonation in a 25 mm diameter Plexiglas tube ranges from 200 to 400 mm at packing densities of 1.2 to 1.4 g/cm³, whereas coarse-grained material with 200 micrometer particles extends the run distance to 600 to 1000 mm due to reduced surface area and slower flame spread rates.

Shock desensitization, a counterintuitive phenomenon observed in some explosives, occurs when a precursor shock pulse of intermediate strength compresses the material without initiating detonation, rendering it less sensitive to subsequent stronger shocks. This behavior arises from microstructural changes induced by the initial shock, including pore collapse, crystal fracture, and the elimination of hot spot nucleation sites. Gap test measurements on pre-shocked samples reveal critical gap thickness increases of 10 to 30 percent relative to unshocked baseline samples, indicating that the shock pre-treatment has elevated the initiation threshold. Shock desensitization complicates the design of explosive reactive armor and tandem warhead defeat mechanisms, where multiple shock stimuli interact in rapid succession. Computational models accounting for shock desensitization incorporate history-dependent damage variables that track the accumulated plastic strain and void closure, modulating the reaction rate law based on the prior loading history.

Thermal ignition in confined explosives follows the Frank-Kamenetskii critical condition, which balances the volumetric heat generation from slow exothermic decomposition against the surface heat conduction losses. For a spherical charge of radius $R$, the critical condition is expressed as:

$$\delta_{\text{crit}} = \frac{Q \rho R^2 k_0 e^{-E_a / RT_{\infty}}}{2 \kappa T_{\infty}} = 3.32$$

where $Q$ is the heat of reaction per unit mass, $\rho$ the density, $k_0$ the pre-exponential rate constant, $E_a$ the activation energy, $\kappa$ the thermal conductivity, and $T_{\infty}$ the ambient boundary temperature. The dimensionless parameter $\delta_{\text{crit}}$ governs the transition from stable steady-state heat conduction to thermal runaway. Explosives with high activation energies and low thermal conductivities, such as HMX in polymer binders, exhibit critical radii of 10 to 20 centimeters at ambient temperatures, increasing exponentially with decreasing ambient temperature due to the Arrhenius factor. In contrast, sensitive primary explosives like lead azide undergo thermal runaway at critical radii below 1 centimeter, necessitating strict limits on sample size and storage temperature.

Hydrocode simulations of detonation propagation employ Eulerian or arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian finite element meshes with cell sizes ranging from 0.1 to 1 mm to resolve the reaction zone structure. The material model couples the JWL equation of state with a programmed burn or reactive flow ignition and growth model. Programmed burn models assign a reaction progress variable to each computational cell based on the arrival time of the detonation front, with the reaction rate artificially set to complete the chemistry within a single computational time step or a fixed number of cells. Reactive flow models, exemplified by the Ignition and Growth model, solve differential equations for the reaction progress variable incorporating both shock-induced ignition and deflagration-driven growth terms:

$$\frac{d\lambda}{dt} = I (1 - \lambda)^b (\rho / \rho_0 - 1 - a)^x + G_1 (1 - \lambda)^c \lambda^d P^y + G_2 (1 - \lambda)^e \lambda^g P^z$$

The ignition term with coefficient $I$ activates when the compression ratio exceeds a threshold $1 + a$, representing shock-induced hot spot formation. The first growth term with coefficient $G_1$ models the early-time expansion of hot spots, and the second growth term with coefficient $G_2$ captures the late-time completion of the reaction as neighboring hot spots coalesce. The parameters $I$, $G_1$, $G_2$, $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$, $e$, $g$, $x$, $y$, and $z$ are calibrated to cylinder expansion test data, gap test thresholds, and wedge test run-to-detonation measurements. For PBX-9501, representative ignition and growth parameters are $I = 4 \times 10^{11} \, \mu\text{s}^{-1}$, $b = 0.667$, $a = 0.037$, $x = 7.0$, $G_1 = 550 \, \mu\text{s}^{-1}$, $c = 0.667$, $d = 0.333$, $y = 2.0$, $G_2 = 500 \, \mu\text{s}^{-1}$, $e = 0.333$, $g = 1.0$, and $z = 3.0$.

Temperature-dependent detonation velocity measurements conducted in environmental chambers at temperatures ranging from -60°C to +80°C reveal sensitivities of 0.5 to 2 m/s per degree Celsius for most secondary explosives. The temperature coefficient arises from the density decrease with increasing temperature, modulating the chemical energy per unit volume and the sound speed in the products. High-melting explosives like HMX and RDX exhibit small temperature coefficients near 0.5 m/s/°C, while low-melting compounds like TNT display coefficients exceeding 1.5 m/s/°C due to the larger thermal expansion coefficients near the melting transition. Extrapolating these temperature dependencies to cryogenic or high-temperature extremes requires caution, as phase transitions, polymorphic transformations, or decomposition pathways may introduce discontinuities in the detonation velocity versus temperature curve.

The synthesis of custom explosive formulations for research purposes requires precise control of the particle size distribution, binder content, and curing conditions to achieve reproducible detonation properties. Solvent slurry coating processes suspend the explosive crystals in a volatile solvent containing dissolved polymer binder, followed by solvent evaporation under vacuum to deposit a thin binder film on each particle. The coated particles are then pressed into pellets at pressures of 100 to 300 MPa using hydraulic presses equipped with hardened steel dies. Pressing at elevated temperatures near the glass transition of the binder improves the flowability and reduces void content, increasing the final density to 95 to 98 percent of theoretical maximum density. Non-destructive evaluation of the pressed pellets employs ultrasonic pulse-echo measurements to detect internal voids or delaminations that could degrade detonation performance or initiate premature failure. Cylinders with void fractions exceeding 2 percent exhibit detonation velocity deficits of 5 to 10 percent and are rejected from experimental test matrices.
